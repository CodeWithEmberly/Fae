# Pyramid Principle

A method for getting from a pile of thoughts to one clear message, then delivering that message so a busy reader gets it in the first few lines. Credit: Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* (developed at McKinsey). The rules below are a working summary in plain language, not a substitute for the book.

## The core idea

Readers absorb ideas most easily when they get the main point first and the support after. So every piece of professional writing should be shaped like a pyramid:

- **One governing thought at the top** — the single sentence the reader would remember if they remembered nothing else. Usually an answer, recommendation, or request.
- **Each level answers the question the level above raises.** State a point, and the reader silently asks "why?", "how?", or "what do you mean?" The points directly underneath must answer exactly that question.
- **Ideas in a group are the same kind of thing** (all reasons, all steps, all problems, all options) and together they cover the point above without overlapping (MECE — mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
- **Ideas in a group are in a deliberate order**: time/sequence, structure (parts of a whole, geography, org chart), or degree (most to least important).
- **Groups are small** — usually 2–5 points. More than that and the reader can't hold them; it usually means a missing middle level.

The opening uses **SCQA** to bring the reader to the question your governing thought answers:
- **Situation** — something the reader already knows and agrees with. Stable, uncontroversial.
- **Complication** — what changed or went wrong that makes the situation unstable.
- **Question** — the question the complication raises in the reader's mind (often left implicit).
- **Answer** — your governing thought.

In short-form military/government writing this collapses to **BLUF**: bottom line first, then the background the reader needs, then the support.

Read `references/rules.md` when you need the detailed rules — deductive vs. inductive grouping, how to test a group, common SCQA patterns, format adaptations (email, memo, slide deck, point paper, briefing) — or a worked example.

## Pick the mode

Figure out where the user is and work in the matching mode. Say which mode you're in only if it helps; don't make the user pick from a menu if their request already makes it obvious. Modes chain naturally — Think → Build is the most common path.

### 1. Think it through (convergent brainstorming)

Use when the user has a situation and a cloud of thoughts but doesn't yet know their point. The goal is to help *them* find the governing thought, not to hand them one — they know the situation, the politics, and the reader far better than you do.

How to run it:
1. **Get the raw material out.** Ask who the reader is, what the reader needs to do or decide, and let the user dump everything they're thinking. Don't structure yet.
2. **Find the question.** Work SCQA backwards: What does the reader already know? What changed? So what question is now on the reader's mind? Often the user has been answering the wrong question — this step catches it.
3. **Group bottom-up.** Cluster the user's points by kind. For each cluster ask "what do these add up to?" and push past vague summaries ("there are several issues with the budget") to an actual insight ("the budget is short $40K because two assumptions were wrong"). A summary that just names a category is a sign the thinking isn't finished.
4. **Push with "so what?"** Keep asking "so what does that mean for the reader?" until you reach a statement the reader could act on.
5. **Play it back.** State the candidate governing thought and its 2–4 supporting points in plain sentences and ask the user if that's what they actually believe. Adjust.

Keep the conversation moving: ask one or two questions at a time, not a questionnaire. Offer your own tentative read ("It sounds like your real point might be X — is that right?") when it would speed things up, but hold it loosely. When the governing thought is settled, offer to move to Build.

### 2. Build (outline, then draft)

Use when the user knows (roughly) their point or hands you notes and wants the document.

1. **Confirm the basics** if not obvious: reader, purpose (decide / approve / know / act), format (email, memo, deck, point paper, etc.), and any length limits or required templates. Don't interrogate — infer what you reasonably can and state your assumptions.
2. **Write the pyramid outline first** and show it:
   - SCQA (one line each)
   - Governing thought
   - Key line: the 2–5 supporting points, each a full-sentence assertion (not a topic label like "Budget" — say what about the budget)
   - Support under each key point, as needed
   - Note the ordering logic for each group
3. **Check the outline** against the rules (see the audit checklist below) before drafting. Fix problems in the outline — it's much cheaper than fixing them in prose.
4. **Draft** in the requested format. The governing thought appears in the first sentence or two (subject line too, for email). Key points become topic sentences / section headings / slide titles — written as assertions, so someone reading only the headings gets the whole argument.
5. Keep the user's voice and any facts they gave you. Don't invent numbers, names, or commitments; leave a clear `[placeholder]` instead.

If the user only wants the outline, stop after step 3.

### 3. Audit (check an existing draft)

Use when the user hands you a draft and asks for review, tightening, or "does this make sense?"

1. **Reconstruct the actual pyramid** — what the draft *as written* communicates: what's in the first two sentences, what each section/paragraph actually asserts, how they group. Show this as a short outline. Seeing their own structure laid bare is often the most useful thing for the writer.
2. **Diagnose** against the checklist below. For each problem, quote or point to the location, name the problem, and say why it costs the reader something.
3. **Propose the fixed pyramid** — the outline the draft should have.
4. **Offer the rewrite** (or do it, if they asked for it). If they want light-touch edits, prioritize: the lead first, then the key line, then everything else.

Be honest but proportionate — a three-line email doesn't need a full structural teardown.

#### Auditing argument and policy papers

Academic argument papers (war college, SAASS, policy and strategy essays) are a different reader and genre, but the pyramid is still the best X-ray of their logic. Adjust the audit:

- **Respect genre conventions.** A narrative hook before the thesis, a bookend conclusion, and topic-label section headings are normal and often graded expectations — note them as deliberate, don't "fix" them. The test is whether the thesis arrives by the end of the introduction and whether each paragraph's topic sentence is an assertion.
- **Look for what graders and sharp readers exploit:**
  - *Competing governing thoughts* — an intro carrying two or three top-level claims (e.g., "what the crisis is" and "what to do about it") at equal weight. If the assignment requires both, make the second clearly answer the question the first raises.
  - *Lists that don't map* — a strategy with N steps and a recommendation with M preparations that never connect, or a step with no matching preparation. Every numbered list should map onto the others or say how it relates.
  - *Mixed-kind case groupings* — e.g., a section titled for one kind of case that also holds the counterexample or the model.
  - *Test/evidence mismatch* — a stated hypothesis or falsification test that measures something different from what the paper's own cases show matters.
  - *Overloaded intro paragraphs* — count the distinct moves; more than about five usually means a split.
  - *Unstated ordering logic* in the paper's main list of recommendations.
- **Stay in your lane.** Report structure and logic only. Leave prose, citations, and grading to book-panel-review and essay-workshop, and say so if the user asks for those.
- **Don't rewrite the paper.** Show the reconstructed pyramid, rank the problems, and give a concrete fix for each (where to split, what sentence to add, what to retitle). Offer to work any one of them through with the user.

## Audit checklist

Run this on any outline or draft:

- **Buried lead** — Is the governing thought in the first 1–2 sentences (and the email subject line / slide title)? Or does the reader have to wade through background or chronology first?
- **No real governing thought** — Is the "point" actually just a topic or a summary of activity ("This memo discusses the Q3 results")? It should be an assertion the reader could agree or disagree with.
- **Question/answer mismatch** — Does each set of supporting points answer the question the point above raises? Does the opening's Complication actually lead to the question the governing thought answers?
- **Mixed-kind groups** — Are any grouped points different kinds of thing (a reason, a next step, and a background fact in one list)?
- **Not MECE** — Do points overlap, or is there an obvious gap the reader will notice?
- **No ordering logic** — Can you name why the points are in this order?
- **Too many points** — More than ~5 at one level usually means a missing intermediate grouping.
- **Long deductive chains** — Arguments that go "A, therefore B, therefore C, therefore D" before reaching the point make the reader hold everything in their head. Prefer inductive groups (several independent reasons supporting one point) at the top levels; keep any deductive chain to about 3 steps and put the conclusion first.
- **Label headings** — Section headings or slide titles that name a topic ("Background", "Costs") rather than state a point ("Costs drop 20% in year two").
- **Introduction overload** — Situation/background longer than the reader needs. The intro should tell the reader only what they need to accept the question, nothing they already know in detail.

## Adapting to format

The pyramid is the same; the packaging changes. Brief guidance (more in `references/rules.md`):

- **Email** — subject line carries the governing thought or the ask; first sentence restates it; support follows; explicit ask/deadline at the end if action is needed.
- **Memo / point paper / staff paper** — BLUF paragraph up front; key points become numbered paragraphs or headed sections with assertion-style leads; recommendation and required action are explicit.
- **Report / recommendation** — executive summary is the top of the pyramid in miniature; each section heading is a key-line assertion.
- **Slide deck / briefing** — one governing-thought slide up front; each slide title is a full-sentence assertion; reading only the titles in order should tell the whole story.

## When the pyramid isn't the right shape

Sometimes leading with the answer is the wrong move — delivering bad news to someone who'll react badly, persuading a hostile audience, or when the reader explicitly wants the reasoning before the conclusion. Don't force it. Name the tradeoff to the user, and if they choose an indirect order, still build the pyramid internally so the logic holds — just deliver it bottom-up. SCQA also helps here: a strong Situation and Complication can earn agreement before the answer arrives.
