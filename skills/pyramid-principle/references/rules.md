## 1. Vertical logic: the question–answer dialogue

Every statement you make raises a question in the reader's mind. The level below must answer that question — and only that question. Writing a pyramid is like holding an imagined conversation:

> **You:** We should move the training conference to March.
> **Reader:** Why?
> **You:** Three reasons: cost, attendance, and the new facility.

If the points underneath answer a *different* question (e.g., "how would we do that?" when the reader is still asking "why?"), the reader gets lost even if each sentence is correct.

Test: read a point aloud, ask "which question does this raise?", then check that the group beneath answers it.

## 2. Horizontal logic: deductive vs. inductive groups

Points at the same level relate to each other in one of two ways.

**Inductive** — several independent ideas of the same kind that together support a conclusion. ("Cost is lower, attendance will be higher, and the new facility opens in February → move to March.") Remove one and the others still stand. Inductive groups are easy on the reader and are the preferred structure for the upper levels of a pyramid.

**Deductive** — a chain where each step depends on the previous one: a statement, a comment on it, and a conclusion that follows. ("All funded courses must finish before the fiscal year ends. This course can't finish before then. Therefore it won't be funded.") Remove one step and the argument collapses. Deduction forces the reader to hold every step in mind before reaching the point, so:
- keep chains to about three steps;
- state the conclusion first, then walk through the chain;
- use deduction at lower levels, not at the key line, where possible.

## 3. Testing a group

For any group of points under a summary:
- **Same kind?** Can you name them all with one plural noun? (reasons, steps, problems, risks, options, changes, regions). If you can't, they don't belong in one group.
- **MECE?** Do any two overlap? Is anything obvious missing that the reader will ask about?
- **Does the summary say something?** The point above a group should state the *insight* the group produces, not just announce the group. Weak: "There are three problems with the plan." Strong: "The plan fails on schedule, cost, and staffing — each enough to sink it." Weaker still: "Several issues were identified." If a summary only names a category, the thinking isn't finished.
- **Size:** 2–5 points. A single point under a summary means the summary is just restating it. More than five usually hides an intermediate grouping.

## 4. Ordering a group

Every group needs a reason for its order. Three kinds:
- **Time / sequence** — steps in a process, cause-and-effect, chronology. Use for actions and how-to.
- **Structure** — parts of a whole: geography, organization, components of a system. Use when you've divided something up.
- **Degree** — most to least important, largest to smallest. Use for reasons, problems, and options of the same kind.

If none applies, the points may not be a real group.

## 5. SCQA patterns

The introduction is a short story that brings the reader to your question. Keep the Situation to things the reader already knows; its only job is to set up the Complication.

Common patterns:
- **Directive / "what should we do?"** S: we have goal X. C: something is blocking it. Q: what should we do? A: do Y.
- **"How do we do it?"** S: we've decided to do X. C: we don't know how / there are obstacles. Q: how? A: these steps.
- **"Is this the right solution?"** S: we have problem P. C: someone proposed solution X. Q: is X right? A: yes/no, because…
- **Status / update.** S: we committed to X by date D. C: here's what changed. Q: where do we stand and do you need to act? A: status + any ask.
- **Decision request.** S: we need to decide X by date D. C: options differ on key tradeoffs. Q: which option? A: recommend option B.

Order can flex for emphasis: ASC (answer, then context — the standard BLUF form), CSA (lead with the complication when it's urgent), QSCA (when the reader has asked you a direct question). Whatever the order, the reader should have the answer within the first paragraph.

## 6. Format adaptations

**Email**
- Subject: the governing thought or the ask, not a topic. ("Recommend moving conference to March — decision needed by Fri" beats "Conference dates".)
- Line 1: governing thought / ask.
- Then: 2–4 short supporting points (bullets fine).
- End: explicit ask, owner, and deadline if action is required.

**Memo / point paper / staff paper (BLUF style)**
- Purpose line (if the template has one) states what the reader must do.
- BLUF paragraph: governing thought + recommendation in 1–3 sentences.
- Background: only what's needed to understand the question (the S and C).
- Discussion: numbered paragraphs, one key-line point each, led by an assertion sentence.
- Recommendation / required action: restated, specific.
- Keep any mandated template headings; put the pyramid inside them.

**Report / recommendation**
- Executive summary = the top two levels of the pyramid.
- Each chapter or section = one key-line point; heading states the point.

**Slide deck / briefing**
- Early slide states the governing thought (the "bottom line" slide).
- Each slide title is a full-sentence assertion ("Attendance rises ~25% in March") — the "action title."
- Reading only titles in sequence should tell the entire argument; the body of each slide is the evidence for its title.
- One idea per slide.

**Oral briefing / talking points**
- Same as a deck: lead with the bottom line, signpost the key points ("three reasons"), then walk through them in order.

## 7. Worked example

### Raw situation (what the user brings to Think mode)

> "Our unit's quarterly training conference is in December. Hotel quote came in way higher than last year. Lots of people are on leave in December. Also the base is opening a new conference center in February which would be free. My boss likes December because it closes out the year. I need to send something up to the colonel. Also the guest speaker said she's only free in Feb or March. And I think the agenda needs a refresh anyway."

### Think it through

- Reader: the colonel. Needs to: approve a date change.
- What the reader already knows (S): the conference is scheduled for December, as usual.
- What changed (C): costs are up, December attendance is weak, and better options appeared.
- Question in the reader's mind: should we keep December or move it?
- Grouping the user's thoughts:
  - Reasons to move: hotel cost, leave/attendance, free new facility, speaker availability — all *reasons* (same kind).
  - "Agenda refresh" — a different kind of thing (a separate improvement), not a reason to move dates. Park it.
  - "Boss likes December" — not a reason for the colonel; it's a stakeholder concern to handle (address the objection, or brief the boss first).
- Can the reasons be grouped more tightly? Cost (hotel vs. free facility) is really one point. Speaker + attendance are both about *who will be there*. → Two to three key points.
- So what? → "Move the conference to March: it's cheaper, more people (including our keynote) can attend, and nothing is lost by moving."

### Build — outline

- **S:** The quarterly training conference is scheduled for December, as in past years.
- **C:** This year December costs more and draws fewer people, and a better option has opened up.
- **Q:** Should we move it?
- **Governing thought:** Move the conference to March — it saves ~$18K and increases attendance, with no loss of year-end value.
  - **Key point 1 (reason): It's substantially cheaper.** Hotel quote is $18K vs. $0 at the new base conference center opening in February. *(order within: biggest number first)*
  - **Key point 2 (reason): More of the right people can attend.** ~30% of the unit is on leave in December; the keynote speaker is only available Feb–Mar.
  - **Key point 3 (reason): We don't lose the year-end benefit.** Year-in-review can be done in the January staff meeting. *(addresses the "December closes out the year" objection)*
- **Ordering:** degree — strongest reason first.
- **Ask:** Approve the March date by [date] so we can reserve the facility.

### Build — email draft

> **Subject:** Recommend moving training conference to March — approval needed by 20 Nov
>
> Sir,
>
> I recommend we move the quarterly training conference from December to March. It saves about $18K and gets more of the unit — and our keynote speaker — in the room.
>
> - **Cost:** The hotel quote for December is $18K; the new base conference center (opens February) is free.
> - **Attendance:** About 30% of the unit is on leave in December, and Dr. [Name] can only speak in February or March.
> - **Year-end value:** We can still close out the year with a year-in-review at the January staff meeting.
>
> Request your approval by 20 Nov so we can reserve the conference center before other units book it.
>
> V/r,
> [Name]

### Audit — what a typical first draft looks like

> **Subject:** Training conference
>
> Sir, as you know we hold the quarterly training conference every December to close out the year. This year I got the hotel quote and it came in at $18K, which is a lot higher than last year. I also looked at the leave calendar and a lot of people are out. I was talking to the base facilities office and they're opening a new conference center in February. Our speaker also mentioned she's only free Feb or March. We might also want to update the agenda. Let me know what you think about possibly moving it.

**Reconstructed pyramid (what the draft actually says):**
- Governing thought: none stated — closes with "let me know what you think about possibly moving it."
- Points, in order of discovery: hotel cost; leave; new facility; speaker; agenda.

**Diagnosis:**
- *Buried lead / no governing thought* — the recommendation is implied, appears only in the last sentence, and is hedged ("possibly"). The colonel has to infer what's being asked.
- *Label subject line* — "Training conference" tells the reader nothing.
- *Chronological order* — points are in the order the writer found them, not by importance.
- *Mixed kinds* — "update the agenda" is not a reason to move the date.
- *Not grouped* — hotel cost and free facility are one point (cost) split in two.
- *No clear ask or deadline.*

**Fix:** the outline and email above.
