Counterpoint: Weinberger's Six Tests Would Have Flunked the War That Worked

Wasser is right and Caspar Weinberger is wrong about whether America can win a war it has deliberately decided not to fight with everything it has. Operation Inherent Resolve failed Weinberger's second test on purpose, and it still destroyed the Islamic State as a territorial entity.

Weinberger laid out his six tests at the National Press Club on November 28, 1984, and Kitfield gives them in full. Vital interests must be at stake. The country must commit enough force to win decisively. Objectives must be clear, forces must be sized to them, the public must be reasonably sure to back the effort, and force must come last. (His speechwriters warned him that six was an awkward number and that he should stop at three. Secretaries of Defense are seldom edited.)

Now hold Inherent Resolve up to that list. Washington built the strategy around local partners precisely to limit risk to American forces and to avoid "a potentially endless commitment." The report calls it "limited-liability, limited risk." The United States took a supporting role by design, and airpower was its main contribution. By Weinberger's standard, that is the gradualist sin itself. By RAND's accounting, "On the whole, OIR was a success," achieved while minimizing costs for the coalition. Sending more force sooner would not have changed much, either. A more aggressive air war might have sped the defeat, the authors judge, "but not dramatically," because the partners set the clock.

Weinberger's side has a serious reply, and Wasser's own pages supply it. The tests came from Vietnam and Beirut, and he warned that casual use of force would bring "domestic turmoil" without achieving the goal. Kitfield's officers then proved the alternative in 1991, with a plan promising "no gradualism or conceded sanctuary." Meanwhile, the report admits that the limited war's result may not last. After the partial American withdrawal from Syria in October 2019, Turkey attacked the Syrian Democratic Forces, Islamic State fighters began to reorganize, and the authors conclude it "remains to be seen" whether OIR achieved "more than an ephemeral victory."

We concede two of the six. Clear objectives and public tolerance are real requirements, and OIR met both, with what the report calls a "relatively coherent strategy" and costs it says were minimized. The decisive-force test still loses, because of what undid the gains. Wasser's list of threats to the victory starts with Americans leaving and a partner weakened. That list does not mention too little force in 2015. The first-order effect of the limited design was a win at low cost. The second-order effect was a partner who depended on the United States staying. The third-order effect, and this is our inference, is that the durable requirement for a limited war is staying power, which Weinberger's checklist has no line for. (He assumed you would win and go home. So did nearly everyone who has said that sentence since 1950.)

What would change our mind? A case in which a partner-led hold collapsed while the Americans were still there and still paying. The line for seminar: "Weinberger asks whether you sent enough to win; Wasser shows the better question is whether you will still be there after you have." Six tests, and none of them is a calendar. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Kitfield · extrapolation

Prodigal Soldiers Would Like a Word About Your Hold

Kitfield's generals would dislike Shaun's thesis on sight, and their objection makes it stronger. The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the Iran war without a settlement, and his thesis is that Airmen should prepare now to hold the strait the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Kitfield, not Shaun's answer.

The book's central creed cuts against an open-ended mission. These officers spent two decades rebuilding the force around the lessons of Vietnam, and their 1991 plan was written as a refutation of gradualism. A standing hold in the Gulf is the kind of assignment they swore off. Kitfield also expects a different service to do the work: after the crisis, he writes, it would "surely be the Navy that supplied the continuing U.S. presence." (Airmen drafting a Hormuz paper may wish to read that sentence sitting down.)

Kitfield helps on the paper's third point, the design of the hold. He describes the Gulf as "an area of the world that had no interest in hosting American military forces," which is the best argument on the shelf for a small footprint. He also supplies the warning about money. Apache readiness hit 100 percent in the Gulf because the Army emptied its parts bins and halved flying hours at home, and Kitfield calls Desert Storm "an unsustainable peak." A hold has to run for decades on a budget that never peaks. Weinberger's public-support test fits the proposed renewal vote in Congress, too, since a recurring vote is a recurring check on that assurance.

Opinion, extrapolated: Kitfield would call the hold a mistake and then, grudgingly, tell you how to run it. Two questions for the draft:

  1. If the Navy is the natural owner of presence in the Gulf, what does an Airman uniquely bring to this hold?
  2. Which line in the budget keeps the hold from draining the home fleet the way the Apache surge did? (S)
The Readings So Far · Zhang, Clodfelter, and Wasser

Absolute Assurance, Some Restrictions Apply

Great powers promise airpower to their partners far more easily than they deliver it, and three books on the shelf catch them in the act. Stalin offered China air cover on July 5, 1950, and on July 13 specified a MiG division of 124 planes, without saying how it would work with Chinese troops on the ground. Zhang's verdict is that Soviet air support "remained a cryptic promise." Chinese leaders resented it. (A vague commitment has one great advantage for the person making it: nobody can prove he broke it.)

Nixon's version was less vague and more interesting. After Haig carried his pledge to Saigon on November 9, 1972, Nixon told Thieu, "You have my absolute assurance that if Hanoi fails to abide by the terms of this agreement it is my intention to take swift and severe retaliatory action." In early January, with both Democratic caucuses voting to cut off funds, he told Kissinger of threatened future raids that "as far as our internal planning is concerned we cannot consider this to be a viable option." That private doubt concerned Hanoi stalling in Paris, so we won't call it proof he was lying to Thieu. Still, within days he was again promising Thieu a strong response to any violation. Clodfelter leaves the reader to compare the statements, and the comparison is unkind.

Wasser brings the pattern up to date. The Syrian Democratic Forces, whom the coalition praised as "reliable, effective combat soldiers," went on to expel the group from Syria systematically, and then the partial withdrawal of October 2019 exposed them to a Turkish offensive. Friday's Vulcan item made the same point in hardware: a force relying on one supplier has a plan only as good as that supplier's next launch. Our take: when you read any alliance in this course, ignore the promise and ask who controls the switch. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 5, Day 1 · active voice and agency

Mistakes Were Made, by an Idiot

Use the passive voice when it puts familiar information first, and use the active voice whenever someone has to own the action. Booth, Colomb, and Williams argue in The Craft of Research that the usual ban on the passive, followed mindlessly, makes prose less clear. Their test is simpler than the rule: does the sentence open with something the reader already knows, preferably a main character? Get that right, they say, and active and passive sort themselves out.

Strategy papers have a special reason to care, because the passive is how officials lose track of who decided. Take this sentence. Before: "Air cover was promised to the Chinese in July 1950, and it was left in vague terms." Who promised? Who left it vague? (The sentence would prefer you not ask.) After: "Stalin promised the Chinese air cover in July 1950 and left the terms vague." Now the man has his fingerprints back on it.

The same authors defend the passive when it does honest work. Try two sentences in a row: "China's armies needed air cover. That cover was promised by Stalin, in terms vague enough to mean almost anything." The second sentence is passive, and it is better that way, because it picks up "cover" from the end of the first sentence and saves the new information for last. Booth's team calls that the main function of the passive. They also draw a line you can use tonight: actions only the author can perform, such as argue, suggest, and conclude, take the active voice. "It can be concluded" is a committee. "I conclude" is a person with a thesis.

An old editor's trick finds the suspects fast. Add "by an idiot" after the verb. "Mistakes were made by an idiot" scans, so the verb is passive, and you can decide whether that idiot deserves a name. In an eight-day sprint to a paper, run it on your topic sentences first. (S)

Defense & Acquisition × Wasser · via Breaking Defense, November 21

Have You Tried Turning Your Ally Off and On Again?

The Senate may soon investigate why Ukraine keeps losing full access to Starshield, the military version of SpaceX's Starlink, Ashley Roque reports in Breaking Defense. Ukraine uses the network to run drone operations against Russian targets. In October, Ukrainian officials said a sudden network change made drones and defenses fail, and they tied that failure to military and civilian deaths. They said service was fully restored later that month, then said this month that it was hampered again. Ukraine's chargé d'affaires, Denys Sienik, told the outlet on November 9 that "speed limitations" had hit the contact line, and that Kyiv had no clarity from the Pentagon or SpaceX about the cause or who ordered it. Speaking at the Halifax security forum on Friday, Sen. Chris Coons said the service was "being turned on and off" and promised an early hearing if he gets the Foreign Relations gavel, which is not yet his. Nobody in the article establishes why any of this happened, and we won't either.

Wasser explains why the cause matters less to an ally than the doubt. Partner forces in Inherent Resolve could not have beaten the Islamic State without coalition intelligence and precise fires, and the report describes how much of their nerve rested on that support. Iraqi forces often refused to move without "visible evidence of reassurance" in the form of coalition aircraft overhead, and the coalition flew sorties of little tactical value just to get them going. (Some of the most expensive motivational speaking in history.) Those partners were watching an airplane they could see. Ukraine's depends on a signal, owned by a contractor, governed by arrangements its own government says it can't get explained.

We would have preferred to pair this with Sekirin, whose drone war it describes, but his book is not on our desk yet, so Wasser gets the assignment. Opinion: this is an acquisition story before it is a political one. When the Pentagon buys a warfighting service instead of owning the system, the contract has to answer three plain questions before the war does:

  1. Who can change the service?
  2. Who must be told when they do?
  3. What does the partner fall back on that night?

Any Gulf state asked to join a Hormuz hold will read this story closely. An alliance can survive bad news. It has more trouble with a loading icon. (S)

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
