July 1965, Party of Two: The Decision Exercise Orders Off Schlight's Menu

Today the seminar time-travels to July 1965, splits in two, and briefs the Chief of Staff. The tasking, for anyone who skimmed Friday's issue between bites of Halloween candy: Gen. John P. McConnell wants an airpower strategy that serves the President's objectives in Vietnam, and the two groups must offer options that differ in some concrete way, in focus, targets, or the center of gravity. Nine hours, one war, no hindsight allowed. (Hindsight will, of course, sneak in. It always does.)

The menu McConnell actually had is in John Schlight's first chapter, and he had already lost the first argument. The question in early 1965 was whether to put the weight of the effort on the ground in Vietnam, which the planners called Plan 32, or in the air and seas around the edge of China, Plan 39. McConnell and his Pacific commander, General Harris, wanted Plan 39: a holding action in the south, plus fifteen strike squadrons in Taiwan, Okinawa, Japan, and Korea to deter or defeat Chinese adventures. When the idea of sending an Army division surfaced in March, his first reaction was that it was a bad idea. His staff estimated it would take more than five divisions just to seal off the north at the 17th parallel, and he wanted the land routes closed by air and the sea routes by the Navy first, with about sixty days to see whether it worked. The ground war won anyway. By April 20, at Honolulu, Schlight records the bombing strategy as "short-lived, and inadequately tested."

Then came the B-52s. On June 18, 1965, the first Arc Light strike hit a Viet Cong base area and found no enemy, by the ground teams' account, and the press spent several days on the wisdom of "using a sledgehammer to kill gnats." The Air Staff was unhappy for its own reasons: a poor showing by the bombers, a small follow-up ground force, and last-minute political attempts to change the mission. McConnell shut down his own doubters with a line worth having in your pocket today: "If SAC hasn't learned to bomb in fifteen years of extensive training," he said, "we are not going to teach them how in a few weeks." Confidence is a fine quality in a chief. It is less useful as a targeting method.

Now go to tomorrow's reading, because Mark Clodfelter dates the turning point to the very month you're sitting in. In July 1965, he writes in his preface, President Johnson concluded that bombing could not stop the North's participation in the war and that strengthening South Vietnam had higher priority. That's the strategic weather your group is flying in. Any option that promises to win the war from the air is arguing with the boss's boss, who has already moved on, though Clodfelter adds that the decision was never final and Johnson kept wavering for the rest of his presidency.

Opinion: the winning brief today will probably be the one that answers the question McConnell's Plan 39 dodged. What, exactly, is airpower for in this war? Schlight's conclusion warns that the Air Force fought in South Vietnam with neither its own war objective nor reliable data on its results. A group that names an objective, and a way to measure it, will already be ahead of the 1965 Air Staff. That bar is lower than it sounds, which is the uncomfortable part. (S)

Tomorrow's Reading · Clodfelter, Tuesday, November 3

Tomorrow's Reading: Clodfelter Wants a Receipt for Every Bomb

Mark Clodfelter opens The Limits of Air Power with Richard Nixon on Meet the Press in April 1988, saying his greatest mistake as President was not Watergate but failing to bomb and mine North Vietnam in 1969 as he later did in 1972. A former President ranking his regrets on Sunday television is a strong opening. Clodfelter then spends the book politely disagreeing with him.

His thesis: bombing "worked" in 1972 because it fit Nixon's specific, limited goals and the situation at the time, and the same bombs would not have bought the same results in 1965 or 1969. The book's yardstick is Clausewitz. The only true test of an air campaign, he argues, is what it contributes to the nation's war aims, so he grades Rolling Thunder, Linebacker I, and Linebacker II against the aims each one served. His key tool is the split between "positive" objectives, reached only by applying force, and "negative" ones, reached only by limiting it, such as Johnson's aim of keeping China and the Soviets out. Political controls on airpower, he says, flow straight from the negative objectives.

What he's arguing against is the belief, which he says permeated the Air Force when he wrote in 1989, that massive bombing could have won the war in 1965 or 1969, and that Linebacker II's eleven days proved it. For this course, that matters a lot, since it's the Korea problem from Crane run one more time with bigger airplanes.

Read for:

  1. Preface: the positive and negative objectives, and the "chameleon" of doctrine that failed in Rolling Thunder and worked in Linebacker I. This is the whole book in five pages.
  2. Chapter II, around ebook pages 59 and 60: Johnson's Great Society as a negative objective, in his own unprintable words about the woman he really loved.
  3. Chapter III, around ebook pages 110 and 114 to 116: the 30-mile circle around Hanoi, and Johnson asking Wheeler whether he was more sure than MacArthur was before the 1966 oil raids.
  4. Chapter IV, around ebook pages 146 and 159 to 160: the tonnage comparison between South and North, and why Rolling Thunder looked like a qualified success to the White House and a qualified failure to the airmen.
  5. Chapters V and VI, around ebook pages 170, 175 to 176, and 204 to 212: the Easter Offensive of March 30, 1972, the mining of the ports, and the 129 B-52 crews briefed on December 18.
  6. Chapter VII, around ebook pages 225 to 227: the epilogue's case that the Air Force learned the wrong lesson from the Eleven-Day War.

Short on time? Read the preface and the epilogue tonight, then Chapter V. If the preface's argument holds up in Chapter V, it holds up. (Page numbers are from the ebook PDF, so your print copy may differ.) (S)

Negative Objectives, Positive Thinking: Clodfelter Reads the Iran War

The prompt, for the regulars, has two parts:

  1. What is the most important potential strategic crisis facing the nation?
  2. Based on your historical understanding of the Age of Limited War, how should Airmen prepare to face it?

One crisis, ten pages, due December 1. The syllabus offers an IRGC nuclear test among its examples, and the news has offered a war with Iran, so today we hand that file to Clodfelter. What follows is our extrapolation from his arguments. He wrote about North Vietnam in 1989, not Iran in 2026, and this isn't Shaun's answer. (ISW's Iran Update for November 1 is in this morning's feed, but its site blocked us, so we won't pretend to know what it says.)

Start with the news. On October 26, in Foreign Policy, RAND's Raphael Cohen and Howard Shatz laid out Washington's three options in the Iran war: escalate, walk away, or stay the course. They pick staying the course, meaning the blockade, economic pressure, force in response to attacks, and talks on the Strait of Hormuz and the nuclear program, and they admit it "almost certainly will not bring quick results." On escalation they're blunt. Air campaigns rarely topple governments, and Iran has about twice Iraq's population and nearly four times its territory. Kharg Island, which handles about 90 percent of Iran's oil exports, would be easy to take and hard to hold.

Now Clodfelter's lens, and from here it's opinion. He would ask first for the list of objectives, sorted into positive and negative. Ending the nuclear program and reopening Hormuz are positive aims. Avoiding a wider regional war, protecting energy markets, and keeping allies aboard are negative ones, and in his framework every negative aim becomes a political control on the air campaign. He quotes Clausewitz's warning that "a preponderantly negative policy will ... retard the decision." Cohen and Shatz have, in effect, chosen a preponderantly negative policy on purpose and bet that time is on Washington's side. Clodfelter would call that coherent, as long as nobody then complains that the bombing is too slow.

His second question is what kind of war the enemy is fighting. Bombing failed in Rolling Thunder, he argues, because Communist forces in the South needed few outside supplies, and it worked in Linebacker I because Hanoi mounted a conventional offensive that consumed huge logistics. An Airman preparing for an Iran crisis should ask whether the IRGC is fighting a Linebacker kind of war, with targets it can't do without, or a Rolling Thunder kind, with proxies and drones that need very little. The doctrine, he warns, is a chameleon: right in one war, wrong in the next.

The paper-writer's takeaway is his last one. Nixon's bombing succeeded partly because China and the Soviets had given him a free hand, which made the bombs more threatening to Hanoi's vital concerns. Whoever drafts the Iran paper should ask who gives Tehran a free hand today, and whose patience runs out first. Bombs, as Clodfelter keeps reminding us, are faster than economies. They are rarely faster than politics. (S)

Five Books In, One War Down: The Air Force Keeps Grading the Wrong Exam

Five books in, the course has started repeating itself on purpose, like a good drill sergeant. Gaddis told us strategy is relating ends to means. Crane showed what happens when the ends get fuzzy: the April 1952 FEAF study found rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts and 243 aircraft lost. Zhang reminded us that both sides inflated their kill claims, the American eight or ten to one later revised by some to about two to one. McDougall's Khrushchev bluffed a missile force into existence. And today Schlight's Air Force measures everything except progress: only 3 percent of its 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops actually engaged with allied forces.

Put Crane and Schlight side by side and the plot connects. Crane's argument was that Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded and go back to planning for general war, a bill it paid in Vietnam. Schlight's Air Force arrives in 1965 with a close air support mission it never wanted, and a MACV staff that spent 80 percent of its effort on Army matters. The invoice is itemized. Clodfelter, tomorrow, adds a third panel: the same Air Force spent 1965 to 1968 bombing the North with doctrine built for the Soviet Union, while dropping 2.2 million tons on the South against 643,000 on the North. Two books, one war, and most of the bombs went to the half nobody wrote doctrine for.

The news web keeps growing. Friday's Cook Political Report item, about campaigns preferring a toss-up rating because a close race raises money, sat next to McDougall's missile gap. Friday's CSIS report on fast following ended on Monday's material, North Vietnamese missile crews keeping their radars dark until targets came close. And last Wednesday's two Michigan pollsters, a few points apart, were Zhang's kill-ratio problem with yard signs. Today the rain forecast joins Clodfelter's monsoons, and Australia's undersea minelayer joins Nixon's mining of Haiphong. (We promise the connections are less forced than they sound. Mostly.)

Now pair Gaddis and Clodfelter, which may be the best link on the shelf so far. Gaddis's Truman team believed resources were limited, so it chose means to fit the end. Clodfelter's Johnson had the resources and limited them himself, because his negative objectives, keeping China out and the Great Society alive, mattered as much as his positive one. Gaddis gives you the definition. Clodfelter shows you what happens when one president is pursuing four ends at once with one air campaign.

Opinion: the theme for this week is measurement. Crane's FEAF counted rail cuts, Zhang's pilots counted kills, Schlight's Seventh Air Force counted sorties, and Clodfelter will ask whether any of it bought a political result. An air force that counts what's easy to count will always look busy. Whether it looks successful depends on who's grading. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 2, Day 1 · active voice and agency

It Was Decided by Somebody: Pollock Hunts the Missing Actor

Monday is active voice day, and our guide is Timothy Pollock's How to Use Storytelling in Your Academic Writing, a book with a whole section titled "Active Writing" and a man who does not hide his feelings. "The passive voice is the biggest bane of academic writing," he writes, and he admits that even confirmed haters like himself keep finding it in their own drafts. (Revising for his second edition, he says, he cut a pile of passive constructions from his own prose. Physician, heal thyself, and he did.)

The rule is simple. In the active voice, a subject does something to an object with an active verb. In the passive, a form of "to be" plus a past participle puts the object up front, and the actor shows up late or never. Pollock's sharpest point is about agency: leaning on passive constructions, he says, "robs the actors of their agency." He borrows a line from another writer, Evans, whose example is a memo announcing that "It was decided to eliminate the coffee break," and whose response is the question every reader should ask: "Which wretch decided that?" Pollock's cure is to name who is acting, use personal pronouns, and swap "of" phrases for possessives where you can.

He's no absolutist, which is the useful part. Following Evans, he allows the passive when the actor is unknown, when the thing acted on matters more than the actor, when tact argues against naming names, or when a long subject would delay the verb. The test is whether you chose the passive or merely drifted into it.

Here's a sentence from today's reading that drifted. Before: "The Army division proposal was opposed, and more than five divisions were estimated to be needed to seal off the north." Opposed by whom? Estimated by whom? After: "McConnell opposed the Army division, and his staff estimated that sealing off the north at the 17th parallel would take more than five divisions." Now the reader knows whom to argue with, which in a SAASS paper is half the point. The actor is only useful if it's the right one, so check the page before you name names.

Pro tip, from an old editor's trick: if you can add "by zombies" after the verb and the sentence still makes sense, it's passive. "The decision was made by zombies." Then ask whether you meant to hide the zombies. Opinion: in a paper about who controlled airpower, a sentence with no actor is a sentence that dodges the thesis. Schlight spent a whole book on who owned the airplanes. Don't let your verbs lose track of them. (S)

Rain, Rain, Go Away, Come Again After Polls Close

On the eve of Election Day, a reader in Columbia, Maryland, asked electoral-vote.com the question every campaign manager is asking while staring at the radar: what does bad weather do to turnout? The forecast, the reader noted, showed rain from east Texas, Louisiana, and Mississippi up through the Midwest to Michigan, which is a lot of states with interesting elections. EV's (Z) answered that there have been many studies, and the conclusion is that rain reduces Election Day turnout by about 1 percent.

Then the strategic wrinkle. These days, (Z) wrote, that dip disproportionately hurts Republican candidates, since Democrats are more likely to vote early and Republicans more likely to vote on Election Day. The same Q&A reported early votes already nearly 10 million above 2022, though (Z) cautioned that it's hard to draw firm conclusions, since some states have expanded early voting and some voters were driven to get ballots in early. In other words, a fair share of this election was decided before anyone checked the forecast.

We'd note that 1 percent is small, until it isn't. In a race decided by less than that, the weather is a campaign issue with no campaign.

The same column answered a question about Alaska, where the Peltola campaign hoped a second Dan Sullivan on the ballot would confuse some Republicans into voting for the wrong one and skipping their second choice. (Z)'s verdict was that the odds were very small: "It was a stupid gamble." EV does not soften its verdicts, which is one reason we read it.

Now go to tomorrow's reading, where weather is a strategic actor with a seat at the table. Clodfelter lists weather alongside enemy defenses, technology, and geography as "operational controls" that limit an air campaign. In Rolling Thunder, the winter monsoon's clouds made continuous bombing impossible from September to April, kept the campaign from starting in late February 1965, and delayed the 1966 oil raids. Johnson even ordered that the oil strikes not hit in marginal weather and not begin on a Sunday, and poor weather caused two postponements, during which the Wall Street Journal published the details.

Opinion: the common lesson is that campaigns built for one day are hostage to that day. Early voting spreads the risk over weeks, the way Linebacker's planners later got laser-guided bombs and better flying weather. Clodfelter's broader point is that the same tool works or fails depending on the conditions, and that applies to get-out-the-vote operations as neatly as to F-105s.

The monsoon never voted, but it vetoed plenty. Tomorrow's forecast is aiming for a smaller role. (S)

Lame Duck à l'Orange: The Pentagon's Budget Waits for the Voters

Seamus Daniels of CSIS published a useful piece last Wednesday on what tomorrow's vote means for the defense budget, and its first fact is that there isn't one yet. The Pentagon, like the rest of the government, is running on a continuing resolution through December 11. The administration asked for 1.15 trillion dollars in base discretionary defense funding plus 350 billion through reconciliation, and a June supplemental of nearly 70 billion, including 67.1 billion for the Defense Department, tied partly to the war with Iran.

Progress is, let's say, patient. The House passed its military construction bill. The defense bill, at about 1.072 trillion dollars, came out of committee but hasn't passed the full House, and neither bill has cleared Senate appropriators. Both chambers' budget resolutions provide 60 billion in defense reconciliation money, well short of the 350 billion requested. Daniels expects any reconciliation bill to land nearer 60 than 350, and notes that Senate Majority Leader John Thune has said securing votes after the midterms would be difficult. Congress has 22 scheduled Senate session days and 20 House days left, which is not a lot of days for a trillion and a half dollars.

History offers some comfort. In 2014, 2018, and 2022, Congress passed full-year defense appropriations before the calendar year ended, even though one chamber changed hands each time. If one or both chambers flip this time, Daniels suggests, the new majority might prefer a continuing resolution into 2027 to gain leverage, possibly tangled up with a debt-limit fight projected for early to mid-2027. If control stays put, reconciliation is still possible but faces disputes over offsets, the debt limit, and Ukraine. Either way, the lame duck will be busy.

Now go to tomorrow's reading, where the election calendar ran the air war too. In October 1966 the Joint Chiefs told Johnson that decisions over the next sixty days could determine the outcome of the war and pushed to expand the bombing. With Congressional elections on the horizon, Clodfelter writes, the President was lukewarm. Several "peace candidates" were challenging the war, and Johnson did not want to encourage them with heavy raids before the voting. On November 8, the chiefs laid out Rolling Thunder 52, their proposal for more bombing.

Opinion: this is Clodfelter's negative objectives in budget form. Johnson limited the war partly to protect the Great Society, the program he called, in language we can't print in full, the woman he really loved. Today's Congress is only timing a budget, which is a smaller thing than limiting a war, yet the mechanism is the same: the domestic calendar sets the tempo, and the military plan waits for the votes to be counted. Planners who treat the budget as a fixed input are making the Joint Chiefs' 1966 mistake, assuming the decision belongs to them.

The chiefs waited until November 8 to ask again in 1966. In 2026 they get to wait until December 11, which counts as progress. (S)

Leadership × Clodfelter · via Reuters in Military Times, October 30

Use Your Inside Voice: The Pentagon Asks the Baltics to Whisper

Reuters reported Friday that Pentagon officials have privately asked several NATO countries to tone down their public statements about the Russian threat. Four people familiar with the requests, three European officials and one American, described them, all anonymously. The countries named are Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, and Reuters couldn't determine whether others got the same message. One of the officials involved, according to the sources, was Elbridge Colby, the under secretary of defense for policy, who reportedly told a Baltic official there should be no "poking" of Russia and called comments that Russia was "cornered like a rat" unhelpful. Estonia's prime minister had called Putin a "rat in a corner" in September.

The tone, by every account, was polite. One European official called the advice "cordial" but "baffling," noting that Baltic intelligence services have recently played down the chance of Russia testing NATO's mutual defense commitment. Another summed up the message as "We are helping you. The Americans are there," followed by "Just don't be loud. Don't make noise." The Pentagon declined to comment, saying "Private conversations among allies are just that," and the three Baltic ministries and NATO didn't respond or declined. So the colorful lines are anonymous, and Colby's remarks were not publicly confirmed.

There is a real argument inside the story. Some Western officials think loud talk about Moscow plays into the Kremlin's hands. Others say governments have to be candid with voters about the money and sacrifice deterrence requires, especially with Russia apparently expanding sabotage in Europe, which Moscow denies.

Now go to tomorrow's reading, where the volume knob was policy. In early 1965, as Rolling Thunder began, McGeorge Bundy understood Johnson's desire to avoid "a loud public signal of a major policy change." Announcing the shift, Bundy argued, would push Hanoi to resist the bombing to save face. That left "only" the problem of telling the allies, and Bundy's note on that is the line to remember: "What we tell them is not likely to stay tightly secret," he wrote, "yet I think it is crucial that they not feel left out or uninformed."

Opinion: Bundy diagnosed both halves of the Baltic story sixty years early. A quiet signal can protect an adversary's face and keep escalation manageable, which is the theory behind asking allies to lower the volume. But a quiet message to allies will not stay quiet, and the allies who hear it will judge whether they were consulted or managed. The leadership lesson is that restraint works only when the partners feel included in it. Otherwise "don't be loud" sounds a lot like "don't be heard."

Bundy predicted the private message wouldn't stay secret. Four sources and one Reuters byline later, he's still batting a thousand. (S)

Defense & Conflict × Clodfelter · via Defense News, November 2

Mine, All Mine: Australia's Ghost Shark Brings Back an Old Idea

Australia has pulled the curtain off what its Ghost Shark undersea drones will carry, and the answer is mostly things that go bang. Defense News's Gordon Arthur reports that the Royal Australian Navy unveiled three payloads for Anduril's extra-large autonomous underwater vehicles on October 19 at Anduril Australia's Sydney factory. One is sea mines, about a dozen per vehicle, which could be laid covertly at maritime chokepoints in a conflict. Another is a swarm of Altius-600 loitering munitions for surveillance and strike. The third is logistics supply caches, whose users an official declined to name, saying people "can use their imagination." We have.

The numbers are large and a little vague. Australia is spending 1.7 billion Australian dollars, about 1.2 billion U.S., on a fleet officials describe as "dozens and dozens" of vehicles. The first Ghost Shark went to the Navy for testing in early 2026, and the factory opened less than a year before the unveiling. The vehicles can launch from shipping containers, small ships' sterns, or wharves. The motive is a gap: Australia's nuclear submarines are years away, and six aging Collins-class boats remain in service. Defence Industry Minister Pat Conroy said the system "places question marks in adversaries' minds," and the Navy chief, Vice Adm. Matt Buckley, said the Navy needs capability "at the speed of relevance," a phrase that has now been used by every navy on Earth.

Torpedoes and electronic warfare payloads are reportedly in development, so the menu will grow.

Now go to tomorrow's reading, where mines were the weapon one president refused and the next one used. Clodfelter writes that Johnson hesitated to mine North Vietnam's ports, partly because he doubted mining would cut imports, and mostly because he feared it would provoke the Soviets. All Soviet imports arrived by sea, and mining might humiliate Moscow by recalling the 1962 quarantine of Cuba. In 1972 Nixon, with a free hand from Moscow and Beijing, mined the ports in an operation code-named Pocket Money to complement Linebacker's attack on the overland routes. Kissinger saw the catch: the North had stockpiled goods for more than three years, and if the bold decision to bomb and mine "did not bring results fairly quickly," it would be attacked as a failure.

Opinion: Ghost Shark puts Clodfelter's question in a new hull. A covert minefield at a chokepoint is the quietest possible escalation, and it may also be the hardest to explain if discovered. Johnson doubted that mines would cut imports, and he feared even more that they would land on the wrong audience. Whoever plans to use a dozen silent mines needs to know in advance which capital will feel humiliated, and whether that is the point. Mines are cheap to lay and expensive to explain.

Johnson wouldn't mine Haiphong for fear of offending Moscow. Australia now builds minelayers in Sydney and sends a press release. Times change, and so do the question marks. (S)

Today's exercise: Schlight, The War in South Vietnam: The Years of the Offensive, 1965–1968, chapter by chapter

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, introduction and chapter I

Get Out of Jail Free, Fly a Combat Sortie

The Introduction, on the advisory years of 1955 to 1964, argues that America built the wrong force for the wrong war and kept the airmen out of the room. The advisory group shaped South Vietnam's army to stop a Korean-style invasion from the north. The Army owned the effort: after 1956, 535 of the group's 740 people were soldiers and 68 were airmen. The Air Force contingent then grew almost a hundredfold, to 6,600 people and 84 aircraft by early 1965, yet none of its jets could fly combat in South Vietnam. MACV's staff spent 80 percent of its effort on Army matters, and Schlight calls it "a unified command in name only." The Vietnamese Air Force grew to 285 planes while flying three-quarters of its fighter sorties as free strikes with no forward air controller. Its commander, Nguyen Cao Ky, was investing his talents in politics.

The image to keep from the Introduction is a Viet Cong soldier in a palm tree. Having learned that firing at aircraft gave away their positions, the guerrillas hid instead. One would carry a piece of green khaki around his waist; when a plane came, he climbed a palm, tied the cloth between two trees, and lay in it while his weight bent the palms over him. The Air Force's answer, the 19th Tactical Air Support Squadron, arrived in mid-1963 with 23 Bird Dogs and 44 pilots to train Vietnamese controllers in a year. The Vietnamese took over after that year, could not do the job, and by January 1965 the squadron was back in American hands. On schedule, in a manner of speaking.

Chapter I, on November 1964 to April 1965, argues that the advisory pretense collapsed in five months and the Air Force went first through the door. After a mortar attack destroyed five B-57s at Bien Hoa, and ARVN headquarters refused A-1s to a ground commander at Binh Gia, Westmoreland decided to lean on air power. On January 27 he could use jets, but only in emergencies and with mission-by-mission approval from the Joint Chiefs, the ambassador, and the Vietnamese high command. Jet missions were duly canceled while awaiting the Vietnamese signature. Pleiku brought Flaming Dart and Rolling Thunder, and on March 9 the restrictions largely fell. McConnell wanted Plan 39, fifteen strike squadrons ringing China, over Plan 32's ground war. He lost. The April Black Virgin raid dropped 816 tons in 417 sorties, and 1,200 ARVN searchers then saw no enemy. At Honolulu on April 20 the bombing strategy, "short-lived, and inadequately tested," gave way to a ground war in the south.

The person to remember is Col. William E. Bethea of the 34th Tactical Group at Bien Hoa. His strike planes had to carry a Vietnamese aboard, by then a student or observer. He was promised 44 observers, usually had about 22, and figured the full number would double his sortie rate. On one desperate occasion he took an observer out of jail, fed him, sent him aloft, and returned him to confinement after the mission. Schlight notes the man's real job was "to lend legitimacy to the employment of U.S. firepower." Legitimacy, it turned out, was available on day release. Washington dropped the rule on March 9, and the Air Force became the first service officially in open combat.

Connection. Opinion: Crane told us Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force think it had won, so it went back to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam. Schlight shows the invoice arriving. By late April the first call on U.S. aircraft in South Vietnam was the ground war, a close support job that two decades of doctrine, procurement, and training had left the Air Force poorly prepared for. Its instinct to centralize air and watch China was defensible. Guarding its aircraft from Hawaii while Army officers wrote MACV's plans in Saigon was not, and the staff seats it declined cost more than the squadrons it kept. Should PACAF have handed Westmoreland every Southeast Asia aircraft in exchange for real generals on the MACV staff? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapters II and III

Strategy to Follow Under Separate Cover

Chapter II, on April to June 1965, argues that American airpower went to war before anyone settled who controlled it. Westmoreland's new priorities put troops in contact first and missions outside South Vietnam last, handing him an effective veto over Rolling Thunder and Laos, while his air commander, Moore, remained a mere coordinator. Demand rose to meet supply: in early May, with the enemy dormant, Westmoreland canceled all bombing outside the country and asked the Navy for 90 more sorties a day, which parked a carrier at a spot called Dixie Station. Then the monsoon offensive came. At Song Be the province advisor called air power the deciding factor; Ba Gia took 651 sorties and more than 2,000 flares. The worst day was self-inflicted. On May 15 a faulty time-delay fuze set off a B-57 at Bien Hoa, killing 28 Americans and destroying 10 of 18 Canberras, double what the Viet Cong mortar crews had managed in November.

The scene to remember is the first Arc Light strike, June 18. Thirty B-52s left Guam for a Viet Cong base near Ben Cat. Political sources in Washington asked, 26 hours out, to move it up a day, and the embassy put a general aloft in a C-123 to supervise. Typhoon tailwinds put the bombers at the tanker rendezvous seven minutes early, the lead cell made a 360-degree turn into the planes behind, and two Stratofortresses collided and fell into the South China Sea. Twenty-seven bombed, dropping 1,300 bombs in 30 minutes, slightly more than half inside the box. Vietnamese search teams found no enemy and surprisingly little damage, apparently because the Viet Cong had been tipped off. The press mocked the wisdom of "using a sledgehammer to kill gnats." McConnell told his skeptical staff to leave SAC alone, since fifteen years of training it to bomb would not be improved upon in a few weeks.

Chapter III, on June to September 1965, argues that the United States took over the war by arithmetic. After Quat resigned in June, the ninth change of government since Diem, Air Vice Marshal Ky became prime minister. On July 20 Westmoreland sent McNamara a shopping list of 133 items, uncoordinated with CINCPAC. Its air math assumed six sorties a day per allied maneuver battalion, "a far from scientific number," and 1.2 sorties per plane: 44 battalions, nearly 15,000 sorties a month, 23 jet squadrons. On July 22 McConnell and the Navy's chief argued the plan was fruitless without concentrated bombing of the north; Johnson eliminated the alternatives one by one and on July 28 announced 125,000 troops. By September's end 360 Bird Dogs watched the whole country, and McConnell, conceding there were no truly good B-52 targets in the south, kept the bombers coming anyway.

The person to remember is McConnell in late June, arguing against sending the untested airmobile division inland to Pleiku. He told the other chiefs that recommending it without a secure road or air link to the coast would make them criminally responsible. The table went silent. A week later he told his own staff he doubted the division would go there, and if it did, it would not need supplying for long, because the Viet Cong would destroy it. His planning staff, meanwhile, posed questions nobody in the theater could answer: Will these troops be enough? Will the reserves be called? Can Hanoi actually be convinced it cannot win? The division arrived at Qui Nhon in mid-September, with the questions still pending.

Author's move. Opinion: Schlight's sharpest blade is a compliment. He calls Westmoreland's July memo "a masterpiece of staff work," then notes in the next sentence that it never mentioned a strategy. Official historians rarely call a four-star general's planning hollow, so he praises the paperwork and lets the timeline finish the job: MACV's formal concept for the war arrived at the end of August, a month after the President committed the forces. Strategy, he observes, was assumed to flow from the deployments. That is the polite way of saying the forces were ordered first and the reason was written afterward. If six sorties per battalion was unscientific, what number should the planners have used when the enemy held the initiative? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapters IV and V

Do You Have Any Influence? Yes, Plus a Sortie Quota

Chapter IV, on September to December 1965, argues that the Air Force went to war with the airplanes it had, built for a different war, and that they worked when someone let them. Bases lagged, so by January 1966 only nine of the thirteen fighter squadrons scheduled for South Vietnam had arrived. Qui Nhon had been surveyed from the air, an efficient way to learn later that you must move three million cubic feet of earth. Improvisation filled the gaps, from twelve F-5 Skoshi Tigers to the AC-47 gunship, pushed past a TAC commander who feared it would distract from Europe. At Ia Drang in November, B-52s flew in direct support of ground fighting for the first time, and the 1st Cavalry Division's nearly 300 helicopters ran down to 7,000 gallons of fuel against a daily need of 70,000, so the division called the Air Force. After Operation Harvest Moon in December, Westmoreland grumbled about three air forces in I Corps. "With the fervor and exactitude of theologians," Schlight writes, each side defended its doctrine.

The one thing to remember from Chapter IV is a radio call at Plei Me. In October 1965, two North Vietnamese regiments and the Viet Cong went after a small fort 25 miles south of Pleiku, held by 350 local irregulars and a 12-man American advisory team. With the runway unusable, C-123s dropped food and ammunition. An Army advisor, low on ammunition, called the forward air controller overhead: "Do you have any influence?" The pilot allowed that three birds were circling, about to drop. The advisor agreed he had influence, still the most honest definition of air superiority on record. The operation used almost 600 strikes, and for its first three days only air power stood between the garrison and the enemy.

Chapter V, on January to April 1966, is called Settling in and Sorting Out, and it argues that the Air Force finished deploying while losing the arguments over strategy and measurement. General John P. McConnell thought the United States was playing to the enemy's strength, and by late February he judged his hopes for a change in vain. In March McNamara sized the air war by arithmetic: 150 sorties a month per Army maneuver battalion, and so 11 new fighter squadrons instead of 16. Schlight warns that such figures carried the seeds of becoming measures of effectiveness. They sprouted in weeks. A bomb shortage canceled 233 sorties on April 7 alone, yet pressure to keep sortie rates up, lest the shortage become known, sent planes out with token loads, including white phosphorus bombs with no relation to the target. The new Seventh Air Force controlled only a third of the airplanes in South Vietnam. "One man's centralization was another's dispersion," Schlight observes.

The one thing to remember from Chapter V is a Saturday morning late in March, when McConnell and the Army chief, General Harold K. Johnson, sat in Johnson's Pentagon office and sketched a deal. The Army would give up its fixed-wing transports, including 104 Caribous; the Air Force would drop its ancient claim to operate all helicopters. The two had talked privately for six months, because their staffs treated the issue as bedrock. The draft went to those staffs with a caveat: anyone who tried to change its meaning would be fired. The text signed on April 6 was identical. Interservice peace, it turns out, takes two chiefs and a credible threat against the people who write the memos.

Opinion, offered as a seminar pro tip: when someone defends the sortie arithmetic, bring Ia Drang. In its critical week, strike planes averaged 23 sorties a day for each battalion in the field, against a planning factor of 5, and a fully committed division might have needed 276 a day. Two cautions. Ia Drang was the first B-52 use in direct support of ground fighting; the bombers were already at work farther south. And expect the counter that Ia Drang was a hit-and-run fight, which Schlight concedes is largely why it succeeded. The planning factor suited a quiet war and failed the one that kept happening. Did McNamara's fixed sortie rates measure what the ground war needed, or only what Washington could count? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapters VI and VII

Many Flowers, No Hot Showers, and No Bombers for Momyer

Chapter VI, on May to December 1966, is titled Setbacks for Centralized Air Power, and Schlight means it as a scorecard. Lieutenant General William W. Momyer took over the Seventh Air Force on July 1, 1966, a lifelong tactical airman whose first conviction was that air power works best under one commander. He then lost most of the fights to prove it. The C-130s, carrying two-thirds of the tonnage moved inside the country, belonged to a division in Japan 2,400 miles away and rotated in for two weeks at a time. Momyer asked for a C-130 wing; PACAF and McConnell's analysts said no, and the Hercules stayed based outside Vietnam for the rest of the war. His deputy was blunt: "We don't even do it in the Air Force." Momyer thought 150 B-52 sorties a month against specific targets would do. Westmoreland used the bombers as long-range artillery and pushed toward 800 a month, an ordnance bill of half a billion dollars for 1967. The Air Force did win one: it absorbed the Army's six Caribou companies.

The one thing to remember from Chapter VI is the B-52 compromise of December 1966. After months of everyone holding firm, the Joint Chiefs proposed moving the small SAC liaison office from MACV to the Seventh Air Force, growing it from five planners to seventeen, and having it plan and coordinate Arc Light requests. Westmoreland accepted every point except the one that mattered: planning strike requests would stay at MACV headquarters. The new office opened in January 1967. Momyer got a better seat for watching the bombers. Meanwhile, ground teams entered the target areas after only about 10 percent of the strikes, so the chiefs reported instead that 99.7 percent of planned bombs had been dropped. Precision, of a kind.

Chapter VII, on 1966, is called The Frontier Spirit, and it argues that the airmen's ability to adapt to Vietnam mattered as much as tactics and doctrine, with self-help as the phrase that echoed through the unit reports. Support units trailed the squadrons by months; the Air Force's civil engineers in Saigon numbered six people in mid-1965. Tan Son Nhut went from 7,780 airmen to over 15,000 in a year. Of 46 pilots who passed through the 416th Tactical Fighter Squadron between July 1966 and March 1967, only 16 completed a year with it. The Nha Trang maintenance squadron, authorized 26 chief master sergeants, had 42, enough, its supervisor said, to run the place for five years. Altitude rules shrank a napalm drop to "the size of a good campfire." And yet the Seventh kept 75 percent of its attack planes flying through 1966, above the 71 percent standard.

The one thing to remember from Chapter VII is a slogan. At Bien Hoa early in the year, seven officers or twenty enlisted men shared each sixteen-by-thirty-two-foot hut, rats scurried everywhere, the showers had no hot water, and one heater for shaving served forty men if they hurried. Momyer's Operation Spruce Up, meanwhile, put flowers along the main streets at many bases. The saying around Bien Hoa: "Many flowers but no hot showers." Schlight's point is that morale held anyway. Combat pay was $65 a month, savings deposits earned 10 percent when banks paid about 4, and airmen built much of their own comfort by hand.

Opinion, on the author's move: Schlight builds Chapter VII from the unit reports of the period and lets airmen gripe on the record, about rents, two-price merchants, and young captains bumped by older field grade officers. Then he closes with a match against the Navy's carriers and rules that airmen did at least as well as the other services. Read the scorecard. Navy planes flew 1.21 sorties a day to the Air Force's 0.93; Air Force planes dropped 1.21 tons per sortie to the Navy's 0.94. Schlight calls ordnance the more important area. That is a defensible call, and it is also the Air Force's home-field metric, the mirror image of Chapter V, where sortie counts flattered the Navy and Admiral Sharp swore by them. An official historian deserves credit for printing both columns. The seminar should notice who picked the column that wins. If every service grades itself on the number its airplanes were built to win, who in 1966 was measuring whether any of it hurt the enemy? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapters VIII and IX

The Air Force Fought Two Wars in 1966: One With the Enemy, One With the Spreadsheet

Chapter VIII, on air operations in 1966, argues that the Air Force did the job when asked and then could not say what the job was. The year opened with Masher, the war's first division-size search and destroy operation, near Bong Son on January 24. MACV renamed it White Wing for the second phase, wary of the "cruel and inhuman implications" of the name. The operation did not change. Up north, Hastings and the Tally Ho campaign blunted the 324B Division, and Westmoreland claimed the two southern route packages of North Vietnam; Momyer noted that by that logic all of North Vietnam belonged to MACV. Then came the counting. Of over 355,000 Air Force sorties in the South, only 74,000 were strikes. Through October, 77 percent of strikes were filed as "direct air support" and 15 percent as close air support. An F-100 squadron commander admitted the line was left to individuals and an "untrue picture is presented." On November 1 the Seventh Air Force redefined close air support, two-thirds of the sorties switched columns, and nobody flew any differently. The Army, meanwhile, asked for air in one ground clash out of ten.

The one thing to remember is A Shau. By early March 1966, 2,000 enemy soldiers surrounded the Special Forces camp near the Laotian border and its 210 irregulars and 10 American advisors. On March 10, Maj. Bernard F. Fisher led four Skyraiders in under the clouds. Maj. Dafford W. Myers was hit and crashlanded on the airstrip. Told a helicopter was 15 or 20 minutes away, Fisher landed himself on the second try, dodging bullets, oil drums, and pieces of Myers' airplane, pulled Myers aboard, and took off at treetop level. The camp was evacuated that evening despite 210 sorties that day. Airpower could pull one man off that runway. It could not keep the camp.

Chapter IX, on refinements of aircraft and munitions in 1967, argues that the war forced an Air Force built for nuclear war to re-equip, and then rethink, on the fly. By 1964 the technological base for nonnuclear weapons was almost nonexistent. The fix was concurrency, running research, engineering, testing, and production at once. By May 1967 only fifteen percent of Seventh Air Force munitions were the older kind, and the battlefield became the final testing ground. McConnell's July 1967 go-ahead on guided bombs would put smart bombs in the war a year later. The VNAF got twenty F-5s on June 1, despite 32 major accidents per 100,000 flying hours against the Air Force's 7.4 (July's mishaps included taxiing into a fence). Doctrine came last. Built from theory "like an idealistic youth," it was turning to experience, though published doctrine was four years off.

The one thing to remember is the CBU-24. It went from conception to the theater in 9 months: a canister that opened like a clamshell and spilled over 600 bomblets, each the size of a flashlight battery. The jungle had been hard on cluster bombs since Masher, when many failed to go off in the foliage and troops picked them up not knowing what they were. The CBU-24 made up 42 percent of all cluster bombs used in the war.

Opinion: Schlight notes that Air Force doctrine mostly dismissed Korea as a temporary diversion from nuclear deterrence. Readers of Crane know why: Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded, so it went back to planning for general war and, Crane argues, paid for it in Vietnam. Chapter IX is the bill: bomb fins damaged at jet speeds, bomb lugs that did not fit the racks. Chapter VIII records the interdiction promise the Air Force would keep for the rest of the war: air power alone could not completely halt a determined enemy, only make him divert enough energy to weaken his attack. That is a humbler promise than the one 15,000 rail cuts failed to keep in 1952, and a more honest one. Lessons, like cluster bombs, apparently get their final testing in combat. Was the Air Force's push to get "interdiction" back into the reports about accuracy, or about protecting a doctrine its own airmen were already rewriting? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapter X

Two Air Forces, One Valley: How Khe Sanh Got Momyer the Keys

Chapter X, on air operations from January 1967 through March 1968, argues two things at once. Close air support kept rescuing troops in the war zones, and divided control of aircraft in the north kept producing confusion until Khe Sanh forced a single manager. Junction City opened February 22 with 845 troopers of the 173d Airborne Brigade jumping from 16 C-130s in the war's first parachute assault. It drew 5,002 fighter-bomber sorties and 126 B-52 sorties, the first time the bombers supported a major ground operation with preplanned sorties. At Ap Gu, Lt. Col. Alexander M. Haig credited tactical air, and the CBU especially. The 1966 paperwork war got its sequel: in February the Joint Chiefs, at the Air Force's urging, swapped "direct air support" for "air interdiction" in the reports, with the word "detailed" doing the heavy lifting in the new definition.

Up north it was messier. On March 2, 1967, two F-4Cs misread a radio bearing and bombed Lang Vei instead of a road in Laos, killing 100 civilians and wounding 250. During Hickory in May, FACs at Dong Ha spent a night drafting a control plan for the next morning; at three in the morning MACV told them Hillsboro would control them instead, and the night's work went for naught. In Neutralize that fall, Marine artillery once stayed silent twenty-one hours out of twenty-four so planes could work, while the enemy kept firing. Incoming rounds still fell from 7,400 in September to 3,600 in October. The Misty FACs in two-seat F-100Fs proved jets could hunt: more than twice as many targets were struck when they were along.

The one thing to remember is Dak To, November 1967. A flight of A-1Es dropped napalm a hundred meters in front of the friendly line, then seventy-five, then fifty. The ground commander wanted it closer. The next flight put it at twenty-five meters, and the voice on the radio reported: "I think we've moved it close enough. The trees are burning over my head." The Air Force flew 2,096 close air support sorties there, and 1,650 of the enemy were known dead. A defecting North Vietnamese colonel later said local commanders wanted the battle for combat experience. Schlight's verdict on the fall border fights is that the enemy lost every one, and was "quite willing to exchange men for experience."

Then Khe Sanh. Trucks on the roads around the base rose from 480 in September to 6,315 in December. Niagara began January 22, 1968; when Admiral Sharp refused Momyer full control, the compromise let him direct and coordinate all the planes, while the Marines kept the four zones closest to the base, which amounted to two air forces in one valley. Sensors supplied forty percent of the data the Marines used to aim their artillery. After a Marine C-130 was destroyed on February 10, Momyer stopped the Hercules from landing, and two-thirds of all supplies arrived from planes that never touched down. On March 6 a C-123 went down on approach, killing all forty-eight aboard. The totals: over 24,400 fighter and B-52 sorties, 100,000 tons of ordnance, 12,500 tons airlifted, 200 Americans dead, and an estimated 10,000 enemy casualties. The frontal assault never came. On March 7 Momyer got "mission direction" over Marine planes in all of I Corps, a phrase invented "to avoid the emotional overtones of operational control." The first single-manager mission flew March 22.

Opinion, seminar pro tip: someone will cite Khe Sanh as proof that airpower wins. Schlight hands you the rebuttal himself. Khe Sanh was not typical; for the first time in the war, the enemy offered the kind of target tactical air was built for. It also cost roughly half the air effort in South Vietnam for two months and gave the enemy room to prepare a second offensive in May. And notice how the single manager actually won: Westmoreland's case rested on the Army outnumbering the Marines in I Corps two to one, which left the question open anywhere the Marines fought alone. Command arrangements, it turns out, get settled by headcount. If Khe Sanh was the untypical battle, why should it decide how air power was commanded in the typical ones? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Schlight, chapter XI (conclusion)

The Air Force Counted Everything Except Whether It Was Winning

Chapter XI, the Conclusion, closes the books in March 1968 with an audit. Schlight calls the previous three years "the largest, most sustained ground support campaign in the history of aerial warfare." The 58,000 airmen in South Vietnam were only 11 percent of the American military presence, yet the Air Force flew one and a quarter million combat and combat support missions. Aircraft in the country went from 84 to 1,085. The share of the whole Air Force parked in South Vietnam rose from 1.5 percent to 19 percent, and with Thailand and the Pacific ring added in, roughly one third of the worldwide inventory was busy with this war. For a service built around nuclear deterrence, that is quite a side project.

Then comes the catch. Schlight's verdict is that the Air Force had neither a war objective of its own in the South nor reliable data to measure results. MACV set ground objectives and filed air strikes under support, so the Air Force graded itself on readiness rates, response times, sortie counts and tons dropped. "An enormous quantity of data described the Air Force's effort, but little its progress," as Schlight puts it. Bomb damage assessment was suspect, since the canopy hid results and pilots often reported "smoliage," meaning smoke and foliage. When MACV dropped the word "interdiction" from sortie reporting in 1966, the Seventh Air Force simply widened its definition of close air support to cover some of it. Problem solved, on paper.

The scorecard still has entries. Air power was decisive against the 1965 offensive: 644 sorties saved the camp at Dong Xoai, and at Plei Me only air power stood between the garrison and the attackers for the first three days. Over the next two years 46,000 tactical strike sorties and 3,300 B-52 sorties supported 73 major ground offensives. Command was another story. The McConnell-Johnson agreement of early 1966 merely recognized that helicopters were never coming to the Air Force. The single manager arrangement of March 1968 was quickly modified to return substantial control to the Marines. "This prevented a true test of the single manager concept."

Behind the shooting, the ledger is mixed. Airlift to and from Southeast Asia leapt from 700 million ton miles to 5.7 billion, and medical evacuation held deaths after reaching a hospital to about 1 percent, against 4.5 percent in World War II. Yet at Da Nang the two airlift control centers sat a mile apart, which made coordination virtually impossible. Schlight ranks reconnaissance as the most disappointing effort; Army requests could sit in Army channels for two weeks. Between 1959 and 1967 the Air Force spent just $170,000 on research for aircraft guns. The Vietnamese Air Force had 55 percent of its people on leave when Tet struck and got 90 percent back within 72 hours. By 1968 the Air Force had reached a qualified acceptance of flexible response, which Schlight calls a watershed.

The one thing to remember is a stopwatch. A ground unit calls for help. A diverted fighter averaged twenty minutes before its bombs came off, and a scrambled one took twice as long, even though no point in South Vietnam was more than fifteen minutes from the nearest jets. The rest of the clock went to marking targets and briefing forward air controllers. Meanwhile a third of all firefights were over in fifteen minutes, half in thirty, and three fourths within an hour. Even the twenty minute response reached only 55 percent of engagements. The Air Force had cut response time in half since 1965 and was proud of it. The Army was still waiting.

Seminar pro tip: three numbers get misquoted. The 1.25 million missions include combat support, so they are not strike sorties. The one third of the inventory includes Thailand and the Pacific bases; South Vietnam alone held 19 percent. Twenty minutes is the diverted average, and a scramble took forty. Expect someone to read President Johnson's superlatives about Khe Sanh aloud. The answer is Schlight's own Chapter X, which warns that Khe Sanh was not typical, since the enemy finally massed into the kind of target tactical air does best. Opinion: the deepest point in this chapter is the measurement problem, because whoever owns the objective owns the scorecard, and the Air Force owned neither. If the Air Force had no objective of its own in South Vietnam, what should it have measured instead of sorties and tons? (S)

Schlight: exercise recall

  • What were Plan 32 and Plan 39, and which did McConnell favor?

    Plan 32 put the weight on the ground in Vietnam; Plan 39 put it in the air and seas around China, with fifteen strike squadrons in Taiwan, Okinawa, Japan, and Korea. McConnell, backed by PACAF's Harris, favored Plan 39 (21).

  • Why did McConnell oppose sending an Army division in March 1965?

    His staff estimated it would take more than five divisions to seal off the north at the 17th parallel; he wanted land routes closed by air and sea routes by the Navy first, with about sixty days to see results (23).

  • What did McConnell say about the first Arc Light strike's critics?

    "If SAC hasn't learned to bomb in fifteen years of extensive training, we are not going to teach them how in a few weeks" (54).

  • What is Schlight's two-part verdict on the Air Force in South Vietnam?

    Air power was militarily successful, but the Air Force had neither its own war objective nor enough reliable data to quantify results (290, 309).

Schlight: chapter recall

  • What did Col. William Bethea do when he couldn't find a Vietnamese observer for a strike mission?

    He took one out of jail, fed him, flew him on the mission, and returned him to confinement afterward, because the rules required a Vietnamese aboard to make U.S. firepower look legitimate. S 20

  • What planning factor did Westmoreland use to size the 1965 tactical air buildup, and how did Schlight describe it?

    Six tactical sorties a day for each non-Vietnamese maneuver battalion, which Schlight calls "a far from scientific number." With 1.2 sorties per plane per day, it produced the requirement for 23 jet squadrons. S 65

  • At Ia Drang, how many strike sorties a day did each battalion get in the critical week, against what planning factor?

    About 23 sorties a day per battalion, against the planning factor of 5 used to size the fighter force; a fully committed division might have needed 276 a day. S 106

  • What did Westmoreland refuse when the Joint Chiefs moved a SAC planning office to the Seventh Air Force in December 1966?

    He accepted everything except giving it the job of planning and coordinating B-52 strike requests, which stayed at MACV headquarters, so Momyer gained no real control of the bombers. S 150

  • What happened to the Air Force's close air support numbers when the Seventh Air Force redefined the category on November 1, 1966?

    Two-thirds of strike sorties suddenly counted as close air support instead of direct air support, although the Air Force flew exactly as before. S 216

  • What did Momyer get on March 7, 1968, and why did Admiral Sharp finally agree?

    "Mission direction" over Marine aircraft in all of I Corps, granted after Westmoreland argued that the Army now outnumbered the Marines there two to one. S 286

  • Why does Schlight say the Air Force could not measure its own effectiveness in South Vietnam?

    It had no war objective of its own, since MACV set ground objectives and treated air as support, and it lacked reliable data, so it counted sorties, tons, and response times instead of effects on the enemy. S 290

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
