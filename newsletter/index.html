Counterpoint: Weinberger Wrote Six Tests, and Kosovo Took the Test Anyway

Henriksen wins this one. Demanding clear objectives before every fight never kept America out of the messy wars. It left the military with nothing to offer when they came anyway.

His witnesses are the generals who had to fight the next war, and they are blunt. Gen. Sir Rupert Smith told him that soldiers are "stupid to ask for that degree of precision" from politicians, and that soldiers sitting in the politicians' chairs would be no cleverer. Naumann went further. Given the choice between tolerating killings and expulsions or acting "with the vague hope that it might work," he said, you choose the latter. Then Henriksen hands part of the blame back to the uniforms: when politicians said "we have got to do something," the military had no adequate reply. France's General Kelche drew the practical lesson and set up a permanent cell to prepare crisis options, because politicians with no options "will opt to wait or do nothing." That's the whole argument in one office. A general who only answers "not yet" gets left out of the decision.

Kitfield's side deserves its best shot, and it's a good one. At the National Press Club on November 28, 1984, Caspar Weinberger laid out six tests for sending forces into combat: vital interests at stake, enough force to win decisively, clearly defined political and military objectives, forces sized to meet them, a reasonable assurance that the public would support the war, and combat only as a last resort. His speechwriters warned him that six was an awkward number and asked for three. He kept six. (Anyone who has argued with a Secretary of Defense will understand.) Powell doubted that one doctrine could fit every crisis, but he liked the questions, and he judged that the Marines sent into Lebanon's five-faction civil war would never have passed them. Kitfield wrote in 1995, and his proof is Desert Storm: an invasion to reverse, a clear objective, a large force, and a quick win. The tests produce good wars of that kind.

So why does it still lose? Because Desert Storm is the only kind of war the tests can vouch for, and Kitfield's own epilogue sees the other kind coming. McCaffrey traveled to Bosnia to weigh the "five-alarm risks" of joining a civil war, and by the last pages Somalia, and potentially Bosnia and Korea, "beckoned." The tests had no answer for those. We'll concede one point to Kitfield's generals: Smith himself, asked about Kosovo in particular, agreed that more clarity was needed there. Wanting clarity is fine. Refusing to plan without it is the mistake. Tomorrow's book shows where that refusal leads. The first-order effect was real: the tests filtered bad wars out of the Pentagon. The second-order effect is that the wars didn't go away; they moved. Fuller says Weinberger's "parsimonious attitude" toward deploying the military denied the Reagan hardliners the Pentagon's resources, so Shultz, Casey, and North turned to other people to do their fighting. The third-order effect, which is our reading of Fuller's opening chapters, is that one of those other people was the CIA, whose Counterterrorism Center grew out of that hardline philosophy and later ran the drone campaign.

The line to say at noon: "Weinberger's tests decided whether the Pentagon would fight, not whether America would, and Henriksen and Fuller show where the excluded wars went." Saying no, it turns out, is a policy only for the people who are listening. In plain terms, a service that refuses an unclear war doesn't stop the war; it only gives up its say in how the war is fought. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Fuller (tomorrow's book) · extrapolation

A Cheap Hold Is Only Cheap Until the Reapers Start Falling

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare for it, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the war with Iran without a settlement. His thesis: Airmen should prepare now to hold the Strait of Hormuz the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Christopher Fuller, not Shaun's answer.

Fuller describes a cheap hold that already worked. For a decade, he writes, the United States kept a lethal presence in Pakistan, Somalia, and Yemen without bases in those countries, and "semipermeable frontiers patrolled by unmanned aircraft" let trade, people, and money keep moving while giving Washington more control. He even notes that the drone bases became a springboard toward places such as Iranian airspace. The presence changed enemy behavior at little cost. In an October 2010 letter, bin Laden told his men to meet no more than once or twice a week, and then only when the clouds were heavy. A strait patrol that makes the other side plan around the weather is doing its job. (It is also the rare military program whose success shows up in a meteorology report.)

Fuller cuts against the plan in two places. The first is the airspace. Armed drones suited the rural tribal areas, Yemen's deserts, and Somalia's camps, he says, but against the Islamic State's antiaircraft weapons the slow drones lost their chance to loiter, and in the campaign against it they mostly found targets for faster jets. Iran is not Waziristan. This week's reporting says Iran has downed about fifty MQ-9s since February 28, and it was fighters, not drones, that struck Qeshm on Tuesday. A cost per kill has to include the drones lost per week. The second is that cheap makes easy. Fuller cites Peter Singer's warning that the technology makes a military response look costless, and David Cortright's that this "reduces the political inhibitions" against deadly force. That is Shaun's own stated limit, that a cheap war is easier to start, now with a book behind it.

Opinion, extrapolated: Fuller would endorse the small footprint and question the idea that cost felt at home is what ends American wars. His drone war didn't end when the cost was felt. It drew down, and the improvised system Obama inherited grew into what Ian Shaw called a "Predator empire." The risk of a cheap hold may be that nobody notices it is still running, which argues for making each renewal a decision someone has to defend. Two questions for the draft:

  1. What does the hold cost per week if Iran keeps shooting down drones at this week's rate?
  2. Who has to vote to keep it going, so that it stays a choice instead of a habit? (S)

Finding the Target Was Always the Expensive Part

Almost every book on our shelf finds that locating the target is harder than hitting it. Schlight reports that until the end of October 1966, 3 percent of Air Force sorties in South Vietnam struck enemy soldiers actually engaged with allied forces. Olsen says the Desert Storm planners knew little about Iraq beyond the locations of buildings and communications. Haun counts airstrikes destroying about 10 percent of Iraqi armor before the ground war. Lambeth has 79 percent of NATO's precision munitions hitting their targets over Kosovo, while attacks on fielded Serb forces were largely ineffective. Naumann put it best to the North Atlantic Council: you cannot stop the individual murderer going from village to village with a knife "from the air." Accuracy kept improving. The address book did not.

Last Wednesday's paper item found the other half of the lesson in Olsen. Thirteen-foot decoy drones drew Iraq's radars into the fight on the first night, radar activity fell by more than 90 percent within three days, and for the rest of the war the Iraqis were reluctant to turn their radars on. Bin Laden's instruction to meet only under heavy clouds, in Fuller, is the same effect. A sensor that stays overhead changes behavior even when it destroys very little. (Persistence is the one airpower virtue nobody has ever lost a promotion over.)

Fuller, tomorrow, gives the bridge between those books. Over Bosnia in 1995, Clinton was shocked at how little his agencies could tell him about Sarajevo. Cloud cover blinded the satellites and U-2s, Serb SA-6s made low-level scouting too dangerous, and the Serbs hid their weapons in barns and woods during the hours they knew satellites passed overhead. Fuller says that search for better intelligence for Clinton-era airstrikes, along with the Reagan-era fights with terrorists, drove drone development more than the hunt for bin Laden did. Yesterday's item on Braver and quiet resignation asked when an officer should walk away from an order. Today's counterpoint asks the institutional version, when a military should refuse a war, and Fuller's answer is that the refused war often turns up later at another agency. Opinion: the shelf's oldest problem is finding the enemy, and its newest solution created a new set of problems. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 4, Day 3 · precise language

"Militant" Is Doing a Lot of Work in That Sentence

A key word in an argument has to mean one thing every time it appears. Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren, in How to Read a Book, call this coming to terms. Their fifth rule tells readers to find the important words and work out exactly how the author uses them, and they define the goal plainly: "a term is a word used unambiguously." Most words in a book need no attention, they add; the ones that matter are the words an author uses in a special way. Writers can apply the rule from the other side. Find your own important words and pin each one down before the reader has to.

Fuller, tomorrow's book, shows what happens when a key word goes undefined. In June 2011, John Brennan said there hadn't been "a single collateral death" from drone strikes. Critics pointed to a May 2012 New York Times report that the White House counted all military-age males in a strike zone as combatants unless intelligence proved them innocent after their deaths. The New American Foundation, defining the word differently, counted 152 to 191 civilians killed between 2004 and October 2012. The two sides were counting the same bodies. They disagreed about the word. Before: "Drone strikes killed few civilians." After: "By counting every military-age male in a strike zone as a combatant, the White House could report almost no civilian deaths; the New American Foundation, counting differently, found 152 to 191 from 2004 to October 2012." The second sentence tells the reader where the number came from. The legal debate in Fuller works the same way: whether a strike is a "targeted killing" or an "assassination," banned since President Ford's 1976 executive order, decides whether it is legal.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: run a search for each key term in your draft and read every hit. If "hold," "deterrence," or "partner" means something different on page eight than on page two, define it once early and keep that meaning, or the reader will pick one for you. (S)

Fifty Reapers Later, the Fighters Got the Call

U.S. fighters struck an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island on Tuesday, after Iran sent one-way attack drones at facilities used by American forces in Bahrain, Chris Gordon reports in Air & Space Forces Magazine. A U.S. official called the strike self-defense against "IRGC aggression." Iran claimed it hit a radar site at Isa Air Base. CENTCOM and the Bahrain Defense Force said the drones were intercepted with no damage or injuries. Qeshm sits in the Persian Gulf just outside the Strait of Hormuz and is a frequent U.S. target. The article's most striking number is buried: according to people familiar with the matter, Iran has downed about fifty MQ-9 Reapers and several MQ-1s since hostilities began on February 28. Iran also claimed a new MQ-1 kill, which the magazine could not verify. Behind it all, the April ceasefire ended the big strike campaign, a blockade continues, a tanker exploded after an attack on Monday, and talks have stalled.

Fuller explains the division of labor. Armed drones, he writes, suited targets hiding in Pakistan's tribal areas, Yemen's deserts, and Somalia's camps. Against the Islamic State's antiaircraft weapons, the slow drones lost their chance to loiter and hunt, and the Pentagon used them mostly to find targets for "faster moving, more evasive jets," as the GNAT-750s had done over Bosnia. Fifty Reapers suggests Iran's airspace is closer to the second case. The drone still sees. The fighter shoots. That is the old reconnaissance-and-strike pairing that the "see it/shoot it" option was supposed to merge into one aircraft. (In contested air, it seems, the Reaper is back to just looking.)

Fuller's conclusion also predicted the drones coming the other way. He notes that Iran sent its own drones to defend Maliki's government in Iraq, and he says it was only a matter of time before the "relatively cheap and easy Radio Shack solution" was adopted by America's rivals, for the same reason the CIA wanted it: to strike targets in places they could not otherwise reach. Tuesday's drones aimed at Bahrain are that sentence carried out.

Opinion: the exchange shows both sides doing the cheap thing and paying for it in hardware, not people, which is how a limited war stays limited. It is also how one lasts. Vice President Vance said on Monday that the president "is not going to let this become a forever war," and Fuller's drone war is a reminder that the wars America never feels are the ones it forgets to end. Fifty Reapers is a lot of aircraft to lose in a war that isn't supposed to be happening. (S)

Writing drills

  • Define the term: "The hold will deter Iran."

    Say what "hold" and "deter" mean once, early, and keep those meanings: which forces, which waters, and which Iranian action the hold is meant to prevent. Then search every use of both words and check that each one matches (Adler and Van Doren, 100–101).

  • Pin down "precise": "The Kosovo campaign was precise."

    "Seventy-nine percent of NATO's precision munitions hit their targets, by Clark's count at the end of May, though attacks on fielded Serb forces were largely ineffective" (Lambeth, 225). "Precise" now means hitting the aim point, which is different from achieving the effect.

  • Review (Tuesday, evidence): show the source chain for "Iran has shot down fifty Reapers."

    "According to people familiar with the matter, Iran has downed about fifty MQ-9s since February 28, Air & Space Forces Magazine reported on November 17." The reader now knows it is anonymous sourcing, relayed by one outlet (Booth et al., 135–136).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
