Seminar 11 · Olsen, Strategic Air Power in Desert Storm · "Cold War Airpower begins 'Unipolar Moment'"

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered

  1. How did the strategy evolve from conception to implementation? It began as John Warden's Instant Thunder, built in the Air Staff's Checkmate office days after the invasion on his Five Rings model, and it promised to move Saddam with six to nine days of strikes on 84 targets. Schwarzkopf liked it as a retaliation option and kept it as Phase I of a four-phase plan, which he quietly had his own staff draft (chapter 2). Horner sent Warden home after the August 20 briefing but kept his planners, and David Deptula carried the concept into Buster Glosson's Black Hole cell, where the target list grew from 84 to 481 by January 15 (chapter 3). Asked how the final plan differed from Instant Thunder, Horner said, "How does a butterfly differ from a larva?"
  2. Did Col Warden's strategy achieve its promise? Half of it. Olsen's verdict is that the campaign degraded Iraqi war-making and put fog and friction into the regime's decisions, but it did not meet the "hoped for, but unstated" aim of changing the regime (introduction). Forty-three days of bombing didn't collapse Iraqi command and control; the Republican Guard withdrew in good order and the regime put down two revolts (conclusions). Olsen still judges the January plan "a huge improvement" on Warden's August concept.
  3. Were Tactical Air Command's doctrinal concepts more effective than the premise behind Instant Thunder? Not in 1990, by Olsen's account. TAC had accepted a subordinate role under AirLand Battle, and the 31 Initiatives treated air power as fire support for the ground forces; Horner's margin note at the July 1990 Internal Look exercise was "Build a hose and point it where the ground commander sees that it's needed" (chapter 1). When Glosson asked for an offensive option in August, nothing existed beyond defending Saudi Arabia except Instant Thunder (chapter 3). The best case for TAC comes from Haun, whose appendix credits the direct attack on Iraq's fielded forces with the decisive work.
  4. How much did the Goldwater-Nichols Act reforms affect interservice cooperation? A great deal, mostly by centralizing power. The 1986 law gave unprecedented authority to the chairman and the theater commanders, so the Air Staff, where Checkmate sat, had no mandate to plan a war (chapter 2). It also meant the service chiefs met the president only when invited, and General McPeak reached Camp David on December 1 after Edward Luttwak called Senator John Warner, who reportedly called the White House (chapter 3).
  5. Were there any strategic mistakes made during the war? Yes, and Olsen names three. After the al-Firdos bunker strike on February 13 killed civilians, Powell and Schwarzkopf approved every Baghdad strike themselves, which amounted to a nine-day bombing halt on the capital; and when division commanders pressed Schwarzkopf, he shifted bombs from the Republican Guard to frontline conscripts, so the Guard escaped largely intact and helped crush the southern uprising (chapter 5). The biggest mistake was that nobody planned the end: senior officers held that "war termination is not our job," and Bush urged Iraqis to rise up in late February without backing them (conclusions).
  6. Why did it end when it did? Because Washington wanted a ground war first. On February 11, Saddam told the Soviet envoy Yevgeny Primakov he would withdraw without compensation, but by then Washington demanded that the army leave its tanks and artillery behind, and Saddam chose to bargain (chapter 5). Olsen argues the United States had already decided to have its ground war, partly because ending without the massed Army might hurt the case for a large Army and would hand Moscow the credit. Haun adds that the ceasefire itself came early because leaders feared the images from the "highway of death."
  7. What was the perception of Saddam Hussein and his military? Washington misread him before and during the war. Before it, NSD-26 sought to bring him "into the family of nations," and senior officers expected at most a grab of the Rumaila oilfield and two islands (chapter 1). Olsen's profile finds him "both a rational and calculating decision-maker" who trusted nobody (chapter 4), but one fed flattery: a council member told him, "I saw you in my dream. You were victorious on your white horse," and he did not grasp the bombing's damage until Primakov showed him pictures in mid-February.

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. (S)

Today's Reading · Olsen, Strategic Air Power in Desert Storm

Olsen Grades the Bombing From the Receiving End

John Andreas Olsen's main contribution is the Iraqi side of the ledger. He interviewed high-ranking Iraqi officials and officers, and they agreed the regime was partly paralyzed, though he concedes their accounts are subjective ("where you stand depends on where you sit," as he puts it). That puts him on the shelf beside Zhang and Nguyen, who also graded an American air war from the capital being bombed. Every book that does this reaches a version of the same finding: the target was more complicated than the targeteers thought.

His closing verdict is careful to the point of being lawyerly. The campaign incapacitated the regime "only in relative terms": it kept Saddam from decisive offensive action and did not stop him from securing his own survival. The attacks on Warden's inner ring took only 2 percent of the air effort. And Saddam, Olsen notes, called the war the "Mother of all Battles," not of all wars, and lived to fight another day. (He had read his Clausewitz, or at least his own press.)

Yesterday's counterpoint ended on a rule from Clodfelter and Randolph: bombing works when the demand is small. Olsen tests the opposite case, a campaign whose hoped-for demand was the regime itself, and the result is the rule's mirror image. (S)

Counterpoint · Haun against Olsen

Counterpoint: Haun Says the Army Got the Bombs and the Credit

Olsen's central claim is that the strategic air campaign against the Iraqi leadership succeeded at degrading the regime and failed at changing it, because Washington never understood how Saddam held power. The failure, in his account, belongs to intelligence and policy: the planners knew little about the regime beyond the locations of buildings and communications, and the White House never declared regime change an objective. Fix the knowledge and the politics, the argument implies, and the concept works. He cites Kosovo and Afghanistan as proof that air power can change a regime when the aim is declared.

Phil Haun, whom we read Monday, makes the strongest case against him. Haun's appendix scores Instant Thunder and the interdiction campaign as the only modern air operations where most operational and environmental factors favored air power, "yet both failed." His evidence that Saddam kept control is concrete. Two weeks into the air war Saddam traveled to Basra to meet his commanders and sent two mechanized divisions toward Al Khafji; he approved Scud targeting daily; he approved the order to withdraw from Kuwait on February 26; and after the war he used the Republican Guard to crush the Shia and Kurdish uprisings. What worked, Haun argues, was the direct attack on the army: 25 to 30 percent of it was attrited before the ground war, desertions did most of that, and armed reconnaissance strikes wrecked its supply distribution. (The paralysis, it turns out, set in at the front.)

Olsen's own chapter 5 helps Haun more than he might like. When Olsen explains why Saddam told Primakov on February 11 that he would withdraw, the reason he gives is that Saddam had visited his troops in Kuwait on January 23 and Basra four days later and had seen that the bombing, "especially of military formations," was increasingly devastating. That is the fielded-forces campaign doing the coercing. Olsen's interviews are his strength, and nobody else on the shelf has them; but Iraqi officers telling a researcher their regime felt foggy cannot show that the fog changed a decision. Haun's evidence has the reverse problem. He uses the Gulf War Air Power Survey and can show that Saddam's orders still went out; he can't show how much worse those orders were for the friction Olsen found.

Opinion: Haun has the better of it on what moved Saddam, and Olsen has the better of it on why the larger aim failed. First order, the bombs that hurt Saddam were the ones falling on his army, and the inner ring drew 2 percent of the effort. Second order, the Republican Guard, the force that linked the regime to the people, escaped largely intact because the bombing shifted to frontline conscripts, and the Guard put down the uprising Bush had encouraged. Third order, the Air Force kept the Warden idea anyway: Michael Ryan called going after the enemy with massive force from the opening moment "classic air power doctrine" after Kosovo, and Michael Short said he would have gone for "the head of the snake" on the first night. The seminar comment: "Olsen's own Primakov evidence says Saddam moved after seeing his army bombed, not his ministries, so the strategic campaign's real product was friction, and Haun's attrition numbers did the coercing." Olsen says airmen are criticized for their promises more than credited for their achievements, which is true, and also what happens when the promises are larger. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Olsen · extrapolation

Saddam Got His Battle Damage Assessment From a Russian

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran, and his working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. What follows is our extrapolation from Olsen's conclusions, and not Shaun's answer.

Olsen supports the answer's central premise, which is that understanding comes before striking. His prescription for better strategic bombing is to study potential enemies, their culture, and how their power systems work, and he lists the things air power lacked in 1991, among them inside information and timely human intelligence. A persistent sensor network over the strait is a way to learn an adversary's habits over months: which units move, when, and in answer to what. Olsen's oddest detail supports a second use. Saddam's council told him what he wanted to hear, and he did not know how bad the bombing was until Primakov showed him pictures in mid-February 1991. Imagery that can be shown to a decision-maker is a coercive tool. (A sensor is cheaper than a Russian envoy and more reliable about showing up.)

Olsen cuts against the answer in two places. He applies Peter Faber's three pathologies of air theory to Warden, and the second, a "fetish of quantification and prediction," is the occupational hazard of any sensor-centered plan: counting boats is easier than understanding Tehran. He also faults Washington for never planning war termination. A "sustainable presence" with no stated end state repeats that error by design. If the paper can't say what the orbit is meant to make Iran stop doing, a reader will ask why the orbit exists.

Opinion, extrapolated: Olsen would back the sensors if the paper aims them at how Iran's leaders decide, names the behavior the presence should change, and says who gets shown the pictures. Otherwise the drones will produce excellent footage of a problem nobody has defined. (S)

The Readings So Far · Olsen, Kitfield, Haun, and Clodfelter

Two Percent, Twice

The same small number shows up in two books about the same war, and it means opposite things. In Kitfield, the F-117s flew only 2 percent of the combat sorties in Desert Storm and attacked more than 40 percent of the strategic and most heavily defended targets, with no F-117s lost. In Olsen, the attacks on Warden's inner ring took 2 percent of the overall air effort, and only 740 of 43,123 strikes hit what he calls the central nervous system. Kitfield's 2 percent is a triumph of efficiency. Olsen's is a question about whether the strategy got a fair trial. (Statistics, like airmen, perform differently depending on who is briefing them.)

Haun supplies the third reading of that number. In his appendix, most strikes fell on the Iraqi army in the Kuwaiti theater, the heaviest on the regular infantry along the Saudi border, and that is where he finds the decisive effect. Put the three together and you get the picture Olsen's own Table 2 hints at: the plan grew from 84 targets to 481, and the growth was mostly military.

The news has been pinning itself here all week. Wednesday's CSIS munitions report met Olsen's Tomahawks, which were spent mostly in the first two days, and the lesson was that the opening salvo gets the attention while the reload decides the war. Yesterday's counterpoint left us with Clodfelter's rule that bombing coerces when the demand is small. Olsen extends it: the demand in 1991 was small on paper (leave Kuwait), large in the planners' hopes (a new regime), and the bombing achieved roughly the paper version. Opinion: the hidden demand is the one that gets graded in the history books. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 3, Day 4 · the thesis and the argument

Olsen Opens With a Fight, Which Is Why You Remember His Thesis

Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein, in They Say / I Say, argue that a thesis needs the conversation it answers. Their example is a conference speaker who spent a whole talk proving a sociologist's work was important, and only in the question period revealed that critics had attacked it. Until then, nobody knew why he was talking. Their rule is to state your position and the one it answers together, as a unit, early: within a paragraph or two in a short essay. Orwell does it in the first lines of "Politics and the English Language," which they reduce to "Most people assume we can't fix the language. But I say we can."

Olsen follows the rule in his conclusions. He opens with Robert Pape, who argues that strategic air power failed in Iraq because Saddam survived and was not coerced, and answers in the next breath that Saddam's survival doesn't mean strategic paralysis was ineffectual. You remember his claim because you remember whom he's arguing with. Before: "This paper examines the strategic air campaign in Desert Storm and its effects on the Iraqi regime." After: "Pape argues the 1991 leadership campaign failed because Saddam survived; Olsen's Iraqi interviews show it partly paralyzed the regime, and its failure to change the regime traces to an aim Washington never declared." The first sentence announces a topic. The second picks a fight and says who wins.

Opinion: in a SAASS paper, the "they say" also tells the grader which books you read. A thesis with no opponent reads as if you didn't find one, and on this shelf there is always one.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: before drafting, fill in one sentence, "Some argue ___; I argue ___ because ___." If the first blank stays empty, you have a topic and no thesis yet. If the third stays empty, you have an opinion. (S)

Acquisition × Olsen · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, November 10

The Air Force Has a New System. So Did Iraq.

The Air Force said Tuesday it has finished Force Design 2026, a plan for how it will fight a capable adversary, and nobody outside will get to read it. A spokesperson told Air & Space Forces Magazine the plan is classified and has "no publicly releasable unclassified version," unlike the 2024 design under General Allvin, which came with an unclassified overview. The service says the plan focuses on "technology, autonomy and integration," cites advances in sensing, networking, artificial intelligence, unmanned systems, long-range weapons, and electronic warfare, and urges Airmen to think less about individual platforms and more about the systems connecting them. General Wilsbach called it "a fundamental shift." The story notes the service's plans for hundreds of autonomous drones. It reports no budget figures and no outside critics.

Olsen has seen a confident Air Force vision before. In 1990, Global Reach, Global Power argued that "a new Air Force" was in the making and would be the dominant service, and Warden built Instant Thunder on it and on his Five Rings, a systems model of the enemy. Olsen credits the Checkmate planners with "great insight" about aircraft and precision weapons. Their problem lay elsewhere: they knew little about the regime beyond buildings and communications. He also describes Warden's "rather mechanistic view of war," the belief that American air power could largely control a war through technological supremacy.

Opinion: the new design asks Airmen to see their own force as a system, and Olsen's closing lesson is that the enemy is one too, and that strategic paralysis "is not a surgical exercise with identifiable cause-effect links." A list of sensing, networking, and autonomy describes what the Air Force will see with. Does it say what the Air Force needs to understand? We can't tell, which is the point of classification and also the trouble with it. Hundreds of drones will find buildings faster than the F-117s did. In 1991, finding the buildings was the easy part.

The Air Force has written a plan nobody outside can read. In 1991, the problem was a regime nobody inside could read. (S)

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

    546 Navy and 730 Fifth Air Force sorties destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential in four days (119).

  • Zhang: what did Moscow order Belov's 151st Air Division in 1950?

    To retrain Chinese pilots first and not to fly beyond the Yalu (62).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how did January 1973's agreement differ from October's?

    "Largely cosmetic" differences (ch. VI, e217).

  • Randolph: how does he score the Easter Offensive for Hanoi?

    "A strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome" (339).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

  • Haun: what do Instant Thunder and the Desert Storm interdiction campaign have in common?

    They are the only modern air operations where most factors favored air power, "yet both failed" (273).

  • Kitfield: what share of Desert Storm sorties did the F-117s fly, and what share of strategic targets did they attack?

    About 2 percent of sorties and more than 40 percent of the strategic and most heavily defended targets (386).

  • Olsen: what is his two-part verdict on the strategic air campaign?

    It degraded war-making and induced fog and friction, but did not meet the "hoped for, but unstated" objective of changing the regime (1).

Writing drills

  • Give this thesis an opponent: "Goldwater-Nichols shaped the Gulf War air campaign."

    "Kitfield's David Jones concluded that reform would have to be forced on the Pentagon from outside (251); Olsen shows the reform that came, Goldwater-Nichols, left the Air Staff outside the planning chain, so Checkmate's plan reached the war only because Schwarzkopf asked for it (87–88, 107)." Position and opponent arrive together (Graff and Birkenstein, 21).

  • Find the missing "because": "Some argue the al-Firdos halt hurt the campaign; I argue it did not."

    "…I argue it did not change the outcome, because Olsen's Iraqi interviewees said 'more of the same' against the inner ring would not have made much difference (292)." Without the because, it's an opinion.

  • Review (Wednesday, precise language): cut the fog: "Leadership targeting generated suboptimal regime-level outcomes."

    "Forty-three days of bombing several hundred targets did not collapse Saddam's command and control; his Guard withdrew in order and put down two revolts" (Olsen, 288). Things and actions replace "outcomes" (Pollock, 41).

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

Write it yourself. The "Your turn" box matters most. Producing the paragraph teaches more than reading mine.

Built for comps. Class is book and notebook only. This page is the testing ground: every book read stays here as recall questions, cumulative through the year.

Weekends: reader mail. News sources: Electoral-vote.com, Punchbowl News, Military Times, War on the Rocks, Defense News, Breaking Defense, Air & Space Forces Magazine, SpaceNews, CSIS, and RAND.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
