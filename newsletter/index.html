Seminar 10 · Kitfield, today at 1200

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered: Five Questions, One Hundred Hours

  1. Was the preparation for and the actual use of force effective? Yes in battle, less clearly at the finish. F-117s flew about 2 percent of the combat sorties, attacked more than 40 percent of the strategic targets, and lost no aircraft, and the two Marine divisions lost one man killed on the first day of a ground war that experts had expected to cost twenty thousand or more American casualties (chapters 42, 45, and 46). The finish is weaker: by February 27 the "Highway of Death" footage produced a clamor for a cease-fire, and Kitfield admits there was little reliable evidence that any mobile Scud launchers were destroyed (chapter 46).
  2. Was the military buildup more effective at deterring a great power than intervening in a volatile region? By Kitfield's account, yes. He concludes that the revitalized forces of the 1980s deterred Soviet military ambitions, though he also lists Afghanistan, Chernobyl, and the Strategic Defense Initiative among Moscow's other worries (chapter 34). The same buildup could not protect the Marines in Beirut, where 241 were dead or dying on October 23, 1983, after rules of engagement generally barred keeping magazines loaded and the gate guards obeyed and orders ran through five headquarters to the Pentagon (chapter 27).
  3. In what ways did the military change in this interwar period? It recruited better, trained harder, maintained better, and fought jointly. Recruits with a high school diploma rose from 62 to 85 percent by 1983 (chapter 32). Red Flag gave pilots their first ten combat missions in Nevada, and the National Training Center mauled nine of ten visiting Army units (chapters 16 and 33). Under Creech, TAC aircraft went from eleven sorties a month in 1978 to twenty-one, and the crash rate fell from one per 13,000 flying hours to one per 50,000 (chapter 25).
  4. What changes were driven internally and what were forced upon the military? The services fixed how each one fought; Congress fixed how they fought together. Top Gun in 1968, Red Flag, Creech's TAC, and Abrams's move of as much as 70 percent of the Army's combat service support into the reserves were internal (chapters 13, 15, 16, and 17). The all-volunteer force came from the president, and Goldwater-Nichols and Special Operations Command came from Congress over strong service opposition. David Jones, after years as a joint chief, concluded that change had to be forced on the Pentagon from outside (chapters 12, 26, 31, and 35).
  5. What regional or international problems did the United States encounter that would define the post Cold War strategic environment? The Persian Gulf, terrorism, and peacekeeping. The Iran hostage crisis and Desert One in 1980 exposed services that could not work together, the Carter Doctrine produced the Rapid Deployment Joint Task Force and then Central Command, Beirut showed the price of presence without a clear mission, and the Earnest Will tanker escorts put the Navy in the Gulf in 1987 and 1988 (chapters 23, 24, 27, and 38). By the epilogue the force is shrinking by at least a third, with Somalia and Bosnia ahead.

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. Pages are in Sources. Opinion: questions two and five answer each other. The buildup was built for the war that never came, and the wars that did come were the kind it handled worst. (S)

The Reading · Kitfield, Prodigal Soldiers

Twenty Years of Homework, Graded in a Hundred Hours

James Kitfield, a defense reporter, argues that the officers who came home from Vietnam in disgrace stayed in uniform, rebuilt their services over two decades, and won the Gulf War. He tells it through a few careers, chiefly Barry McCaffrey, Chuck Horner, Colin Powell, Jack Galvin, and Walt Boomer. In the prologue McCaffrey hears Schwarzkopf brief the left hook in November 1990 and thinks, "By God, we learned." (The book is four hundred pages of explaining what, exactly, they learned.)

McCaffrey's nightmare in the prologue, gradual escalation with no attacks inside Iraq and protests growing at home, is Rolling Thunder as Clodfelter described it, and the "slowly ascending tempo" Haun quoted from 1965. Kitfield's officers spent twenty years making sure the next war would not be run on that schedule. Horner, a Red Flag flight lead in 1978, ran the 1991 air war; McCaffrey, wounded as a company commander in 1969, led the 24th Infantry Division three hundred kilometers into Iraq.

Opinion: Kitfield is a reporter, and his evidence is mostly what his subjects told him. That makes the book a strong record of what the officers believed they had fixed and a weaker record of what the Iraqis did. A redemption story is easier to tell when the opposing team rarely gets interviewed. (S)

Counterpoint · Haun against Kitfield

Counterpoint: Haun Audits the Victory Parade

Kitfield's central claim is that the reforms of the Vietnam generation produced the victory in Desert Storm. Phil Haun, whom we read yesterday, agrees that the force of 1991 was superb and disagrees about which parts of it won. His Appendix B scores the war's three air campaigns separately, using the Gulf War Air Power Survey of 1993, and two of the three fail.

The strategic campaign that Kitfield's chapter 42 celebrates failed first. Instant Thunder's night raids eliminated the highest-priority targets around Baghdad, and Saddam Hussein stayed in power and in command. Haun gives four proofs: Saddam drove to Basra two weeks into the air war to meet his generals and ordered the attack on Al Khafji, he approved Scud targets daily, he approved the order to withdraw from Kuwait on February 26, and after the war he used the surviving Republican Guard to crush the Shia and Kurdish uprisings. Interdiction did no better. Bombing cut Iraqi transport capacity from 200,000 tons a day to 20,000, but an army sitting in static defenses needed only 10,000 to 15,000. Haun's bluntest sentence is that Instant Thunder and the interdiction campaign are the only modern air operations where most factors favored air power, "yet both failed."

What worked, in Haun's account, was the unglamorous middle: armed reconnaissance against the Iraqi army in Kuwait. By the ground invasion 25 to 30 percent of that army had been attrited, about 10 percent of its armor and artillery by airstrikes and most of the rest by desertion, and the air threat kept the remainder dispersed and dug in until coalition armor rolled over it. Haun adds two points Kitfield doesn't stress. The end of the Cold War "provided a surplus of forces," and the Air Force put no battlefield air interdiction missions on its tasking orders, which spared it from coordinating targets with ground commanders.

Kitfield's evidence can show competence. Red Flag graduates, stealth statistics, a TAC mission-capable rate approaching 85 percent, and one Marine killed on the first day are real and page-sourced. It can't show cause, because his sources are the Americans who ran the war, and the officers who built Instant Thunder are not the people to ask whether it worked. To his credit he reports the gaps: intelligence and damage assessment struggled, and there was little reliable evidence that any mobile Scud launcher died. Kitfield even writes that for Horner the point "was never about whether airpower could win a war on its own," which is Haun's thesis in a pilot's voice.

Opinion: Haun has the better of it on what won, and Kitfield on why the force could win at all. First order, crediting Instant Thunder teaches planners that decapitation works, though Saddam kept command of his army for the whole war. Second order, the Air Force, by Haun's account, dropped battlefield air interdiction from its doctrine again after Desert Storm, the second time it had deleted the mission that kept an enemy army pinned. Third order, by our inference, the service went into the 1990s funding the campaign that failed and forgetting the one that worked. The seminar comment: "Kitfield proves the 1991 force was competent, but Haun's appendix shows the strategic bombing and interdiction campaigns failed and direct attack on a static Iraqi army did the work, so the reform that mattered most was the ground force that made the Iraqis hold still." The parade was earned. By Haun's count, two of the three air campaigns marching in it were not. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Kitfield

In the Strait, Kitfield Would Ask Who Fixes the Airplanes

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. The crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran, and the working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. This is our extrapolation from Kitfield, not Shaun's answer.

Kitfield supports the answer in three places. The Gulf War's quiet star was a sensor: Joint STARS, rushed to the theater at Schwarzkopf's request, could track almost anything moving on the ground within 155 miles, and on Highway Six it cued Strike Eagles onto a fleeing convoy within ninety minutes. Sustainability, in his book, is a maintenance statistic. Creech nearly doubled TAC's monthly sorties per aircraft while Air Force spare parts were set to rise about 60 percent, which is what a permanent orbit over a strait would require. And the Vincennes, which misidentified an Iranian airliner and killed hundreds of civilians, shows that identifying the target is half the mission in crowded Gulf waters. (This week's news adds that unmanned boats have already rescued downed pilots and struck an Iranian submarine.)

He cuts against it in three others. Gulf presence has been a Navy job: Earnest Will in 1987 and 1988 was largely a Navy operation, and in 1990 Navy leaders assumed their service would supply the continuing presence afterward. Beirut shows what an open-ended presence mission with unclear rules can cost, 241 Marines, and Weinberger's tests ask for clear objectives and public support before force is used. And the withdrawal from Vietnam left only four of ten active Army divisions deployable by 1979, which suggests the years after this war are when readiness will decay.

Opinion, extrapolated: Kitfield would back the sensors and ask for three things the paper should answer, a Red Flag for drone and sensor crews, a rules-of-engagement annex written before the first orbit, and a maintenance budget that outlasts the news cycle. A presence mission ends when the money does. (S)

The Readings So Far · Gaddis through Kitfield

Seventy Years of Cutting Supply Lines to Just Above What the Enemy Needs

Four books on the shelf report the same interdiction result: the bombing cut supplies sharply and never below what the enemy needed. Crane's FEAF study of April 1952 found rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts. Clodfelter's Linebacker I cut North Vietnam's overland imports from 160,000 tons a month to 30,000. Haun's CIA analysts noted the enemy in the South needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day. In Iraq, bombing cut transport from 200,000 tons a day to 20,000 for an army that needed 10,000 to 15,000. (Four wars, one footnote: the enemy kept eating.)

The news keeps landing on the same shelf. Yesterday's item on cheap cruise missiles dropped from C-17s met Haun's Assault Breaker, and the question was who finds the targets. Kitfield answers with Joint STARS on Highway Six, the way McDougall's reconnaissance satellites cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen in 1961. Last week's war-funding stories met Randolph's Senate caucus voting 35 to 8 to cut off funds in 1972 and Haun's Cooper-Church amendment. Kitfield adds Abrams, who moved as much as 70 percent of the Army's support into the reserves so that a war would need a call-up, and with it the public.

Gaddis explains why Kitfield's generation hated gradualism. Flexible response, he showed, rested on "calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions, and Kitfield's officers spent twenty years making sure nobody calibrated them again. Opinion: Gaddis described a doctrine and Kitfield describes a generation's allergic reaction to it. Thursday's book, Olsen's Strategic Air Power in Desert Storm, takes up the very campaign Haun just failed, so bring his appendix to class. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 3, Day 2 · using evidence and evaluating sources

Kitfield's Two Percent Passes the Sneaker Test

Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup, and FitzGerald, in The Craft of Research, say readers judge evidence on five tests: accurate, precise, sufficient, representative, and authoritative. They demonstrate with a child asking for new sneakers, whose parent rejects "these are too small" as imprecise, "look at how I limp" as unrepresentative, and "Harry said so" as unauthoritative. Their warning words are some, most, many, often, and usually, which can limit a claim honestly or fudge it when the writer didn't find the number. They also warn that one or two examples, however vivid, invite the charge of cherry-picking.

Kitfield's best evidence passes. Before: "Stealth did most of the heavy lifting in the air war." After: "F-117s flew about 2 percent of the combat sorties and attacked more than 40 percent of the strategic targets without a loss." The second sentence is accurate, precise, and checkable. It fails only the representative test if you use it to prove the whole air war worked, since Haun shows the strategic targets were the part that failed.

The authority test cuts hardest in today's book. Kitfield's witness for whether the reforms worked is usually the reformer, which is "Harry said so" with four stars. Opinion: quote him for what happened in the room and go to the Gulf War Air Power Survey, through Haun, for what happened to Iraq.

Pro tip: for every number in your draft, write "out of how many?" in the margin. If you can't answer, the number is decoration. (S)

News × the Books

Leadership × Kitfield · via War on the Rocks, November 10

The Pentagon Wants a Top Gun for Contracting Officers. Kitfield Says Bring a Promotion Board.

Two former Defense Innovation Unit insiders want the unit to become the Pentagon's weapons school for buying commercial technology. Writing in War on the Rocks today, Ben McNally, a former DIU analyst, and Donald "Chee" Gansberger, a retired Air Force tactical air control party specialist who was DIU's first warfighter-in-residence, propose selecting "military mavericks," running them through a course, warranting them to award contracts, and sending them back to the services. Graduates would get a special experience identifier and a spot on the Joint Duty Assignment List, "giving Joint Staff credit." They argue the current system "incentivizes fear and compliance," and one author's record is their exhibit: the DIU work, they write, meant "permanently derailing the author's military career." The unit's wins include unmanned boats later used to rescue downed pilots and strike an Iranian submarine.

Kitfield has both halves of this plan, decades apart. The Navy's Top Gun opened in 1968 with selected pilots, expert instructors flying enemy-like aircraft, and blunt after-action reviews, and its graduates reached the fleet in 1969 better at killing and at surviving. A "maverick band" of Air Force fighter pilots pushed Red Flag for the same reason. The services built that half themselves, and Congress had to force the other. David Jones wanted joint duty to be "mandatory for promotion rather than a career dead end," concluded the change would have to be forced from outside, and Goldwater-Nichols made a joint assignment a requirement for a general's star in 1986, over service opposition.

Opinion: the authors have read the right history, perhaps without meaning to. Training creates mavericks, and only the promotion system keeps them. Jack Galvin, Kitfield notes, suspected his own "maverick ways" had earned him an invisible black mark at Army personnel. The Joint Duty Assignment List is the part of this proposal with teeth, since a course without career credit is a sabbatical. The Pentagon already knows how to build a weapons school. The harder part, on Kitfield's evidence, is promoting the people who graduate from one. (S)

Writing drills

  • Make it precise: "Many Marines were killed in Beirut because of a lot of problems with the rules."

    "On October 23, 1983, a truck bomb killed or mortally wounded 241 Marines at a compound where the rules of engagement generally barred keeping magazines loaded" (Kitfield, 260–261). Numbers and a date replace "many" and "a lot" (Booth et al., 139).

  • Fix the authority problem: "The reforms clearly worked, as the generals who led them later said."

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
