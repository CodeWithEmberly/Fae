Seminar 9 · Haun, today at 1200

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered: Four Questions, Twenty-Three Campaigns

  1. What was airpower most effective at doing in the limited war of the Cold War? Attacking enemy armies alongside a friendly army that could fight, mostly by scaring them out of massing. Direct attack met its military aims in nine of eleven campaigns, and in seven of those nine its main contribution was making the enemy disperse (chapters 1 and 8). When the North Vietnamese massed anyway, combined arms destroyed two divisions at Khe Sanh and defeated fourteen in the Easter Offensive (chapters 2, 4, and 7).
  2. Where did it fall short on its promises? Wherever it was asked to win alone. Strategic bombing met its political goal in two of six campaigns, and interdiction met its military goal in one of six (chapter 8). Rolling Thunder neither isolated the South nor coerced Hanoi (chapter 3), and at Quang Tri in 1972 low cloud, SA-2s, and a freshly handed-over air control system kept it from stopping the NVA (chapter 7).
  3. Were the gradual escalation and interdiction campaigns effective at all, and how? They bought delay and raised Hanoi's costs without changing its decisions. Hanoi put 20 percent of its non-agricultural workforce on dispersal and repair and still tripled its supply flow by the end of 1965 (chapter 3). Over the Trail, DIA claimed an 82 percent cut while the CIA noted the South needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day; Haun rules both correct, since Commando Hunt capped NVA operations without stopping them (chapter 5).
  4. What lessons from Vietnam can strategists apply to other and future air campaigns? Bring a competent ground partner: direct attack with one succeeded militarily seven times in seven, and without one twice in four (chapter 8). Put suppressing SAMs ahead of dogfighting, fund ISR and controllers, and run his nine-factor check before the war. For Taiwan he prefers denial against Chinese forces and sea lanes over punishing the homeland (chapter 9).

Pages are in Sources. When someone at the table says Linebacker II proves bombing works, chapter 8 has the reply: Hanoi signed terms identical to the ones it had already agreed after its army lost the Easter Offensive. (S)

The Reading · Haun, Tactical Air Power and the Vietnam War

Haun Grades the Whole War and Gives the Bombers an Incomplete

Phil Haun, a SAASS graduate who flew A-10s over Kosovo, wrote the book this course has been circling for a week. Clodfelter graded the bombing of the North; Randolph followed one spring offensive; Nguyen sat in Hanoi's Politburo. Haun scores thirteen Vietnam air campaigns and ten later ones on a single yardstick, whether they changed the enemy army's decisions, and finds the winners were the unglamorous ones flown with soldiers nearby.

The shelf agrees with him more than airmen might like. Crane's FEAF study of April 1952 found rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, and Haun's interdiction column reads one success in six. Nguyen, meanwhile, explains the thing Haun's theory admits it did not predict, which is why the NVA kept massing into the bombs. Haun says Hanoi sacrificed armies for broader political goals; Nguyen shows a first secretary who bet three times, in 1964, 1968, and 1972, on a general uprising that never came. Opinion: a theory that needs the enemy's Politburo to explain its exceptions is a good theory with honest footnotes. Le Duan made American air power look brilliant, and nobody in Hanoi ever sent a thank-you note. (S)

Tonight's Reading · Kitfield, for seminar Tuesday, November 10

Tonight's Reading: Kitfield's Majors Swore "Never Again," Then Rewrote the Manuals

James Kitfield, a defense journalist, tells the story of the officers who came home from Vietnam in disgrace and spent twenty years rebuilding the military that won in the Gulf. His subtitle states the thesis plainly: a generation born of Vietnam revolutionized the American style of war. The prologue sets the hook. Schwarzkopf unveils a "left hook" by two Army corps, classic AirLand Battle on a grand scale, and Barry McCaffrey thinks, "We're not going to fight a war of attrition, or a limited war."

The syllabus warns that this arc is alluring and incomplete, since it skips a collapsing Soviet Union and a coalition assembled for a limited objective. (A course on the Age of Limited War assigning a hero who swears off limited war is either irony or a trap.) Watch which changes the services drove themselves and which were forced on them.

Read for:

  1. Prologue, pages 17 to 25: the Schwarzkopf briefing and McCaffrey's "By God, we learned."
  2. Chapters 11 to 15, pages 125 to 164: the reckoning of 1973, from Days of Rage through the all-volunteer force and Bloodless Battles.
  3. Chapters 16 and 17, pages 165 to 181: Red Flag, the Aggressors, and Bill Creech.
  4. Chapters 20 and 23, pages 197 to 208 and 223 to 234: the hollow force and Desert One.
  5. Chapters 30 and 31, pages 277 to 298: the reformers and the fight over Goldwater-Nichols.
  6. Chapter 42 and the epilogue, pages 380 to 391 and 423 to 435: Horner's air war, and McCaffrey's warning that the military rights itself only after debacles from Kasserine Pass to Desert One.

Short on time? The prologue, chapter 31, chapter 42, and the epilogue. The book runs over four hundred pages, so tonight you, too, will be fighting a war of attrition. (S)

The 628 Paper · through today's author

If Russia Crosses a NATO Border, Haun Would Send the Air Force After the Tanks

The prompt asks for the single most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1 at 1630. Today we run the syllabus's third example, a Russian invasion of a NATO member, through Haun. This is our extrapolation from his epilogue, not Shaun's answer.

Haun's first move is to take punishment off the table. Threats to strike Russian nuclear forces, cities, or leaders are less credible because of the escalation risk, and he puts the question bluntly: "Would the United States risk Washington over Warsaw or Los Angeles over Taipei?" He prefers denial aimed at Russian fielded forces, the logic of AirLand Battle and NATO's Follow-on Forces Attack in the early 1980s. His Kyiv check shows what that requires, since Russia lost the fight for air superiority to mobile SAMs it could not suppress. And he insists that no American strategy substitutes for an ally willing and able to defend its own ground, so guarantees should depend on partners keeping credible forces that can talk to American airpower, the way the ARVN did in the Easter Offensive.

Opinion, extrapolated: Airmen should prepare with SEAD first, controllers embedded with allied armies, and long-range strike that announces its targets clearly, since Haun warns that Assault Breaker-style weapons could look like a threat to Russian command and nuclear forces. In Haun's version of deterrence, the most important thing an ally contributes is a tank that's worth bombing. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 3, Day 1 · active voice and agency

Battlefield Interdiction Was Removed, by Persons Unknown

Helen Sword's Stylish Academic Writing names two habits that drain the life from academic prose. In the "research as agent" sentence, the article or the study does the arguing instead of the scholar; in the "agentless" sentence, things simply happen, and nobody did them. She finds both so common they often cohabit in one paragraph, and her cure is to let writers speak as themselves. Her checklist: hunt down your passives (a form of "be" plus a past participle), keep a few for variety, and give each paragraph at least one sentence with a person as subject and an active verb. Too many passives, she warns, add up to "lifeless, agentless prose."

Haun supplies both versions of the same fact, two hundred pages apart. Late in the book he writes that in 1966 battlefield interdiction "was removed from USAF doctrine, and again after Desert Storm BAI disappeared." Early in the book he names the culprits: Air Force leaders struck BAI from doctrine. Before: "Battlefield interdiction was removed from doctrine in 1966." After: "The Air Force deleted battlefield interdiction from its doctrine in 1966, two years after adding it, during major combined arms operations in South Vietnam."

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: add "by an idiot" after the verb. If the sentence still makes sense ("was removed by an idiot"), it's passive, and you should decide whether the idiot deserves a name. In doctrine, missions rarely wander off on their own. (S)

Representative Thomas Massie is expected to force a vote by Wednesday on impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

Haun has seen this calendar. After the Cambodian incursion in 1970, congressional action threatened to cut war funding, and in his words "the clock was ticking for South Vietnam to defend itself." That November, Democrats widened their antiwar caucus in the House, and at year's end Congress voted Cooper-Church into law, forbidding American ground troops beyond South Vietnam's borders. Haun traces the result to the air war: the pressure helped push Saigon into Lam Son 719, where the ARVN went into Laos without the American advisors and air controllers who could have aimed the bombs.

Opinion, nonpartisan: whichever side wins the funding fight, planners should read the appropriations bill like an annex to the rules of engagement, because Congress's terms become operational constraints. A law about soldiers in 1970 decided where bombs could land in 1971. In 1970 the clock hung in Saigon. This year it hangs in the Capitol, and it reads December 11. (S)

Acquisition × Haun · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, November 8

The Loadmaster Joins the Strike Package

The Air Force is about to turn its cargo planes into missile trucks. Speaking November 6 at the Airlift/Tanker Association symposium, Chief of Staff Gen. Kenneth Wilsbach said he was excited about arming the mobility fleet with FAMM-P, the palletized version of the Family of Affordable Mass Missiles: a container of cruise missiles rolls out of a C-17 or C-130 on a standard airdrop platform, the parachute opens, and the missiles light their engines and head for their targets. The idea grew out of AFRL's Rapid Dragon experiments in 2019 and appears in the 2027 budget request as $355 million for 1,000 weapons, with 28,000 missiles projected over five years at $12.6 billion. Wilsbach says each costs about a tenth of a JASSM and arrives in "months, not years." A CSIS report cited in the story says about 1,100 of an estimated 4,400 JASSMs went into Operation Epic Fury.

Haun's epilogue reads like the requirements memo. He warns that Chinese sensors can now target regional airfields and even carriers, so the old template of fighters winning air superiority and then hammering fielded forces may not deter an assault on Taiwan. His alternative is multi-domain ISR networked to long-range strikes against mobile targets and Chinese sea lanes, an Assault Breaker reimagined. DARPA's original Assault Breaker of the late 1970s never became operational, largely because the Air Force disapproved of a design with no role for its strike aircraft.

Opinion: forty-odd years later the service has found a long-range strike role it likes, and the strike aircraft is a C-17. Cheap mass answers the magazine problem those JASSM numbers expose (by our arithmetic, the 2027 request works out to about $355,000 a round). Haun would ask who finds the targets, since a pallet of missiles without sensors is just very expensive cargo. Wilsbach calls it "a cultural shift." For a service that once vetoed a strike weapon for leaving out the pilots, it certainly is. (S)

Today's seminar: Haun, Tactical Air Power and the Vietnam War, chapter by chapter

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 1

Haun Sorts Air Campaigns by Theory of Victory, Then Reads the Box Score

Before arguing about whether air power worked, ask which theory of victory the campaign was betting on, then check that theory's record. Haun sees three kinds. Strategic bombing coerces a nation through its population, economy, or leadership; interdiction goes after supply lines; direct attack hits the fielded army, usually alongside friendly troops. His thesis is that the third, the one air force leaders long called a waste of air power, is the one that works.

The box score covers twenty-three modern American campaigns since 1965, thirteen of them in Vietnam, the longest modern air war at almost eight years. Direct attack went nine for eleven on military objectives, strategic bombing two for six, interdiction one for six. Haun flags the limit too. Over Kosovo in 1999 there was no credible ground threat, so Serb soldiers dispersed, swapped military vehicles for civilian cars, and kept up the ethnic cleansing while "NATO tacair circled helplessly overhead."

The weak seminar comment is "Air power failed in Vietnam." The strong one asks which air power: Rolling Thunder failed to isolate the South or coerce Hanoi, while combined arms broke the North Vietnamese Army at Khe Sanh and again in the Easter Offensive. Opinion: the best line in the chapter is that Air Force leaders supported combined arms reluctantly, and only when ordered to. The winning strategy had to be assigned, like homework. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 2

Haun Scores Air Power by the Battles That Never Happen

Judge air power against an army by what the army stops doing. Once air superiority is won, aircraft can spot ground forces that mass or move, which puts the enemy on the horns of a dilemma: concentrate and get hammered from the air, or disperse and hide, which gives up the initiative and leaves him open to a ground attack. Most commanders choose to hide, so the payoff rarely shows up in a body count.

History supplies both horns. At Khe Sanh and in the Easter Offensive the North Vietnamese concentrated, and American combined arms destroyed two divisions in the first and defeated fourteen in the second. Iraq in 2003 took the other horn, dispersing in expectation of an air-only campaign, and was left exposed to the ground invasion. Doctrine never explained why; the July 1943 field manual declared land and air power coequal in capital letters, then ranked close air support last.

The weak seminar comment is "We destroyed hundreds of tanks, so the air campaign worked." The strong one asks whether the enemy massed, and if he dispersed, whether a friendly ground force cashed in. Haun's line: "The dead make no decisions and take no actions on the battlefield." Opinion: the theory's best evidence is the dog that doesn't bark, which is also its weak spot, since an absence is hard to photograph. Why did Hanoi twice pick the horn that gets bombed? Haun says it sacrificed armies for political goals, which is one way to make an air force look brilliant. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 3

Rolling Thunder Ascended Slowly, and Hanoi Adapted Quickly

Bombing coerces only when something else threatens what the enemy values most; if nothing forces him to mass, he scatters, repairs, and waits. Rolling Thunder, billed as strategic bombing by gradual escalation, was mostly interdiction, and it neither cut the Ho Chi Minh Trail nor bent Hanoi. Johnson ruled out invading the North for fear of repeating MacArthur's march to the Yalu, so the NVA never had to concentrate to defend its border.

In April 1965 National Security Action Memo 328 ordered planners to keep the "slowly ascending tempo." They did. Attack sorties rose from 55,000 in 1965 to 148,000 in 1966, and bomb tonnage from 33,000 to 128,000. Hanoi put 20 percent of its non-agricultural workforce on dispersal and repair, and by the end of 1965 it had tripled its flow of supplies. The June 1966 strikes on fuel wrecked 70 percent of storage capacity, which mattered little because Hanoi had already scattered its fuel. Targets went to Tuesday luncheons at the White House.

The weak seminar comment is "Rolling Thunder failed because of political micromanagement." The strong one adds Haun's operational cause, which no rule change would fix: light infantry and guerrillas needed so few supplies that air power alone could not starve them. Opinion: before any interdiction plan, ask how much the enemy actually eats. Losses over the North climbed from 171 aircraft in 1965 to 318 in 1966. Washington chose targets over lunch, and Hanoi spent the rest of the week fixing bridges. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 4

Khe Sanh Shows What Air Power Does to an Army That Holds Still

Air power is deadliest when the enemy concentrates, and a friendly force that holds its ground is what keeps him concentrated long enough to pay for it. TAP theory expected the NVA to disperse. At Khe Sanh in early 1968 it massed two divisions instead, and combined arms decisively defeated it. Haun admits the theory missed the massing but says it explains the slaughter, since air power proves lethal when "a cooperative adversary continues to concentrate and maneuver."

The siege opened January 21 and ran seventy-seven days, 20,000 NVA against 6,000 Marines and ARVN Rangers. On day one an NVA round destroyed 98 percent of the Marines' munitions, which is one way to test an airlift. Tacair flew 22,000 sorties, and B-52s arriving in threes about every ninety minutes dropped 60 percent of the tonnage. The NVA lost 10,000 to 15,000 dead or wounded.

The weak seminar comment is "Khe Sanh proves air power wins battles." The strong one: it worked because the Marines held, airlift fed them, the NVA chose to stay, and clearing weather in March proved most decisive. Run the same check on Tet, where the Viet Cong slipped into the cities dispersed, reconnaissance could not track them, and South Vietnamese police and militia won the fights. Opinion: before promising decisive air effects, ask whether the enemy has agreed to cooperate. At Khe Sanh he did. In the cities he declined, and on March 12 Eugene McCarthy took 40 percent in New Hampshire. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 5

Igloo White Counted Trucks. Hanoi Sent the Infantry on Foot.

Before grading an interdiction campaign, ask what the sensors counted and how much the enemy actually needed. Commando Hunt I and III, the dry-season campaigns against the Trail in southern Laos from late 1968 to spring 1970, squeezed the supply line, barely touched the soldiers walking down it, and left the back door at Sihanoukville open until allied ground troops went into Cambodia and shut it.

Igloo White eventually air-dropped 20,000 battery-powered sensors along the Trail. Their signals went to Nakhon Phanom, Thailand, where two IBM 360-65 mainframes, the fastest in the world at the time, helped 400 analysts predict convoy movements around the clock. In 1969 DIA and 7th Air Force said Commando Hunt I had cut supplies reaching the South by 82 percent. The CIA replied that the forces there needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day and were getting them. Haun, refereeing: "Both DIA and CIA assessments were correct."

The weak seminar comment is "Commando Hunt cut supplies by 82 percent." The strong one: it capped NVA operations and raised the bill, because the South needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day. Gunships got credit for 28 percent of all truck kills, yet the NVA chose to drive at night, which Haun reads as fear of the daytime FACs and fighters. Opinion: the tally rewarded the airplane that killed trucks, while the enemy's timetable revealed which one he feared. The mainframes could track every convoy, and the infantry never needed one. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 6

Lam Son 719 Brought the Sorties and Left the Controllers Home

Air power against an army is only as good as the link between the airplane and the soldier who needs it, so check the link before you count the sorties. In Lam Son 719, the ARVN raid toward Tchepone in February 1971, the NVA massed anyway, because the ground half of the team could not call in the air half.

The handicap was written into law. Cooper-Church barred American ground troops from operating outside South Vietnam, so the ARVN crossed on February 8 without its US advisors and tactical air controllers, and President Thieu had already told his commander to limit losses (an odd brief for an invasion). The NVA grew past 34,000 men and had every ARVN unit back home by March 24. US air forces flew 8,500 tacair and 1,358 B-52 sorties, and the Army lost 107 helicopters. Haun's verdict: the ARVN "could not leverage air power to defeat the enemy."

The weak seminar comment is "Lam Son 719 proves air power can't stop a determined army." The strong one: at Khe Sanh, dug-in Marines with coordinated air support forced the NVA to mass into the bombs, while in Laos the ARVN attacked into NVA ground with nobody to direct strikes. Ask of any plan who on the ground talks to the airplanes, and whether the law lets them be there. Opinion: Cooper-Church was a decision about soldiers that quietly decided where bombs would land. Fighters checked in every ten minutes, and the people who aim them were across the border by act of Congress. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 7

At Quang Tri, the Clouds Had Air Superiority

Before promising that air power will stop an army, check the conditions on the day: the cloud deck, the enemy's missiles, who runs the radios, and whether friendly troops will stand. In the Easter Offensive the NVA massed and maneuvered, and at An Loc and Kontum air power wrecked it. At Quang Tri, the same air forces in the same spring could not stop it.

The North Vietnamese crossed the DMZ on March 30 under low cloud, 40,000 troops and 200 tanks behind more than 5,000 artillery rounds in the first day. The NVA fired 1,000 SA-2s, which drove off the gunships and slow FACs. The Air Force had also just handed the region's air control to a Vietnamese-only center that now fielded hundreds of English-only American pilots. By May 2 the ARVN 3rd Division was destroyed. At An Loc, 6,200 defenders and 25 American advisors held while MACV sent 300 tacair and 30 B-52 sorties on May 11 alone, and the NVA lost three divisions there.

The weak seminar comment is "Air power defeated the Easter Offensive." The strong one: it won where the sky cleared, the missile threat was moderate, Americans still ran strike control, and the ARVN held. Lieutenant General Hollingsworth said "give me something to bomb and I'll win"; Abrams said that without Vietnamese who would fight, "ten times the air would not have stopped them." Opinion: Abrams describes a precondition, and Hollingsworth a wish. Hanoi's best air defense that April was an overcast, and it cost nothing to build. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 8

Twenty-Three Air Campaigns, One Moral: Bring an Army

Before grading any air campaign, ask who was fighting on the ground alongside it. Haun codes his twenty-three campaigns against four operational and five environmental factors. His verdict is that direct attack on fielded forces, paired with a capable friendly army, succeeds far more often than any independent air campaign, mainly by making the enemy scatter and hide.

The scorecard is blunt. Eleven campaigns were direct attack, and nine succeeded militarily, five of them politically as well. Strategic bombing reached its political goal in two of six. Interdiction, the seminar's favorite punching bag, succeeded militarily once in six and politically never. Split direct attack by ground partner and the gap widens: with a competent friendly army, seven of seven met their military aims and four their political ones; without one, two of four and one of four. In seven of the nine wins, dispersal was air power's main contribution. Only at Khe Sanh and in the Easter Offensive did the NVA keep massing, and air power wrecked it.

Why does the Air Force keep forgetting? Armed reconnaissance does not justify an independent service, its targeting is shared with Army commanders, and when it works best the Air Force "drops fewer bombs while the Army takes the credit." The service scrubbed it from doctrine in 1966 and again after Desert Storm. Haun's fix is to adopt Marine aviation doctrine as joint doctrine.

The weak seminar comment is "Rolling Thunder failed because politicians tied the airmen's hands." The strong one: independent bombing rarely works on this scorecard, restrictions or no, and Linebacker II, the famous exception, got Hanoi to sign terms identical to those already agreed after its army lost the Easter Offensive. Opinion: with six cases a column, one more Kosovo would make the bombing column look a lot friendlier. Analysts who count dead tanks have criticized successful direct attack, which is like grading a goalkeeper on how many saves he had to make. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Haun, chapter 9

The Kyiv Forecast the Kremlin Never Ran

Before a war starts, score the air plan on the nine factors and count how many come out against you. Haun's epilogue runs the check on someone else's air force: applied ahead of time to the Battle of Kyiv, he argues, five of the nine would have flagged trouble for Russian air power. His eleven recommendations mostly say less dogfighting, more SEAD, more sensors, and a real ground partner.

The Kyiv numbers looked like a mismatch. Russia invaded on February 24, 2022 with an estimated 1,200 tactical aircraft, 350 of them deployed against Ukraine, while Ukraine fielded 100 inherited from the Soviet Union. Russia kept the air-to-air edge and still lost the fight for air superiority, because it could not suppress Ukraine's mobile SAMs, which after three days drove its strikers down into the guns and manpads. Clouds covered Kyiv 70 percent of the time in late February, and only the Su-34 could really fight at night. The one factor that broke Russia's way was civilians, since the Russians showed little regard for them.

Haun says the check would not have called the battle; it would have told Moscow what to expect from its air force, which is more than Moscow apparently knew. Opinion: his third recommendation, "Enemy fighters are less of a threat than SAMs," will be the hardest sell, since it isn't what fighter pilots put on the squadron patch. Services fund what their culture already likes, which is why AirLand Battle sold and AirSea Battle did not. The hardest target in modern air warfare may be the budget. (S)

Haun: chapter recall

  • What are Haun's three types of air campaign?

    Strategic bombing to coerce a nation through its population, economy, or leadership; air interdiction against supply lines and reinforcements; and direct attack on fielded forces, usually with friendly ground troops. H 3

  • What is the scorecard across the twenty-three modern US air campaigns?

    Direct attack: eleven tried, nine military successes, five political. Strategic bombing: two successes in six. Air interdiction: one success in six. H 7

  • What dilemma does TAP theory say a threatened army faces?

    Concentrate and maneuver and be hit by airstrikes, or disperse and hide and lose the initiative while staying open to ground attack. H 14

  • Where in Vietnam did the NVA take the concentrate horn, and with what result?

    At Khe Sanh, where combined arms destroyed two divisions, and in the Easter Offensive, where fourteen NVA divisions were defeated. H 32

  • Why, in Haun's account, could the NVA freely disperse north of the DMZ?

    Johnson ruled out a ground invasion of North Vietnam for fear of Chinese intervention as in Korea, so the NVA never had to concentrate to defend its border. H 54

  • What operational reason does Haun give for why air power alone could not interdict the Ho Chi Minh Trail?

    NVA light infantry and VC guerrillas needed minimal supplies, and Hanoi's repair crews and bypasses absorbed the damage. H 51, 55

  • How did the NVA at Khe Sanh depart from what TAP theory predicted?

    Instead of dispersing under a lethal air threat, it massed two divisions against the Marine base, and air power and artillery decimated it. H 77, 103

  • Why was air power largely irrelevant in the Tet Offensive's city fighting?

    ISR could not track dispersed VC infiltrating cities, street fighting ruled out tacair and B-52s, and South Vietnamese police and militia won the urban battles. H 105

  • How did the DIA and the CIA disagree about Commando Hunt I, and who was right?

    DIA and 7th Air Force said it cut supplies reaching South Vietnam by 82 percent; the CIA said the enemy there needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day and got them. Haun says both were correct. H 128

  • Why does Haun say the AC-130 was not the greatest threat to the NVA, despite killing the most trucks?

    The NVA chose to move at night, when the gunships flew, which shows it feared the daytime FACs and tacair more. H 132

  • Why could the ARVN not direct US air power during Lam Son 719?

    The Cooper-Church amendment barred US ground troops from leaving South Vietnam, so the ARVN went into Laos without its US advisors and tactical air controllers. H 140

  • What two differences from Khe Sanh does Haun use to explain the allied overconfidence in air power?

    At Khe Sanh the Marines defended fortified positions, forcing the NVA to concentrate on the offensive, and air and ground action was well coordinated. In Laos the ARVN attacked into NVA territory without its advisors and controllers. H 148

  • What three reasons does Haun give for air power failing to prevent the fall of Quang Tri?

    Effective NVA air defenses along the DMZ, bad flying weather, and an ineffective South Vietnamese tactical air control system that had just been handed over by the USAF. H 170

  • Why does Haun call An Loc a natural experiment?

    Its defenders never had artillery support or naval gunfire, so they relied on massive US and South Vietnamese airstrikes and parachute resupply. H 176

  • How did direct attack campaigns fare with and without a capable friendly ground force?

    With one, all seven succeeded militarily and four politically; without one, two of four succeeded militarily and one of four politically. H 222

  • What was air power's main contribution in most successful direct attack campaigns?

    Dispersing the enemy army, in seven of nine cases; only at Khe Sanh and in the Easter Offensive did the NVA keep concentrating, and air power then played the decisive role in its defeat. H 224

  • What was the primary, predictable reason Russia failed to gain air superiority over Kyiv?

    It could not suppress Ukraine's mobile SAMs, which after three days forced Russian strikers down into the low-altitude gun and manpad threat. H 239

  • Which of the nine factors did not work against Russian air power at Kyiv?

    Civilians: population density did not constrain the Russians, who showed little regard for civilian suffering. H 241

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
