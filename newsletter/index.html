Lost in Space, Found in Translation: McDougall Goes to Seminar

The syllabus opens today's seminar with a country that thought its oceans would protect it, then woke up on October 4, 1957, to find that an enemy could reach the heartland quickly and with tremendous firepower. Then it gives away the ending. The Soviets, it says, used their win in the information domain to hide the fact that they were never actually ahead technically, and the fear they created may have changed American politics and the way the military buys things. That's the whole book in two sentences, which leaves Walter McDougall about 450 pages to prove it. He does.

His thesis is bigger than the race. Between Sputnik and 1965, McDougall argues, the West abandoned its belief that state direction of science defeated itself, and Western governments quintupled their spending on research for national goals. He calls the result technocracy, technological change turned into an institution that serves the state. And he ends on a warning: no state can quit the race while its rivals stay in, and the race itself threatens to "erode the very values that make one's society worth defending." Cheerful stuff for a Friday.

The syllabus asks five questions, and McDougall answers all of them:

  1. How did the Soviet Union seemingly get ahead in space technology?
  2. The Eisenhower administration was generally excellent at propaganda, so why did it not counter effectively?
  3. What are the capabilities and limitations of propaganda?
  4. Did the Kennedy administration fight the Space Race more effectively than its predecessor?
  5. What impact did the Space Race have on military technological and operational development?

On the first, the Soviets got "ahead" by building big because they couldn't build well. "For it was all a bluff," McDougall writes. Their giant booster was giant because their rocketry was primitive. The first confirmed count of deployed Soviet ICBMs is 150, in 1965, and in late 1962 the Americans led roughly 194 to 72. Khrushchev told his party congress that missiles were coming off the line "like sausages," and he was not, it turns out, describing the production rate. On the second, Eisenhower had the facts and couldn't show them. The U-2 photographs proved the missile gap was a myth, and they were classified. So Ike told reporters "What you want is enough," which is good strategy and terrible television. He also publicized the wrong event: Vanguard TV-3, billed as a satellite attempt, rose four feet on December 6, 1957, and collapsed in flames, leaving the press to invent Flopnik and Kaputnik. On the third, propaganda moved allies (by 1960 Britons judged the Soviets stronger than the Americans, 59 to 15) and it lasted only as long as nobody could look. Once American reconnaissance satellites flew in 1961, the CIA's count of Soviet missiles fell from 120 to 60 to at most fourteen. McDougall's summary: the first satellite sparked the missile gap, and the first reconnaissance satellites snuffed it out.

The fourth and fifth questions are where seminar will argue. Kennedy's team, McDougall says, thought of space in terms of ends rather than means, picked the moon, and bought 1,000 Minutemen after its own satellites showed there was no gap to close. Was that more effective? It was certainly more popular. By mid-October 1964, 77 percent of Americans wanted Apollo continued at its pace or faster. As for the military, its professionals won bigger budgets and lost control of their own research and deployment. The Air Force's Dyna-Soar died in December 1963 after seven years and 400 million dollars.

Opinion: bring the connection the syllabus didn't spell out. Crane's Air Force in Korea admitted it didn't "advertise our limitations." Zhang's Soviets disguised their pilots and both sides inflated their kill claims. McDougall's Khrushchev advertised strengths he didn't have, and his Eisenhower couldn't advertise strengths he did. Four books in, the course keeps teaching the same lesson about limited war: the image is part of the weapon. In the Space Race, the image was the cheaper part. (S)

Monday's Reading · the Vietnam decision exercise

Close Air Support: The Mission Nobody Wanted, Delivered by the Ton

Monday is the decision exercise, and the syllabus's tasking is blunt. You're on the Air Staff in July 1965, the Chief of Staff, Gen. John P. McConnell, wants an airpower strategy to meet the President's objectives in Vietnam, and the seminar splits into two groups that must offer options that differ in some concrete way: where to focus, what to hit, what the center of gravity is. The reading is John Schlight's official history, The War in South Vietnam: The Years of the Offensive, 1965 to 1968, introduction and the first two chapters, plus a document pack. (Schlight flew in support of the French in Indochina in the early 1950s, which gives him a longer view of this war than most of its planners had.)

His topline is uncomfortable for the home team. Before the war, he writes in the preface, close air support "was not a favored mission of the Air Force," because it handed much control of the aircraft to ground commanders, and many airmen considered it the least efficient use of the air weapon. South Vietnam made it the main event. His verdict, which he saves for the conclusion, is that the Air Force fought hard and well while possessing neither its own war objective nor enough reliable data to measure the results. Few service histories put that in writing, fewer still with the Air Force's own seal on the cover.

What he's arguing against is the comfortable idea that the Air Force simply did what it was told. The book traces a running fight over who would control airpower. The Air Force wanted it centralized at MACV, the Army wanted it at the corps level, and Air Force leaders saw MACV as "a unified command in name only." For Monday, that fight is the background to every option your group can put on a slide.

Read for:

  1. Introduction, pages 3 to 11: who ran the advisory effort, how few airmen were in it, and why the Air Force called MACV a unified command in name only.
  2. Introduction, page 6: the Viet Cong soldier who hid from spotter planes in a hammock of green khaki strung between two palm trees, and what it says about finding targets in this war.
  3. Chapter I, page 20: Col. William Bethea at Bien Hoa, who once took a Vietnamese observer out of jail to fly a mission, and why the rule requiring a Vietnamese aboard existed at all.
  4. Chapter I, pages 21 to 31: McConnell's Plan 39, built around strike squadrons, losing to a ground war in the south, and the April conference where bombing as a strategy was judged short-lived and inadequately tested.
  5. Chapter II, pages 50 to 54: the first B-52 Arc Light strike on June 18, 1965, two bombers lost in a midair collision, no enemy found, and the press asking about a sledgehammer used to kill gnats.

Short on time? Read the preface and Chapter II's Arc Light pages, then skim Chapter I for Plan 39. That's the July 1965 menu, and Monday you're the one ordering. (S)

Perception Is Nine-Tenths of the Alliance: McDougall Reads a NATO Crisis

The prompt, for the regulars: one crisis only, ten pages, due December 1, and it asks two questions:

  1. What is the most important potential strategic crisis facing the nation?
  2. Based on your historical understanding of the Age of Limited War, how should Airmen prepare to face it?

We've run Zhang against Taiwan and an alliance coming apart. Today we try McDougall on another of the syllabus's examples, a Russian invasion of a NATO member. What follows is our extrapolation from McDougall's evidence. He didn't write it, it isn't Shaun's answer, and McDougall, who has opinions about almost everything, never weighed in on Lithuania.

The news supplies a starting point. On Wednesday RAND published a study of how France, Germany, Poland, and the United Kingdom would react to a limited American pullback from Europe, built on Cold War precedents and interviews with 51 officials and experts. Its main finding, as Defense News reported it, is that how the United States leaves matters more than whether it leaves. The allies think they could handle an orderly, coordinated drawdown. A rapid, uncoordinated one, they fear, would open capability gaps and might encourage Russian aggression. None of the four would side with Russia or China in any scenario studied, which is reassuring as far as it goes.

Now McDougall's lens. Opinion from here. His Khrushchev shows that a crisis in Europe can be won or lost in the minds of allies before a shot is fired. The Soviet missile force was mostly stage set, yet by 1960 British respondents judged the Soviets stronger than the United States by 59 to 15, and an April 1960 poll found only one Frenchman in fourteen expecting the United States to prevail over twenty years. The bluff didn't move the Americans over Berlin. It moved the allies, and the allies are the alliance. An Airman preparing for a Russian move on a NATO member should expect the first campaign to be a contest of perception, aimed at the capitals deciding whether to fight.

The second lesson is the remedy McDougall documents. Reconnaissance ended the missile gap faster than any missile did. When American satellites started returning film in 1961, the CIA's estimate of Soviet missiles fell from 120 to at most fourteen. For a NATO crisis, the Airman's contribution may be less about the first strike and more about the first photograph: intelligence that can be shown to allies quickly, and declassified fast enough to matter. Eisenhower lost the missile-gap argument because his best evidence was secret. Don't repeat the mistake in Vilnius.

McDougall's last warning is about the bill. He'd remind the paper's author that a nation racing to prepare can erode the values that make it worth defending. In a NATO crisis, that's the alliance's credibility. RAND says it rests on what its authors call "abrupt, easily reversible decisions." Bluffs, as Khrushchev learned, have a shelf life. (S)

The Readings So Far · Gaddis, Crane, Zhang, and McDougall

Four Books, One Theme: Everybody Was Bluffing, Some Better Than Others

Four books in, the course has a running theme, and it isn't the one on the syllabus cover. Gaddis gave us the definition: strategy is relating ends to means, and Truman's people believed resources were limited no matter how frightening the threat. Crane showed that belief meeting a real war in Korea, where FEAF's General Smart said Americans "certainly don't advertise our limitations." Zhang turned the map around and found the Soviets flying MiGs in Chinese uniforms, with both sides inflating their kill claims, the Americans at eight or ten to one before some revised it to about two. McDougall now finishes the thought. Khrushchev advertised a missile force he didn't have, and Eisenhower couldn't advertise the one he did.

Put Gaddis next to McDougall and you get the course's sharpest contrast. Truman's team picked the bomb because a 14.4-billion-dollar ceiling on the fiscal 1950 defense budget couldn't buy a foothold in Europe, which makes deterrence a budget decision before it was a doctrine. A decade later, McDougall's Kennedy team chose the end first, the moon, and assumed the economy would grow to pay for it. That's Gaddis's definition of strategy run backwards. Ike sat in the middle with his favorite word, "enough," and lost the argument anyway.

Now pair Zhang and McDougall, which is a smaller link and maybe a better one. Zhang argues that Stalin's death in March 1953, more than American air pressure, broke the Korean armistice deadlock. McDougall agrees in passing: with Stalin gone, Malenkov, Mikoyan, and others approved a negotiated end to the war. And McDougall reports that Soviet scholars dated the American scientific-technical revolution from 1953, because Korea had locked in large-scale government research. In Moscow's reading, the most lasting thing the Korean War produced was the American research machine.

The news has been building the same web all week. Wednesday's Michigan Senate polls, two pollsters a few points apart, were Zhang's kill-ratio problem with yard signs. Thursday's campaign ads in borrowed faces and voices sat next to Zhang's Soviet pilots in Chinese uniforms, and Thursday's Navy mishap numbers sat next to the Chinese pilots rushed out with sixty-three flying hours. Today the Cook Political Report's preference for "toss-up" ratings joins Kennedy's space gap, raised more than twenty times in 1960 and closed, by the new administration's own admission, eighteen days into its term. A close race raises money. A gap raises budgets.

Opinion: Monday's book is where the bluffing stops being cheap. Crane told us Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded and go back to planning for general war, a bill it paid in Vietnam. Schlight's Air Force arrives in South Vietnam with a close air support mission it never wanted. It had prepared for a different war, and the image it had of itself was the first thing to go. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 1, Day 5 · citations and Chicago style

Footnotes Are Load-Bearing: Booth on Why Citations Aren't Decoration

Friday is citation day, the topic every writer skips until the night before the deadline, and our guide is The Craft of Research, by Wayne Booth and his coauthors, a book published by the University of Chicago Press that is therefore in no danger of getting Chicago style wrong. Their case for citations is practical. Citations protect you from a charge of plagiarism and build your credibility, since getting the small details right marks you as a careful researcher. They let readers check how reliable, current, and complete your evidence is, and pick up the trail from your sources. And they honor the people you borrowed from. In the authors' words, "readers don't trust sources they can't find." Neither do graders.

The rules that matter for a seminar paper are short. Cite every quotation, paraphrase, and summary, and cite each one separately when it comes from a different page. Add the citation the moment you insert the quotation, because the most common problem, they write, is losing track of which words are yours. Put striking phrases in quotation marks even when they're short. And run the paraphrase test: read the passage, look away, write it in your own words, then see whether you can run your finger along your sentence and find the source's words, in the same order, with synonyms swapped in. If you can, the sentence still belongs to the source, now wearing a fake mustache. On intent, they're unbending: the test is whether the original author would recognize her words or ideas in yours. "Well, we read words, not minds."

Here's a sentence from the 628 readings that fails the test. Before: "The missile gap was a myth, and the first spy satellites snuffed it out." No citation, and the second half is nearly McDougall's own line. After: "Walter McDougall calls the missile gap a myth, and argues that if the first satellite sparked it, the first reconnaissance satellites 'snuffed it out.'" Then the note, in Chicago's notes-and-bibliography style, which Booth calls author-title and which the humanities favor: "Walter A. McDougall, ...The Heavens and the Earth: A Political History of the Space Age (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1997), 329." The note puts the first name first, separates elements with commas, and puts the publication data in parentheses. The bibliography entry reverses the name and uses periods. Chicago is particular about commas, which is why it fills a manual of its own.

Booth also tells you when to quote at all: when the words themselves are evidence, when they come from an authority who backs your claim, when they're strikingly original, or when you're stating a view you disagree with and fairness requires the exact wording. Everything else gets paraphrased or summarized. And he insists that "evidence never speaks for itself," so a long quotation needs a sentence in front of it telling the reader what to take from it.

Pro tip, from an old editor's bag of tricks: never paste a quotation without its page number attached, even in a rough draft. Three weeks later you'll remember the quote perfectly and the page not at all, and McDougall runs to about 460 pages. Opinion: the citation is the one part of your paper the grader can check in thirty seconds. Make those thirty seconds go your way. (S)

Mind the Gap: Why Every Campaign Wants to Be a Toss-Up

Electoral-vote.com spent part of Thursday on the people who grade the races, building on a Washington Post story about why official Washington hangs on every word of the Cook Political Report. The Report started in 1984 as a niche newsletter for lobbyists and PACs, the kind of publication you subscribe to because your boss already does. It added its Partisan Voting Index in 1997 and moved online in the early 2000s, where it found a much bigger audience. Its ratings run solid, likely, lean, and toss-up. They are subjective, built from polls, fundraising, the candidates themselves, and whatever the campaigns did that week, and a rating change is rare enough that it takes a significant development to get one.

The track record is better than most things in politics. An analysis of 10,374 races from 1984 through 2024 found that 97 percent of candidates rated "likely" won, 94 percent of those rated "lean" won, and the toss-ups split about evenly, which is what a toss-up is supposed to do. Charlie Cook, now retired, has little use for prediction markets. "I want expertise," he said, a sentence that will not be appearing on any betting app's home screen.

Candidates react fast when a rating moves, and some campaigns reportedly hide their good internal polls. Why on earth would a campaign hide good news? Because campaigns generally prefer to be rated a toss-up. A toss-up raises money. A comfortable lead tells donors to go help somebody else.

So the most valuable thing a campaign can own, four days out, is a gap it might fall into.

Now go to today's book, where Washington ran the same play at the scale of a superpower. McDougall's 1960 campaign was fought partly over a gap that the CIA's worst cases sized at up to 100 Soviet ICBMs by 1960 and 400 in 1961, while the U-2 photographs seemed to show none deployed at all. Kennedy raised the "space gap" more than twenty times during the campaign, and Nixon answered about half as often. Then, on February 6, 1961, Secretary of Defense McNamara admitted at an off-the-record briefing that each side seemed to have about the same small number of missiles, and Republicans sarcastically congratulated the new president on closing the gap in eighteen days. (Few programs in history have delivered so quickly.) McDougall also catches the bureaucratic version of the toss-up strategy. NASA's budget testimony leaned on the Soviet challenge, and in his words, NASA clearly needed the Soviets.

Opinion: the lesson cuts across party lines, because both parties have lived on both sides of it. A threat that looks close is an asset to whoever is asking for money, whether the money is for a Senate race or a space agency. That doesn't make the threat fake. It does mean the person describing the gap is rarely a neutral referee, and the analyst's job is to ask who benefits from the race staying close. Cook's team, to its credit, publishes its misses.

Campaigns hide their good polls so they look vulnerable. Eisenhower hid his good photographs because they were classified. Only one of them lost the election over it, and he wasn't on the ballot. (S)

Acquisition × McDougall · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, October 29

Fill 'Er Up, Eventually: The Tanker That Needs a Decade of Good Behavior

The Hudson Institute released an analysis on Tuesday, written by Timothy A. Walton and Shane Dennin, that tells the Air Force to buy 250 medium-sized, long-range tankers, such as a blended-wing body design, that could refuel from a safer distance than today's fleet. The numbers are generous to the idea. The study says a medium blended-wing tanker could refuel more than twice as many force packages as the KC-46A, or refuel them more than twice as far, and at long range could deliver up to five times as many useful increments of fuel. It would carry about 300,000 pounds of fuel against 212,000 for the KC-46 and 245,000 for the A330 MRTT+. Over thirty years, the authors say, a mixed fleet of roughly 260 KC-46s and 250 blended wings would cost 398 billion dollars, against 413 billion for 510 KC-46s. That's fifteen billion dollars saved, which in Pentagon terms counts as finding money in the couch.

The study's advice comes in a sensible order: extend KC-46 production first, harden and expand the most important tanker airfields, keep funding command and control and self-defense, and then, if the demonstrator flies well in 2027 and 2028, steer fiscal 2027 research money toward a medium blended-wing design. Fielding could begin by the mid-2030s. The demonstrator in question is JetZero's Z4, built with Northrop Grumman since 2023, and it isn't a tanker. JetZero has proposed a tanker version, the KC-Z4. An Air Force spokesperson says the first flight is on schedule for late 2027.

The Air Force itself is less settled. Its fiscal 2027 request moved the Next-Generation Aerial Refueling System toward protection systems instead of a new aircraft, and it still plans to buy up to 75 more KC-46s beyond the 188 on contract, once the Pegasus's remote vision problems are fixed. The magazine's caveats are worth reading twice. Retired Gen. Mike Minihan, the former Air Mobility Command chief, supports the effort and now advises JetZero, which the article discloses. And Douglas Birkey of the Mitchell Institute, who likes the aerodynamics, doubts the service can hold a decade-long funding line against the B-21 and the F-47. "They need a decade's worth of solid investment," he said. Few programs have ever been accused of receiving that.

Now go to today's book, which has a graveyard for exactly this kind of plane. Dyna-Soar, the Air Force's spaceplane, was funded for seven years, to a total of 400 million dollars, and terminated in December 1963. The Manned Orbiting Laboratory was announced the next day, which is the procurement version of "the king is dead, long live the king." McDougall's explanation is bigger than one program. He cites the systems theorist C. West Churchman's test: a system's real objective is whatever its managers will knowingly sacrifice other goals to get. American technocracy, by that test, wanted growth and clean air, excellence and mass education, guns and butter, and so McDougall concludes that its objective was to avoid defining its objective.

Opinion: run Churchman's test on the tanker. The study is honest about the bill: more research money in the late 2020s and early 2030s, before a single aircraft shows up. The question is what the Air Force will knowingly give up to pay it, with the B-21 and the F-47 already standing in line. If the answer is "nothing," Birkey's doubt is the forecast. A tanker is the least glamorous airplane in the inventory and the one every other airplane needs, which is roughly the position Dyna-Soar's backers would have killed for.

The blended wing promises to refuel jets more than twice as far away as today's tanker can. First its funding has to survive the next budget, which is the longer flight. (S)

Leadership × McDougall · via Reuters in Defense News, October 29

Need to Know, Need to Go: A Planner's Firing and the Price of Secrets

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has fired Army Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia, who led the Joint Staff's Directorate for Strategy, Plans, and Policy, the J5, which advises the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, Gen. Dan Caine. Sylvia previously commanded the 101st Airborne Division. The Atlantic first reported the dismissal, and Reuters' Phil Stewart had the details. Sylvia is accused of leaking classified information. A senior Pentagon official, speaking anonymously, said he failed three polygraph exams about the leaks. Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell said an Air Force Office of Special Investigations inquiry led to the decision and that the matter, along with others, has been referred to the Justice Department. "We vowed to find you, and we meant it," Parnell said, a line that sounds better from a search-and-rescue squadron.

The article is careful about what it doesn't know. It doesn't say what information Sylvia allegedly leaked, the polygraph account comes from an unnamed official, no charges are reported, and Sylvia could not be reached for comment, so his side of the story is missing. The leaks reportedly concern Pentagon planning and munitions spending. One detail stands out: the Joint Staff withdrew an earlier statement thanking him for 32 years of service, saying it "did not appropriately reflect the full circumstances." Retracting a thank-you note is unusual, even in Washington.

The firing is the most prominent result so far of a broad effort to find officials suspected of talking to the press during the Iran war, now in its ninth month. That effort includes a Pentagon leak task force and widespread polygraphing of senior officials. It follows a media policy that drew legal objections from press organizations and cost credentials to journalists who refused to sign it. We'll leave the merits of this case to the investigators, which is where they belong.

Now go to today's book, which has a long chapter on secrets kept from the wrong audience. In January 1961, McGeorge Bundy imposed a blackout on reconnaissance satellite launches, and the Air Force was forbidden even to mention satellite observation in public. McDougall points out who actually knew what the satellites were doing: the Soviets, foreign experts, and opinion makers around the world, who assumed the worst anyway. In his words, "Only the American public was being fooled." A chapter earlier, Eisenhower had the opposite problem. He knew from U-2 photographs that the missile gap was a myth and couldn't say so without showing his cards, so he lost the argument on the facts he couldn't release.

Opinion: the leadership lesson here doesn't depend on how the Sylvia case ends. Classification is a trust arrangement that runs in two directions. Officers who leak break it in one direction, and that is why the system treats leaks so seriously. A government that classifies what the adversary already knows strains it from the other, because the only people left in the dark are the ones paying for the war. McDougall's blackout fooled no one in Moscow. A leader who controls secrets should know exactly who each secret is being kept from, and why.

Thirty-two years of service, three polygraphs, one withdrawn thank-you, and still no public account of what was leaked. Somebody, it appears, is very good at keeping a secret. (S)

Defense & Conflict × McDougall · via CSIS, October 29

Second Mouse Gets the Cheese: CSIS Makes the Case for Copying Fast

Mick Ryan, an adjunct fellow with the CSIS Australia Chair, published a report on Thursday titled "Fast Following: Military Emulation and Twenty-First-Century Transformation," and its argument fits on a bumper sticker: going first is overrated, copying well is underrated. Fast following, in Ryan's definition, means watching an innovation that somebody else has proven, then copying it, improving it, and fielding it quickly. He treats war as a contest of learning and adaptation. Western militaries, he says, have spent heavily on innovation units and experiments and built very little systematic ability to fast follow, while China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea operate together as a fast-following system. Collaboration, it turns out, is the one thing that bloc does efficiently.

The evidence starts in business. Golder and Tellis, studying 500 brands, found that first movers failed about 47 percent of the time and early followers about 8 percent. Then it moves to the battlefield. Russia took Iran's Shahed-136, built it as the Geran-2 in the tens of thousands, and added jet-powered versions. By February 2026, Ukrainian interceptor drones costing 800 to 3,000 dollars accounted for about 70 percent of Shahed kills around Kyiv. The United States answered with LUCAS, developed from a Shahed training replica at about 35,000 dollars apiece and used from the first day of the Iran war, February 28. Ryan puts the gap between the Shahed's demonstration and America's combat use at about three and a half years. In Ukraine, the same gap is measured in weeks. And in August, ISW reported that Russia had sent upgraded Shahed-type drones back to Iran, so the original design is now copying its own improved descendant. That is either a supply chain or a family reunion.

His eight recommendations are practical. Two stand out. Measure the gap between a demonstration and your own adoption, and report it to leaders and legislatures. And change promotion incentives to reward emulation and adaptation, which would be a novel use of a promotion board. He also warns against copying a lesson without the conditions that made it work, which may be the most useful sentence in the report.

Now go to today's book, where both superpowers practiced fast following before anyone called it that. After the war, the Americans and the Soviets each carried off German rocket engineers as the choicest loot of the conflict. Eisenhower then went second on purpose: four days after Sputnik, Donald Quarles of the Pentagon admitted that the Army's Redstone could have orbited a satellite a year or more earlier, and added that the Russians had "done us a good turn" by establishing freedom of international space. Then Moscow fast-followed Washington. The Soviets spent years calling American spy satellites illegal. On April 6, 1962, they launched their own, labeled with great blandness Kosmos IV, and by 1965 they were launching more than twice as many reconnaissance satellites as the United States.

There's a darker passage too. By 1973, McDougall counts 124 Soviet spacecraft launched to 23 American, and he had already warned, before the first moon landing, that "the American hare had stopped again to take a nap." Fast following only works if the leader keeps moving. If the leader stops, the follower wins.

Opinion: Ryan and McDougall agree on the part that stings. Going first is a choice, and so is going second, and Eisenhower made the second choice deliberately and paid for it in prestige. What neither man accepts is going second by accident, at a pace of three and a half years, while the enemy iterates in weeks. Ryan's report includes one more case for Monday's readers: North Vietnamese missile crews who kept their radars dark until targets were close, and the American Wild Weasels built to answer them. The race, as Ryan concludes by quoting Simpkin, is still to the swift. The tortoise has not been consulted. (S)

Space × Sci-Fi × McDougall · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, October 28

Welcome to the Dark Forest, Please Keep Your Transponder Off

The Defense Intelligence Agency released the third edition of its "Challenges to Security in Space" report on Monday, following editions in 2019 and 2022, and Greg Hadley of Air & Space Forces Magazine read it for changes in wording, which is how intelligence reports announce news. China, the DIA says, is developing space-based technology, including on-orbit counterspace weapons, capable of holding space systems at risk. By the late 2020s it may field space-based directed-energy systems, plus more powerful ground lasers that could reach satellites in higher orbits. For the first time, the DIA lists on-orbit jammers as part of China's electronic warfare. It now says flatly that Chinese firms are developing jammers aimed at synthetic aperture radar, where the 2022 edition said "probably." China also "probably intends to deploy" more satellite-killing missiles for low, medium, and high orbits, up from "probably intends to pursue." In intelligence prose, losing a "probably" is a five-alarm fire.

Two details belong in seminar. Chinese satellites have maneuvered within ten kilometers of other countries' satellites in geosynchronous orbit, in approaches the report calls "largely inconsistent with the stated mission of the satellites." And the report includes a rare commercial Maxar image of China's experimental spaceplane, which looks a good deal like the American X-37B and has released, maneuvered around, and rendezvoused with other satellites. Meanwhile the Space Force acknowledged at the September AFA conference that it has on-orbit weapons of its own for space control, which takes some of the suspense out of who is armed up there.

Now go to today's book, because none of this would have surprised the Eisenhower administration. In 1958 its Operations Coordinating Board wondered how to develop "a cover for such reconnaissance satellites." By October it had settled on rhetoric: wrap the military program in "space for peace," call it vital to deterrence and therefore peaceful, and build a world climate that tolerated classified programs. Washington won the wording fight at the UN, so space would serve "peaceful" purposes rather than "nonmilitary" ones, and the Pentagon's programs fit in the gap between the two words. The Outer Space Treaty, ratified 88 to 0 on April 25, 1967, banned nuclear weapons in orbit and demilitarized the moon but left orbit open to military use. At the hearings, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs said the treaty would require more effort to detect orbiting nuclear weapons, and when a senator asked if that meant more money for military space research, he answered, "That is correct, Senator." The peace treaty came with a budget request attached.

Now for the fiction. In The Dark Forest, the second novel of Liu Cixin's Three-Body Problem trilogy, the universe is a dark forest. Every civilization is an armed hunter moving silently through the trees, and any civilization that reveals its location invites destruction from hunters it can't see. Nobody announces anything, and everybody assumes the worst. A satellite that drifts within ten kilometers of yours, on a mission its owner describes one way and flies another, is a small piece of the dark forest in geosynchronous orbit.

Opinion: McDougall's story is how the superpowers avoided Liu's forest for half a century. They let the cameras fly, kept the bombs out of orbit, and accepted each other's spy satellites, a peace that rested, in our earlier phrase, on mutual hypocrisy. The DIA report describes the weapons that make that arrangement shakier: jammers, lasers, a spaceplane, and satellites whose stated missions don't match their maneuvers. The 1967 treaty never defined where peaceful ends and military begins. Liu's hunters wouldn't have bothered drafting one.

Eisenhower wanted a cover story for his satellites. China's satellites, apparently, came with one already installed. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, introduction and chapter 1

The Long View Starts With a Fish and Ends in a Prison Bureau

McDougall opens a political history of the Space Age with a fish that crawled out of a river 360 million years ago, which is one way to warn readers you plan to take the long view. The introduction's claim is simpler than the fish. For most of history, states reacted to new tools, and inventing them was somebody else's job. The world wars made state research a duty, and World War II left four "war babies": British radar, the American atomic bomb, the German ballistic missile, and the American electronic computer. After the war the West decided that state-directed science defeated itself, and in the ten years up to 1965 that belief collapsed. McDougall calls it a saltation, an evolutionary leap, and Western governments quintupled their R&D funding for national goals. His name for the result is technocracy, technological change made into an institution that serves the state. The trigger was Sputnik, a predictable feat of engineering that arrived at an awkward moment. Lyndon Johnson summed up the new creed: "first in space means first, period; second in space is second in everything."

The introduction's moment to remember is the trap on its last page. A state can turn off its own research machine. It cannot stop the lone problem solver from thinking, and it cannot stop foreign rivals from making new challenges. Only a Big Brother strong enough to control every nation and every subject's thoughts could end the race. Until he arrives, everyone keeps running, and the running threatens to "erode the very values that make one's society worth defending." McDougall calls that "the dilemma of the Space Age and the moral of our story." Cheerful stuff for an introduction.

Chapter 1 takes the theory to Russia, where rocketry and revolution grew up together. Part I opens in 1881 with Tsar Alexander II killed by a People's Will bomb, and the bomb maker, Nikolai Kibalchich, spent his final days in prison designing a rocket-propelled aircraft. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky hated gravity the way other people hate the tax man, published the principles of rocket motion in 1903, and received exactly one grant from the old regime, 470 rubles. In 1919 Lenin drew his lesson from the Great War: master the highest technology or be crushed. A 1924 rocket bureau made the Soviet Union the first government to back spaceflight. Stalin then delivered money and terror in the same envelope. The Industrial Party affair accused eight leading technologists of heading a plot of over 2,000 engineers, while the Academy of Sciences grew from 1,000 people in 1927 to over 16,000 by 1940. Civilian research ran badly, since a failed experiment could brand you a "wrecker." McDougall finds one exception: "That sector was defense." By 1939 Soviet factories turned out 700 to 800 planes a month.

Chapter 1's scene is the sharaga, the prison design bureau. Sergei Korolev got the deputy directorship of the main rocket institute from Marshal Tukhachevsky, whose name became a synonym for death after 1937. Korolev moved to Tupolev's aircraft bureau just in time to follow Tupolev into prison in 1938, and he is said to have spent months in the Kolyma mines. The writer Ozerov later walked into the dining room of Tupolev's prison bureau and found the elite of Soviet aircraft design, every one a prisoner. He concluded that "this meant a catastrophe for Soviet aviation!" McDougall replies, "In fact, it did not." The prisoners designed fighters and dive bombers, and Georgy Langemak, reprieved from a death sentence, designed the Katyusha rocket launcher. It was the most exclusive engineering faculty in the world, and admission came with an arrest warrant.

Opinion: the connection is Conrad Crane, whose subject is "the image and reality of American airpower." Chapter 1 is the Soviet edition. In 1933 Stalin declared August 18 Aviation Day, his "Falcons" chased altitude and distance records, and the campaign handily drew eyes away from the purge trials. Then in 1938 the Messerschmitt 109E arrived in Spain, swept the more numerous Soviet planes from the sky, and Stalin arrested his chief designers. FEAF's General Smart said Americans "certainly don't advertise our limitations." Stalin did him one better. He advertised the records and jailed the limitations. Next to the introduction, the lesson is grim: the technocratic state runs fine without consent.

Seminar question: if terror did not stop Soviet military innovation, what makes us confident that a free society holds the advantage in a race McDougall says nobody is allowed to quit? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 2 and 3

Moscow Got Orbit, Washington Got a Budget Ceiling

Chapter 2 argues that the German V-2 held few theoretical surprises for Soviet rocketeers. Its real effect was to push both victors into early rivalry and make German engineers the choicest loot of the war. The Americans won the loot. Wernher von Braun led 525 people south, his brother Magnus surrendered to a puzzled American private on May 2, 1945, and Stalin fumed that the Americans got the rocket engineers. The true accelerator was Hiroshima. Stalin told his munitions commissar and the physicist Kurchatov, "The equilibrium has been destroyed," and demanded a bomb fast. Kurchatov guessed five years, and the Soviet bomb came in August 1949, a year early. Soviet R&D spending rose from 6,300 million rubles in 1946 to 9,000 million by 1950, when the U.S. government spent $1,083 million, and McDougall judges Soviet military research perhaps six times the American effort as a share of GNP. Korolev's R-7, twenty engines and 1.1 million pounds of thrust, flew on August 3, 1957. In the Part I conclusion McDougall weighs eight traits of Soviet R&D and finds only terror peculiar to defense work, and terror was not decisive: "What slowed Soviet rocketry in the 1930s and 1940s was not terror, but war."

Chapter 2's scene is a cocktail party at the Soviet Embassy in Washington, where scientists from the International Geophysical Year were drinking vodka. A Russian émigré needled his former countrymen: "Poor Tsiolkovsky is turning in his grave," since the prophet's hundredth birthday had passed with no Russian satellite. A Soviet guest said the émigré would eat his words before he got back to Moscow in two days. Soon after, the New York Times reached Walter Sullivan by phone, and Lloyd Berkner clapped for silence to announce that Radio Moscow had a satellite in orbit. Khrushchev, told the news, congratulated the engineers and calmly went to bed. The émigré's words were served well ahead of schedule.

Chapter 3 opens Part II and turns to the United States, which spent 1945 to 1960 trying to deter the Soviets without becoming a garrison state. The answer, nuclear deterrence, chained American defense to fast technological change, and that thrift helped make the country second into space. American science, McDougall says, "grew up an orphan." The NACA began in 1915 as a rider on a naval bill, with a $5,000 budget and twelve unpaid appointees. Robert Goddard collected eighty-three patents and was dismissed as a crackpot. Then came the war, and federal R&D rose from $74 million in 1940 to $1.59 billion in 1945. Peace put the orphan back on short rations. When Congress created the National Science Foundation in 1950, the Senate gave it $225,000, while the military already held 62 percent of federal R&D by 1948. A post-unification directive gave "strategic" missiles to the new Air Force and "tactical" ones to the Army, just as the budget ax fell on everybody.

Chapter 3's scene is a confession. In 1947 Thomas Finletter's Air Policy Commission held more than 350 meetings and asked to see the war plan. After a Pentagon runaround, Truman ordered Eisenhower, Vandenberg, Leahy, and Nimitz to testify, and Eisenhower finally told his colleagues they owed the civilians the truth: "there is no war plan." The force was just as thin. Spaatz figured there were a dozen or so warheads. In August 1946 only six of twenty-six B-29 groups had aircraft, based in the Southwest, where they "threatened no one but Canada and Mexico." A $14.4 billion ceiling on the FY 1950 budget left the Joint Chiefs unable to hold a foothold in Europe, so the bomb became the cheap option, and in July 1949 Truman concluded, "we must be strongest in atomic weapons."

Lesson learned. Opinion: put the two chapters side by side. Moscow, frightened by an American monopoly, poured money into the bomb and beat its own deadline. Washington, comfortable with that monopoly, had a dozen warheads and no war plan, and let a budget ceiling pick its strategy. The lesson holds for any commander: before you lean on a deterrent, ask to see the plan and count what stands behind it, because the other side is counting too, and its fear sets the pace of the race.

Seminar question: if Truman's budget ceiling chose air-atomic deterrence and Stalin's fear of the monopoly set the Soviet pace, who really started the race that ended with Sputnik? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 4 and 5

RAND Called It in 1950, and Ike Came Second on Purpose

Chapter 4 explains why the richest country on earth shelved its rockets in the late 1940s. Truman's war on inflation won. Missile R&D for FY 1947 was cut to $22 million, and eleven programs died at once, including Convair's MX-774, a 5,000-mile ballistic missile. No fuel could yet throw a five-ton warhead that far, and SAC already had bombers near Soviet borders. Vannevar Bush doubted an accurate ICBM was possible, while admitting he was "not much of a prophet." The navy's satellite program was cancelled on June 22, 1948. Fear revived both. The Soviet bomb brought Truman's H-bomb go-ahead on January 31, 1950, then NSC-68 and Korea, and defense spending tripled. BRAVO's 15 megatons in March 1954 came in a package a rocket could carry, so a cruder missile would do, and on June 21, 1954, Atlas became the Air Force's highest priority. Nothing focuses the mind like a Soviet bomb.

Chapter 4's thing to remember is a pair of RAND reports. On May 2, 1946, RAND told the Army Air Forces that a satellite would cost about $150 million and five years, and predicted it "would inflame the imagination of mankind." The October 1950 report, which McDougall calls the "birth certificate of American space policy," saw that satellites were reconnaissance tools, that a closed Soviet Union would treat spying from orbit as an attack on its secrecy, and that nobody knew whether national airspace had a ceiling. The fix was an "experimental" satellite on an equatorial orbit, never crossing Soviet territory, to test "freedom of space" first. RAND guessed the Soviets would have little reason to build a satellite of their own, the one call it badly missed. The report went to the Air Force on October 4, 1950, seven years to the day before Sputnik 1.

Chapter 5 argues that Eisenhower lost the race to orbit because he was running a different race. His New Look, NSC-162/2 of October 1953, planned to cut military spending 30 percent over four years. Cutting safely needed hard intelligence, so the U-2 was built in eighty days. By 1955 Washington had two goals, being first and winning the legal right of overflight, and the NSC put protecting the spy satellite ahead of speed. On August 3, 1955, the Stewart committee voted three to two for the navy's Viking over von Braun's Redstone, with one member home sick who later sided with the army. McDougall calls the choice ideal politically, if not technically. Vanguard's estimate then climbed from $20 million to $63 million by June 1956. When John Hagen called it the Naval Research Lab satellite in front of Soviet visitors, a junior staffer corrected him: "The National Academy's satellite, Dr. Hagen." Message discipline, at least, was never Vanguard's problem.

Chapter 5's scene is the dummy upper stages. On September 20, 1956, the army's Jupiter-C flew 682 miles high and came down 3,355 miles from Cape Canaveral. With a live upper stage it could have reached orbit, so inspectors made sure every upper stage was a dummy and von Braun could not launch a satellite "accidentally." On October 4, 1957, Defense Secretary McElroy was at cocktails in Huntsville when word came of the Soviet satellite. Von Braun begged for sixty days, and Medaris corrected him to ninety. Four days later Quarles admitted to Eisenhower that the Redstone could have orbited a satellite a year or more earlier, then looked on the bright side: the Russians had "done us a good turn" by establishing freedom of international space. Ike asked about a reconnaissance vehicle.

Seminar pro tip. Expect someone to say Eisenhower simply blundered. The evidence that wins the point is chapter 4's 1950 report, which wanted an experimental satellite to go first and settle freedom of space, and chapter 5's Quarles meeting, where the administration's first reaction to Sputnik was relief about the law and a question about spy satellites. The counterargument comes from McDougall himself: his Part II conclusion calls Sputnik "the greatest defeat Eisenhower could have suffered," because it handed critics a weapon against his whole philosophy of government. And watch the dates. The report that landed seven years to the day before Sputnik is the October 1950 one; the 1946 study is the one with the $150 million price tag. Mix them up and a classmate will enjoy correcting you.

Seminar question: was letting the Soviets go first a sound trade for the freedom of space, or did Eisenhower misjudge what a public defeat would cost the policies he was protecting? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 6 and 7

Flopnik Fizzles, Goldstone Has the Bird, and Ike Wants No Fuss

Part III opens with Eisenhower at Thanksgiving 1959, holding a $92.2 billion budget with a billion-dollar surplus and asking his Cabinet what the true problem facing Western civilization was. McDougall casts him as Janus, in the vanguard of the Space Age and its rearguard at once. Chapter 6 argues that the early uproar over Sputnik was a "media riot" with no grassroots movement behind it. A Gallup poll of October 11 to 14 found half the sample calling Sputnik a serious blow to prestige, 46 percent saying it was not, and 61 percent expecting satellites to be used for good. The press, as ignorant of space as anyone, taught the public like a rookie professor one chapter ahead in the textbook. Life told readers, "Let us not pretend that Sputnik is anything but a defeat for the United States." Defense Secretary Wilson helped by calling it a neat scientific trick, and Sherman Adams said the goal was not a high score in a celestial basketball game. A memo to Lyndon Johnson noted that the issue could make him President. Sputnik II went up on November 3 with a 1,121-pound payload and a dog named Laika, and Johnson opened hearings on November 25 by calling Sputnik a "technological Pearl Harbor."

Chapter 6's scene is Vanguard TV-3. The White House insisted that a test flight be billed as a real attempt to orbit a satellite, so reporters from around the world gathered at Cape Canaveral. Shortly before noon on December 6, 1957, the rocket rose four feet, burst into flame, and settled back to earth. The nose cone fell clear, and the little satellite lay nearby, chirping innocently. The press renamed it Kaputnik, Stayputnik, and Flopnik, and Americans drank the Sputnik Cocktail, "two parts vodka, one part sour grapes." At the UN, Soviet delegates asked whether the United States would like aid for underdeveloped countries. Of all the things Eisenhower could have publicized that autumn, he publicized a test.

Chapter 7 argues that Eisenhower answered Sputnik with four initiatives, each built to be temporary, limited, or self-limiting, and that each opened a door he could not shut. In his 1958 State of the Union he said the Soviets were waging "total cold war," yet his first post-Sputnik budget rose only 1.5 percent. The National Defense Education Act was written to run seven years only, but a $60 million rider for students not bound for college went in, and McDougall sees no stopping point after that. Pentagon reorganization was about control: letting every service list its needs produced something like $100 billion, against Ike's FY 1960 ceiling of $44 billion. The Space Act split space into an open civilian program and a closed military one and aimed to keep the United States "a leader, not the leader." NASA opened on October 1, 1958, and McDougall says the act started the civil-military fight instead of ending it.

Chapter 7's scene is the night of January 31, 1958. Vanguard slipped from the 18th to the 23rd to the 26th, then lost its turn to a faulty engine. Jet stream winds held the army's Jupiter-C, and January 31 was its last chance before Vanguard got another crack. At 10:48 p.m. it lit up the Banana River swamps. Then came an hour of waiting, with Army Secretary Brucker complaining from Washington about running short of coffee and cigarettes. Finally a slip of paper: "Goldstone has the bird." Explorer 1 was in orbit. Eisenhower, at Augusta National, said that was wonderful, then added, "Let's not make too great a hullabaloo over this." The only man in America who wanted less hullabaloo was the one who had just won some.

Author's move. Opinion: McDougall's best trick in these chapters is checking the noise against the numbers. Instead of taking the newspapers' word for a national panic, he sets the headlines beside a Gallup poll and finds a public split down the middle and mostly hopeful. That lets him call it a "media riot" and make the label stick, and it shifts the blame from voters to editors, senators, and an administration that kept handing them material. He reads Eisenhower's measures the same way, checking each built-in limit against what followed. Historians who measure the panic before describing it write better history.

Seminar question: if the polls show the public was not panicking at first, who owns the "media riot," and can a government whose best facts are secret ever win one? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 8 and 9

Space for Peace, Some Restrictions Apply, Prestige Sold Separately

Chapter 8 claims that Eisenhower's space strategy had two faces, and the second one ran the show. In public, the United States would champion "space for peace," cooperation, and the rule of law. In private, it would keep its freedom to fly whatever missions the nuclear deterrent needed, above all spy satellites. Ike wrote to Bulganin on January 12, 1958, proposing that outer space be used for peaceful purposes only. Moscow answered by asking, among other things, that American foreign bases be liquidated. Shockingly, that went nowhere. The real policy was NSC 5814/1, approved in mid-August 1958. It split missiles off from space, so no UN space deal could freeze missiles, called reconnaissance satellites critical to national security, and declined to say where outer space began, preferring a functional test: if it is in orbit, it is in space. On November 24, 1958, the General Assembly voted 54 to 9, with 18 abstentions, for the committee that became COPUOS, and the Soviets boycotted it. Washington also won on wording. Space would serve "peaceful" purposes instead of "nonmilitary" ones, and that gap sheltered the Pentagon.

Chapter 8's thing to remember is a question from Eisenhower's Operations Coordinating Board in 1958, which wondered about developing "a cover for such reconnaissance satellites." Its October answer was rhetoric: wrap the military program in "space for peace," call it vital to deterrence and therefore peaceful, and build a world climate that tolerated classified programs. The hardware lagged the rhetoric. Discoverer 1 tumbled in orbit, and Discoverer 2's capsule came down somewhere in northern Norway and was never found. A consultant warned, "Satellites are our last chance." Staffer Sol Horwitz told Lyndon Johnson not to fret over Moscow's protests: "The Russians will scream on any occasion they think it desirable to scream." McDougall's bottom line is that space was about spying because the USSR was secretive.

Chapter 9 argues that the picture of a tight-fisted Eisenhower misses half the story. Ike founded NASA, protected it from the military, handed it the big missions, and tied it to national prestige. The first pick for administrator, Hugh Dryden, sank himself by telling Congress that putting a man in orbit in a Redstone nose cone was like the "circus stunt of shooting a young lady from a cannon." T. Keith Glennan got the job, with Dryden as deputy, sworn in on August 19, 1958. To give NASA muscle, the administration raided the Army: in September 1959 the Pentagon gave military space operations to the Air Force, the Army's Saturn ran short of money, and the von Braun team moved to NASA effective July 1, 1960. Ike gave manned spaceflight to NASA in August 1958, partly because the Air Force looked ready to grab it, and Project Mercury got its name on November 26. In May 1959 Glennan admitted the agency's deeper problem: "It had no objective." McDougall's verdict is blunt. NASA needed the Soviets.

Chapter 9's scene is a dinner. On December 10, 1959, the Greenewalt Committee, chaired by DuPont's president, met at NASA and then went to the White House. Its science group wanted about $100 million a year. Its space race group wanted $2 billion a year. After dinner, in the White House basement, Vice President Nixon spoke for forty-five minutes without notes. Politics outranked science, he said, the real motive was prestige, and in 1963 to 1966 the question would be "how many more Soviet successes can we stand." He would trade space for medical programs or foreign aid if Congress would buy them, but it would not. NSC 5918, approved January 12, 1960, made overall American superiority in space an objective, without leading in every phase, and NASA's FY 1961 budget came to $964 million after Congress added $50 million. For an agency told to be sensible, it ate well.

Lesson learned. Opinion: Eisenhower never drew the line he meant to fly across. He refused to define where space began, chose "peaceful" over "nonmilitary," and let practice write the law while his spy satellites caught up. The lesson for a commander is that when you need a right your adversary will not grant, establish it by doing before you invite a debate over its definition. The second lesson comes from chapter 9 and cuts the other way. An agency whose budget grows with the threat has an interest in the threat, so ask who benefits before you accept anyone's estimate of the danger.

Seminar question: if NASA's budget depended on the Soviet challenge, did Eisenhower build an institution with every reason to keep the race going? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 10 to 12

Sausages, Bluffs, and a President Who Said Enough

Chapter 10, which closes Part III, finds the whole country demanding more government except the government. By 1960 a new consensus had formed against Eisenhower, and it was expensive. Herman Kahn wanted the national budget raised 10 to 20 percent. Maxwell Taylor sold "flexible response." Medaris wanted the civilian staff of the Secretary of Defense cut by 90 percent, an idea that never quite goes out of style in uniform. Walt Rostow's January 2 campaign memo told the Democrats to hit the missile gap, and Kennedy raised the "space gap" over twenty times in the campaign, with Nixon answering about half as often. CIA worst cases ran as high as 100 Soviet ICBMs by 1960 and 400 in 1961, while U-2 photos seemed to show none deployed. After the election a panel priced a manned lunar landing at an extra $26 to $58 billion. Ike said he was "not about to hock his jewels," vetoed Apollo, and approved the Saint satellite interceptor on his way out the door.

The scene to keep is three press conferences, a week apart. Asked whether American prestige was at stake in space, Ike said, "Not particularly, no." A week later, pressed on urgency, he said, "What you want is enough," and added that a deterrent gains nothing once it is fully adequate. A week after that, asked about Khrushchev's flag on the moon, he called the remark crazy and told Americans not to get hysterical. He was right, and he could not prove it, because the proof sat in classified U-2 photographs. On May 1, 1960, a Soviet missile shot down Francis Gary Powers. Ike lost the Paris summit. Some trade.

Chapter 11 opens Part IV in Moscow and argues that Khrushchev was a true believer who staked his program on a rocket force that was mostly stage set. He called an extraordinary party congress for January 1959 to adopt a Seven Year Plan toward full communism, and Sputnik became the proof. He sneered that the Americans were planning a satellite of eleven kilograms, while Sputnik II weighed 508, and told the congress that ICBMs were coming off the line "like sausages." McDougall calls the missile gap a myth, since the USSR trailed in every meaningful category of missile technology. The bluff still worked on allies. In June 1955 only 6 percent of West Europeans thought the West militarily weaker. By 1960 Britons judged the Soviets stronger by 59 to 15 percent. Sausages sell.

The story there is the Nedelin disaster. In September 1960 Khrushchev sailed into New York with miniature spaceships in his luggage, presumably to celebrate a Mars shot. Mars rockets fizzled on October 10 and 14. On the evening of the twenty-third a third failed to ignite, and Marshal Nedelin, commander of the new Strategic Rocket Forces, ordered technicians out of their blockhouses to look at it. It exploded. Estimates of the dead run from scores to hundreds. Moscow announced that Nedelin had died in a plane crash, and a 1979 biography upgraded that to dying "tragically in the performance of his official duties," which is accurate if you squint. On April 12, 1961, Gagarin orbited the earth and came down near the towns of Marx and Engels. McDougall calls it "A very appropriate spot."

Chapter 12 says it in six words: "For it was all a bluff." While Khrushchev mocked massive retaliation, the USSR had yet to deploy a single ICBM. The SS-6 that lofted Sputnik was huge because Soviet rocketry was primitive, a cluster of small engines with radio guidance and a range of about 4,340 miles. By 1958 the Kremlin decided against deploying it, and about thirty-five SS-6s went into space shots, which bought more headlines than a baby missile force could buy security. The bluff had costs. The Americans did not blink over Berlin, Mao took the boasts at face value and asked why Moscow was so timid, and space cooperation stalled because it meant "both giving away our strength and revealing our weakness." The scene is Khrushchev's film from Powers's U-2, shown to prove the plane had found no missile bases: airfields and factories, no ICBMs. McDougall's deadpan note is that "there were none to photograph." The real danger to the bluff was the spy satellite, and the Soviet chorus against it droned on at the UN until 1963.

Seminar pro tip: get the missile numbers straight, because they scramble easily. The CIA's worst case was 100 Soviet ICBMs by 1960 and 400 in 1961. The first confirmed count of deployed Soviet ICBMs is 150, in 1965. By late 1962 the Americans led roughly 194 to 72. Cite the 400 as a count and someone will correct you with the 150 all the way to lunch. The counterargument to expect is that the bluff worked anyway, since it moved British opinion 59 to 15. Answer with the clock. McDougall gives the bluff seven years, from Sputnik to the aftermath of the Cuban crisis, and it held only as long as nobody could look.

If the bluff survived only while the Soviet Union stayed closed, did American reconnaissance do more to end the missile gap than any American missile? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 13 to 15

Moscow Fizzles, Washington Picks the Moon and Skips the Math

Chapter 13 argues that Sputnik handed the Kremlin a crisis of success. If Sputnik was a technological Pearl Harbor, the Soviets, like the Japanese, "found they had awakened a sleeping giant." Khrushchev then turned the ICBM on his own army. He sacked Marshal Zhukov in October 1957, and in January 1960 he justified a one-third cut in the standing army by telling the Supreme Soviet that almost the entire air force was being replaced with rockets. That was false, and it made enemies of nearly every branch of the military, which is a lot of enemies for a man who needs the military. Within a year Khrushchev retreated, the defense budget shot up 29 percent, and the army cuts were restored. Then came the irony. Marshal Sokolovsky's Military Strategy appeared in the summer of 1962, just as the Soviets were finally ready to deploy ICBMs, and they discovered that "it was the space program that represented a bluff." They were at least five years behind in the military satellites a real missile force needs.

The thing to remember is a satellite with a deliberately boring name. On April 6, 1962, a Soviet satellite went up from Tyuratam, orbited for three days, and parachuted back inside the USSR with film. It was "labeled, blankly, Kosmos IV." The country that had spent years calling American spy satellites illegal now had its own, and by 1965 it was launching more than twice as many. In December 1963 the Soviets ended their three-year tirade, and the old RAND hope of open skies came true, by mutual hypocrisy.

Chapter 14 and the Part IV conclusion argue that Khrushchev staked his rule on the scientific-technical revolution, and it ate him. Soviet R&D grew 17 to 18 percent a year through 1960. The politics were a disaster. Khrushchev alienated the army, heavy industry, the ideologues, the KGB, the provincial Party, and even the scientists, who resented his patronage of Lysenko. By October 1964, Medvedev wrote, not a single power group of any size would have backed him. McDougall's summary: "So Space Age communism fizzled like a capitalist rocket." The worse things went, the more he leaned on spectaculars. Titov flew a full day in August 1961 and called Khrushchev the cosmonauts' "space father," and a week later Soviet troops sealed off East Berlin.

The story is Voskhod I. With the two-man Gemini sketched out in America, Khrushchev ordered a three-man capsule launched before the next anniversary of the revolution. The only hardware was the one-man Vostok, so Korolev's team stripped it and crammed in three seats. There was no room for space suits, which was advertised as proof of routine spaceflight. Safety systems were dropped for weight, which was advertised as proof of reliable rockets. Test monkeys died in the impact, a heavier parachute went in, and the cosmonauts dieted like wrestlers making weight. Voskhod I flew on October 12, 1964, and Pravda headlined "Sorry Apollo!" On October 13, Khrushchev was relieved of all his Party posts. The capsule outlasted its sponsor.

Chapter 15 opens Part V at Locke-Ober's in Boston, where the maître d' introduced Jack and Bobby Kennedy to MIT's Charles Stark Draper and the brothers dismissed rocketry as a waste of money. On May 25, 1961, President Kennedy asked Congress to land a man on the moon before the decade was out. McDougall's argument is that the Kennedy men thought of space "in terms of ends" rather than means. Jerome Wiesner's panel warned against a prestige race for man in space, so Kennedy made Wiesner his science adviser and set the report aside. Then Gagarin flew, and Kennedy asked, "Is there any place we can catch them?" NASA estimated at least $11.4 billion extra over ten years, and the final message asked for upward of $20 billion. The man who had to run it, James Webb, had axed early ICBM and satellite programs as Truman's Budget Director and told Hugh Dryden the job was not for him. Dryden agreed. Webb took it because he had never said no to a President, and he later reminded Johnson that LBJ had "to almost drive me" to recommend the moon.

Opinion: Connection time, and Korea sits under all three chapters. Zhang argues that Stalin's death in March 1953, not air pressure, broke the armistice deadlock. McDougall reports that Soviet scholars dated the American scientific-technical revolution from that same year, because the Korean War had locked in large-scale government R&D. So the war left the United States a permanent research machine, and Khrushchev spent a decade trying to out-build it with a system that could not take the strain. Then Gaddis supplies the yardstick. He defines strategy as relating ends to means, and he notes that Truman's people believed resources were limited. Khrushchev ran out of means. Kennedy's team picked the end first and assumed the economy would grow to cover it. One of those habits got a man fired the day after his rocket landed.

If the moon decision settled the end and never seriously asked about the means, was it a strategy at all? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 16 to 18

The Gap Closed, the Checkbook Didn't

Chapter 16 charges that Kennedy's men, sure of their own brilliance and eager to look nothing like Eisenhower, made decisions in their first four months that set the space program for a decade and the strategic posture for more than two. A December 1960 Weapons Systems Evaluation Group study called for about forty Polaris submarines and about 900 Minutemen. SAC asked for 10,000 ICBMs, which at least showed ambition. McNamara told Kennedy that 950 Minutemen was the lowest he could take to Congress and "not get murdered." The country got 41 Polaris boats and exactly 1,000 Minutemen. The "no-cities" counterforce strategy McNamara unveiled at Ann Arbor in June 1962 was going obsolete by late 1963, and its replacement, assured destruction, needed no new missiles. The whiz kids then decided space was "just a place," blessed passive satellites as stabilizing, and branded orbital weapons destabilizing. Dyna-Soar died in December 1963 after seven years and $400 million, and the Manned Orbiting Laboratory was announced the next day.

The thing to remember is how the missile gap died. On February 6, 1961, McNamara admitted at an off-the-record briefing that each side seemed to have about the same small number of missiles, and Republicans sarcastically congratulated Kennedy on closing the gap in eighteen days, the fastest defense program in history. Then the cameras went up. In June the CIA's estimate of Soviet missiles dropped, without explanation, from 120 to 60. Discoverer 25 flew on June 16, 1961, missed its recovery area, and plunged into the sea, and navy frogmen fished out the film. The count fell to at most fourteen. McDougall's line: "The first satellite sparked the missile gap; the first reconnaissance satellites snuffed it out." The buildup went ahead anyway, and in 1967 McNamara conceded the force was more than the country needed.

Chapter 17 argues that American space diplomacy in the 1960s preached sharing and practiced self-interest, and that this was mostly the right call. McDougall calls it "benign hypocrisy": cooperation in science, competition in engineering, agreements where the United States was safely ahead. McGeorge Bundy imposed a blackout on spy satellites in January 1961, and the Air Force was forbidden even to mention satellite observation in public. Moscow and foreign experts already knew. "Only the American public was being fooled." The Communications Satellite Act needed the Senate's first cloture vote since 1927, and by July 1964 INTELSAT gave the Comsat Corporation 61 percent, the Europeans 30.5 percent, and the United States a veto. Sharing, with a veto.

Its story is Telstar. In July 1962 a satellite built by AT&T and launched by NASA, thirty-five pounds and three feet across, sent the first live television between continents. It was the first satellite built with private money, and a British band named a hit record after it. Six months later Telstar was dead, knocked out by radiation from American high-altitude nuclear tests. The symbol of space uniting mankind was taken out by its own country's bombs.

Chapter 18 argues that James Webb turned Apollo from a race with the Russians into a model for running the country. NASA grew a crescent of installations along the Gulf from Houston to Cape Canaveral, built while Texans and Oklahomans ran the committees that paid for them. Webb spread the money on purpose: 90 percent of NASA's funds went to private firms, and grants went to second-rank universities to grow expertise in more regions and please more congressmen. By FY 1964 NASA's budget passed $5 billion, five times what it had been three years before. The scene is how the Manned Spacecraft Center found Texas. On May 23, 1961, two days before the moon speech, Webb wrote Lyndon Johnson about the message and then got down to business: Congressman Albert Thomas, who ran Appropriations, was very interested in Rice University's 3,800 acres. Keith Glennan, pitching Ohio, laughed that 25 states were doing the same and offered to "lay you a year's salary" the center was going to Houston. Nobody took the bet, which shows good judgment. On the command module, Martin won the evaluation and North American got the contract, because, a lobbyist explained, five times as many people were happy. "That's democracy in its finest form."

Opinion: here is the lesson learned, and it holds for any commander. Evidence does not cancel a program once the program has a constituency. The satellites proved by late 1961 that there was no gap, and Washington built 1,000 Minutemen. The spy-satellite blackout fooled nobody abroad and kept the voters from learning what the government knew. Webb's contracts went to so many districts that the moon program could survive any argument about its purpose. So the moment to ask what would change the buy is before the first contract is signed, while the answer can still matter. Afterward, the answer arrives on film fished out of the sea and changes nothing. Even von Braun had to remind Webb that "space is bigger than Texas."

If the satellites proved by late 1961 that there was no gap, was the 1,000-Minuteman force a hedge against surprise or a purchase that made the Soviet buildup inevitable? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 19 to 21

Everyone Hated Apollo, So Everyone Funded It

Chapter 19, with the Part V conclusion folded in, argues that the great Apollo debate of 1962 to 1964 was loud and settled nothing important, because nearly every side had already accepted technocracy. Scientists complained: one straw poll found non-NASA scientists opposed to Apollo by 110 to 3. Eisenhower asked why the great hurry, and in June 1963 called spending $40 billion to reach the moon "just nuts." Goldwater wanted the money moved to military space, and liberals like the sociologist Amitai Etzioni wanted it moved to social programs. Everyone wanted the money spent; they disagreed only about what it should buy. McDougall's verdict is that the danger was never rule by technicians. It was "a technocracy of politicians," setting a national agenda and ordering up the hardware and management to deliver it. For Eisenhower, McDougall writes, courage meant resisting dangerous tools, and for Johnson it meant picking them up in a good cause, in cities, in space, and in Third World jungles. Civilian space spending peaked in FY 1965, and as LBJ put it, some people's feet got tired.

The scene to keep is Kennedy's offer to go to the moon together. Kennedy proposed at the UN on September 20, 1963, that the two countries do the big things together. Moscow did not answer. A month later Khrushchev said the Soviets were not planning a lunar flight, quoted a joke about people who cannot bear earth flying to the moon, and added, "But we are all right on earth." The offer worked anyway, just on Congress. The Senate kept its half-billion-dollar cut, which slipped the first landing a year, but beat Fulbright's further 10 percent cut 36 to 49 and passed a rider forbidding a joint lunar landing without the consent of Congress. Offering to share the moon persuaded the Senate to keep it. Nine days after the rider passed, Kennedy was dead.

Chapter 20 opens Part VI with Apollo 8 circling the moon on Christmas Eve, 1968, and names two ironies. The means, technocracy, came to matter more than the end, though it would fail on most of the new national agenda, since going to the moon was an engineering problem and ending poverty was not. And the moon was never what space was about. Space was about spy satellites, comsats, and national advantage, and the rules were written to keep it that way. The Outer Space Treaty, ratified 88 to 0 on April 25, 1967, denuclearized space and demilitarized the moon but left orbit open to military use. Even before the first landing, McDougall writes, "the American hare had stopped again to take a nap." The tortoises kept going: in 1973 the USSR launched 124 spacecraft to 23 American.

The scene is the Senate hearing. Ambassador Arthur Goldberg, jet-lagged from an eleven-day Asian tour, tried to explain what the treaty meant by space for the benefit of all countries and produced a sentence the transcript had to flag twice with [sic]. Senator Gore: "I can almost use the word fuzzy." Then General Earle Wheeler said the Joint Chiefs backed the treaty but it required intensified efforts to detect orbiting nuclear weapons. Senator John S. Cooper asked if that meant more money for military space R&D. "That is correct, Senator." A peace treaty whose top military witness asked for a raise passed without a single no vote.

Chapter 21 is the indictment. The government spent $146 billion on R&D from Sputnik to the moon landing with no national plan. Wolfgang Panofsky retired the spin-off argument: "If you want the by-product, you should develop the by-product." The systems theorist C. West Churchman offered a test: a system's real objective is whatever its managers will knowingly sacrifice other goals to achieve. American technocracy promised guns and butter, so McDougall concludes that "The objective of American technocracy was to avoid defining its objective." Every system needs something outside itself to say its goals are worth pursuing, a Guarantor of Decisions, or G.O.D., and analysis cannot supply one. The image to keep is his chess game by mail, where you write your orders twelve moves ahead while players on bigger boards decide knights will not be funded next year and players on still bigger boards switch everyone to parcheesi. Anyone who has survived a budget cycle knows the board.

Opinion: the author's move to steal is irony as argument. McDougall rarely tells you a policy failed. He shows it producing the opposite of its intent and lets you notice. Offering to share the moon locked it in as an American project. A treaty sold as peace came with a request for more military R&D. Apollo's real legacy was the method, which then flunked poverty. Each irony does the work of a page of polemic, and it is harder to rebut, because the critic has to argue with the record instead of with McDougall. Find where a decision produced its own opposite and the argument mostly writes itself.

By Churchman's test, has the United States ever had a real objective for airpower or space since Sputnik, and what did it knowingly sacrifice to get it? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, conclusion

The Orphan with the Gun

McDougall ends the book by stepping out of the archives and into philosophy, and he warns us he is doing it, which is more notice than most authors give. His thesis, stated one last time: the rivalry that began with the Communist victory of 1917 and peaked with Sputnik caused a leap in the politics of technology, turning the state into the main promoter and manager of technical progress. He borrows Alexander Gerschenkron's rule that the more backward a country, the more the state must force change, and adds the twist that in an age of perpetual innovation every country is permanently backward. So wartime methods became normal, peacetime values were suspended, and the line between state and society blurred. Nations justify the pace by security, yet that progress may erode the very values that make a society worth defending.

Then he surveys how people tried to make sense of it. One camp surrendered to the machines. Dean Wooldridge, whose firm did systems integration on the Atlas, argued that man is essentially a complex machine, and C. West Churchman concluded that the real question was "how to design a god." The atheist Soviets said space probes were the last nails in God's coffin. Liberal clergy like Bishop James Pike traded preaching for counseling, which McDougall likens to the Wizard of Oz: pull back the curtain and there stands the same old social engineer. The orthodox, from Pius XII to Wernher von Braun, called technology a neutral tool, and von Braun thought the Ten Commandments adequate without amendments. McDougall dismisses the first, finds the second has stripped religion of what makes it religion, and calls the third naive, because technology has a logic of its own and pulls attention away from love, death, and justice. Nobody gets out of the chapter unbruised.

His sharpest move is to deflate human power. Mount St. Helens erupted with the force of hundreds of megatons. Migrating plovers navigate from arctic to antarctic and back more precisely than a Minuteman, without a single contractor. The nearest planet that might sustain life is at least five light-years away, so a moon voyage is a toe poked onto our own front porch. Oppenheimer thought he had become Death, the destroyer of worlds, and McDougall says he was no such thing. The enlightened view had it backward, with pride in our works and contempt for ourselves as stardust. McDougall flips it: spaceflight "will teach us to have contempt for our works and value for ourselves." That, he says, is true humility.

The image to keep is the one that names the chapter. We stand with our technology, McDougall writes, like Dylan's orphan with his gun, fatherless and dangerous, about as impressive before the cosmos as a fire in the sun. He predicts science will reach hard limits. Uhuru, a modest satellite launched in 1970, found 161 X-ray sources, and physicists will keep splitting particles until there is nothing left to split. Then science may become a branch of moral philosophy again, and the orthodox message becomes a guide: God made us, is disappointed in us, but loves us anyway. "Technology is our subcreation." We will be disappointed in it too, and we must love it anyway. Frankenstein's creature, he notes, ran amok only after people turned on it in hatred and fear. The prescription is to avoid both mellonolatry, worship of the future, and misoneism, hatred of change, two words that will send most readers to a dictionary and most text-to-speech apps into a stammer. We must compete for power without making power an end in itself, and "pursue knowledge without mistaking knowledge for truth." For a book about rockets, it ends on Dante and Plato.

Opinion: the lesson learned for a commander is to confess the limits of the kit out loud and early. Seminar has already met the opposite creed. In Crane's Korea, FEAF's General Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations," and the war's ambiguous ending let the Air Force think it had succeeded and go back to planning for general war, a bill it paid in Vietnam. McDougall's advice runs the other way: admit the mortality of our works without turning on them or on ourselves. A service that loves its machines too much stops asking what they cannot do, and a service that hates them stops buying what it needs. The middle path is to value the people who use the kit above the kit, and to say so before the war, when it is cheap.

McDougall says the security-driven race for progress may erode the values that make a society worth defending; is that a real risk for the airpower enterprise, or a luxury no Cold War service could afford? (S)

McDougall: seminar questions (from the syllabus)

  • How did the Soviet Union seemingly get ahead of the United States in space technology?

    A big, primitive booster plus a bluff: "For it was all a bluff" (249); no deployed ICBMs at first, 150 confirmed in 1965, U.S. ahead about 194 to 72 in late 1962 (251–252). Eisenhower chose Vanguard to protect the spy satellite and freedom of space, with dummy upper stages on the Jupiter-C (121–124).

  • Eisenhower was good at propaganda. Why didn't he counter effectively?

    His best evidence, the U-2 photographs, was classified (219–220), he publicized a test that failed four feet up (151–154), and his message, "What you want is enough," was strategy rather than reassurance (219–220).

  • What are the capabilities and limitations of propaganda?

    It moved allies, 59 to 15 in Britain by 1960 (239–240), and it ended when reconnaissance could look: the CIA count fell to at most fourteen after 1961 (329–330). McDougall gives the bluff seven years (261).

  • Did the Kennedy administration fight the Space Race more effectively?

    More popularly, 77 percent backing Apollo in October 1964 (399–400). More expensively: ends first (303–308), 1,000 Minutemen after the gap was disproved (327–330). Argue it either way, with numbers.

  • What impact did the Space Race have on military technological and operational development?

    The military won bigger budgets and lost control of its own R&D and deployment (335–336); Dyna-Soar died after seven years and $400 million (341); passive satellites were blessed and space weapons branded destabilizing (335–338); reconnaissance became the main military use of space (194, 413–416).

McDougall: chapter recall

  • What does McDougall call the leap in state-funded R&D in the ten years up to 1965, and what happened to Western R&D budgets?

    A "saltation," an evolutionary leap in the state's relation to new knowledge, sparked by Sputnik; Western governments quintupled R&D funding for national goals. M 5–6

  • What was a sharaga, and what does McDougall conclude about its effect on Soviet aviation and rocketry?

    A prison design bureau where arrested engineers like Tupolev and Korolev kept working under guard; McDougall concludes it was no catastrophe, since the prisoners kept producing military designs such as the Katyusha. M 37–40

  • According to McDougall's Part I conclusion, what slowed Soviet rocketry in the 1930s and 1940s, and what put it back on schedule?

    War, not terror, slowed it; the German inheritance after 1945 put the Soviets back on schedule. M 65

  • What did Eisenhower admit to the Finletter Commission in 1947?

    That there was no war plan; the new global power had no operative plan for war with the Soviet Union, and only a dozen or so atomic warheads. M 93–94

  • What did RAND's October 1950 satellite report recommend for the first American satellite, and why?

    An "experimental" satellite on an equatorial orbit that never crossed Soviet territory, to establish "freedom of space" before reconnaissance satellites flew over the USSR. It went to the Air Force on October 4, 1950, seven years to the day before Sputnik 1. M 109–110

  • Why did the Eisenhower administration pick Vanguard over von Braun's Redstone for the first U.S. satellite?

    Protecting the secret reconnaissance satellite program and establishing "freedom of space" mattered more than being first, and a civilian-looking science rocket was less likely to draw a Soviet legal challenge than a military missile built by the Peenemünde team. M 121–124

  • What happened to Vanguard TV-3 on December 6, 1957?

    Billed by the White House as a real satellite attempt, it rose about four feet, exploded in flame, and fell back, leaving its little satellite chirping on the ground; the press called it Kaputnik and Flopnik. M 151–154

  • How did the 1958 Space Act divide American space activity?

    It created a civilian NASA for open, scientific space work and left military applications to the Defense Department, two parallel programs, aiming to keep the U.S. "a leader, not the leader" in space. M 171–172

  • What did NSC 5814/1 decide about missiles and the definition of outer space?

    It treated missile policy separately from space, so a UN space deal would not freeze missiles, and it avoided a fixed boundary, favoring a functional definition (anything in orbit is in space). M 179–180

  • How did NASA acquire the von Braun rocket team?

    After the Pentagon gave military space operations to the Air Force in September 1959 and the Army's Saturn ran into funding trouble, the ABMA team was transferred to NASA, effective July 1, 1960, and became the Marshall Space Flight Center. M 197–198

  • What did Eisenhower do about Apollo and Saint after the 1960 election?

    Faced with a panel estimate of an extra $26 to $58 billion for a lunar landing, he refused to "hock his jewels" and vetoed Apollo, but he approved development and testing of the Saint satellite interceptor. M 225–228

  • What was the Nedelin disaster and how did Moscow explain it?

    On October 23, 1960, a Mars-bound rocket exploded on the pad after Marshal Nedelin ordered technicians to inspect it, killing scores to hundreds; Moscow said Nedelin died in a plane crash. M 241–244

  • How many ICBMs had the Soviets actually deployed during Khrushchev's missile bluff, and what did they do with the SS-6s instead?

    None at first; the first confirmed total was 150 in 1965, while about thirty-five SS-6s went into space launches, which had more publicity value than a small missile force had military value. M 249–252

  • What did Sokolovsky's 1962 Military Strategy reveal about the Soviet space program, and what was Kosmos IV?

    Just as the Soviets were ready to deploy ICBMs, they found the space program itself was the bluff, at least five years behind in military satellites; Kosmos IV, launched April 6, 1962, was their first recoverable spy satellite. M 269–272

  • What was Voskhod I, and what happened on the day after it launched?

    A one-man Vostok stripped and crammed with three cosmonauts without space suits, launched October 12, 1964, to beat the two-man Gemini; on October 13 Khrushchev was relieved of all his posts. M 291–294

  • Why did James Webb insist that NASA be asked to go to the moon rather than propose it himself?

    He feared that if NASA appeared to initiate an expensive, risky project it would be abandoned when the mood changed, so he wanted the nation, through Johnson and Kennedy, to give NASA the task; he later wrote that LBJ had to almost drive him to recommend it. M 319–320

  • What finally killed the "missile gap," and what did the U.S. build anyway?

    Reconnaissance satellites (Samos 2 and Discoverer 25 in 1961) cut the Soviet count to at most fourteen missiles, yet the Kennedy team still built 41 Polaris boats and exactly 1,000 Minutemen. M 327–330

  • What does McDougall mean by "benign hypocrisy" in U.S. space diplomacy?

    The U.S. preached space for all mankind but cooperated in science while guarding engineering know-how, signing agreements only where it was safely ahead; McDougall judges this self-interest necessary. M 345–346

  • What did James Webb think NASA was ultimately for, beyond beating the Soviets to the moon?

    A model of government-industry-university management for social change: spreading R&D money across regions and universities to drive growth, education, and regional uplift. M 381–386

  • What happened to Kennedy's September 1963 UN offer of a joint moon mission?

    Moscow ignored it and Khrushchev said the USSR was not planning a lunar flight; in Washington the Senate defeated a further 10 percent NASA cut 36 to 49 and passed a rider barring a joint landing without congressional consent. M 395–396

  • What did the 1967 Outer Space Treaty ban, and what did it leave alone?

    It banned weapons of mass destruction in orbit and all weapons on the moon, but it left orbital space open to military use; the Senate ratified it 88 to 0 on April 25, 1967. M 417–419

  • What is McDougall's "G.O.D.," and why can't systems analysis provide one?

    A Guarantor of Decisions (or Destiny): something outside a system that confirms its goals are worth pursuing. Analysis can optimize toward a goal but cannot justify the goal, and American technocracy avoided defining its objective at all. M 447–448

  • How does McDougall flip the "enlightened" lesson of astronomy and spaceflight?

    The enlightened view took pride in man's works and held man himself to be insignificant dust; McDougall predicts spaceflight will teach the reverse, contempt for our works and value for ourselves, which he calls true humility. M 455–458

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
