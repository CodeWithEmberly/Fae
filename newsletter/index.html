Today's Seminar · Clodfelter, Tuesday, November 3, 1200

Same Bombs, Different War: Clodfelter Comes to Seminar

It's Election Day, so the whole country will spend tonight arguing about what the results mean. Seminar gets a head start at noon. Mark Clodfelter's The Limits of Air Power is a book about reading results, specifically the eleven days in December 1972 that convinced a generation of airmen they had found the answer they'd been looking for since 1965. The syllabus frames it bluntly: the book took on the claim that less restricted bombing, earlier, would have won the war, and its view, a minority one when the book came out in 1989, is now widely held among airmen. Minority views have a way of becoming the reading list.

The argument fits on an index card. The only true test of an air campaign, Clodfelter says, is what it contributes to the nation's war aims, so he grades Rolling Thunder, Linebacker I, and Linebacker II against the aims each one served. Some aims could be reached only by applying force; he calls them positive. Others could be reached only by limiting it, such as Johnson's goal of keeping China and the Soviets out; he calls them negative, and political controls flow straight from them. Bombing "worked" in 1972, he argues, because Nixon's goals were narrow, Moscow and Peking had given him a free hand, and Hanoi had chosen, for the first time since 1965, to fight a sustained conventional war that ran on supplies. That combination did not exist until 1972. Nixon, who told Meet the Press in 1988 that not bombing in 1969 was his greatest mistake, is the first witness Clodfelter cross-examines. He's not the last.

Bring three numbers. Communist forces in the South needed about 34 tons a day from outside before Tet, which seven trucks could carry. American aircraft dropped 643,000 tons on the North from 1965 to 1968 and 2.2 million on the South. And Linebacker I dropped 155,548 tons, a quarter of Rolling Thunder's total, yet cut overland imports from 160,000 to 30,000 tons a month. Small bomb loads, big results, because the enemy was finally eating what the bombers could reach.

The syllabus asks whether technology as a mere backdrop is a feature or an oversight of the thesis. Opinion: mostly a feature, though one worth defending out loud. Clodfelter does count the laser-guided bombs, three for three on the Son Tay warehouses, and the epilogue lists them, along with good flying weather, as helps in 1972. He just refuses to let them carry the argument, since the same doctrine and many of the same targets failed in 1965. A precise bomb on the wrong war is still on the wrong war. Expect someone to push back with the Paul Doumer Bridge, and have your answer ready.

Bring to seminar:

  1. How did airmen tie airpower to political objectives, and where did Johnson's negative objectives cut the cord?
  2. Given their druthers, would the air chiefs have erased every limit? (Clodfelter says their doctrine and their consciences would have kept Hanoi standing anyway.)
  3. Was Nixon and Kissinger's bombing as fruitful as they claimed, given that the January agreement differed from October's only cosmetically?
  4. What did it cost to fight a gradual war with an air force built for massive retaliation?

Then the web. Crane showed the Air Force calling Korea an aberration and going back to planning for general war; Clodfelter quotes the same word. Schlight, yesterday, showed the Air Force in the South counting sorties instead of results. Clodfelter shows the same habit in the North, plus a sequel: after Linebacker II, the service graded its own homework and gave itself an A. If anyone asks which book on the shelf best explains why that keeps happening, the answer is the one on the table today. (S)

Fifteen Hours From England: Clodfelter Reads a Russian Move on NATO

The prompt asks for the single most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare for it, in ten pages, by December 1. Yesterday we handed Clodfelter the Iran file. Today we rotate to another of the syllabus's examples, a Russian invasion of a NATO member. What follows is our extrapolation from his arguments about North Vietnam, which he wrote in 1989, and it isn't Shaun's answer.

The news supplies the geography. In Breaking Defense on Monday, John Deni of the Army War College, writing in a personal capacity, warned about the costs of pulling U.S. troops out of Europe. About 80,000 are there now, against nearly half a million at the Cold War's height, and a Pentagon posture review was expected to finish in early November with major cuts reportedly under consideration. His airpower math is the part to keep. A round-trip bomber mission from the United States to Iran takes about 36 hours, he writes, against as little as 15 from Britain, which saves roughly 2.05 million dollars per B-1 sortie at 97,600 dollars an hour. By early April, the U.S. had flown 62 bombing missions against Iran, 18 of them from North America. Bases, it turns out, are a weapon system with a lease.

Meanwhile Defense News reported Monday that Russia is fitting its drones and missiles with bigger satellite-navigation antennas to beat Ukrainian jamming. Ukrainian military intelligence says Russia already uses 16-element arrays at scale, is starting serial production of 20-element arrays, and that Russian firms ordered more than 100,000 Kometa navigation modules this year. A Zelenskyy adviser, Serhii Beskrestnov, put the race simply: it is easier for the enemy to add elements than for Ukraine to add jamming sites.

Now Clodfelter's lens, and from here it's opinion. His first question would be the list of objectives. Restoring an ally's territory is a positive aim. Avoiding a nuclear exchange with the invader is the negative aim that will shape everything else, and in this crisis the nuclear power isn't a patron standing behind the enemy, as China and the Soviets stood behind Hanoi. It's the enemy. His epilogue warns that political controls are no anomaly, since the atomic bomb made them a standard feature of modern war. An Airman who plans a Baltic campaign assuming the controls will come off is planning the 1965 Air Staff's war.

His second question is what kind of war the enemy is fighting. A Russian armored push across a border would look a lot more like the Easter Offensive than like the Viet Cong: tanks, fuel, ammunition, and roads, the kind of target Clodfelter says finally suited the doctrine in 1972. But the antenna story shows the other half of Russia's way of war, mass-produced drones and glide bombs that adapt faster than the defenses. His doctrine "chameleon" cuts both ways here. Interdiction might work against the tanks and fail against the drones in the same campaign.

His third question is operational controls, the category where he puts geography and weather. Deni's numbers put geography on the budget line. Every base given up in Europe adds hours to every sortie, and in Clodfelter's framework those hours are a control just as real as a restricted zone around Hanoi. The paper-writer's takeaway: before you say how Airmen should prepare, say which war you expect and where the airplanes will take off from. Washington can withdraw from bases faster than it can withdraw from commitments. (S)

Six Books In: Everybody Learned Something, Mostly the Wrong Thing

Six books in, the course is starting to look like a long argument about who learned what from which war. Gaddis gave us the definition: strategy is the process of relating ends to means. Crane showed Korea's ambiguous ending letting the Air Force believe it had succeeded. Zhang showed both sides inflating kill claims. McDougall's Khrushchev bluffed his way to a missile gap. Schlight's Air Force counted sorties in the South. And today Clodfelter's Air Force draws a doctrine from eleven days of 1972 and applies it, retroactively, to every year before. Every book on the shelf so far has a character who reads the scoreboard wrong. Some of them got promoted.

Start with the best new link, Gaddis and Clodfelter on flexible response. Gaddis writes that Kennedy's symmetrical strategy took its public name, "flexible response," from Maxwell Taylor's book The Uncertain Trumpet. Clodfelter shows the other side of the room. In the late 1950s, he writes, Army generals including Ridgway, Gavin, and Taylor pushed for flexible response over massive retaliation, while LeMay's Air Force put the strategic bomber on a higher pedestal, until three-fourths of the top Air Force officers in the Pentagon came from SAC. So the administration adopted a strategy of measured, graduated force at about the moment the Air Force finished building itself for the opposite. Rolling Thunder was the bill. The syllabus asks what it cost to fight gradually with a force built for massive retaliation, and those two books, read together, give you the answer.

Then Zhang and Clodfelter, linked by a ghost. When Wheeler pushed in June 1966 to mine Haiphong and promised the Chinese and Soviets would stay out, Johnson asked whether he was more sure than MacArthur had been. Zhang is the reason that question had teeth: Chinese armies did cross the Yalu, after Moscow had held back the air cover they were counting on. Johnson's negative objective was Korea's memory, written into the target list.

The news web keeps growing. Monday's rain forecast met Clodfelter's monsoon, which delayed the first Rolling Thunder strike and the 1966 oil raids. Monday's Australian undersea minelayer met Nixon's mining of Haiphong. Monday's report that the Pentagon asked the Baltic states to speak more quietly met McGeorge Bundy's 1965 warning that what you tell allies won't stay secret. Today, Bundy turns up again, in an acquisition story, and the 1964 Working Group's Options A, B, and C turn up in a CSIS paper on Iran. Sixty-year-old memos have a surprisingly good attendance record.

Opinion: the theme so far is that wars teach lessons, and the institutions keep the ones they like. Crane's Air Force kept general war. Clodfelter's kept Linebacker II. Schlight's kept the sortie count. The next three books are all Vietnam, and the open question for the week is whether the North Vietnamese, in Nguyen's account on Friday, learned anything more useful than we did. The bar is not high. Technical Writing · Week 2, Day 2 · using evidence and evaluating sources

Take the Note, Then Talk Back: Ruppo's Third Column

Tuesday is evidence day, and our guide is Irina Ruppo's A Guide to Academic Writing, which treats research as part of the writing itself. (Reading a book in bed, she notes, is lovely, but without notes it isn't research. Several SAASS students just sat up.) Her method is to spend enough time with each source to come away with three things: a full bibliographic entry, the points that bear on your paper, and your own thoughts about those points.

The third column is the one that matters. In her worked example, she records an author's claim with its page number and then writes, in effect, that she agrees with the claim but isn't sure the author proves it very well. That's source evaluation in one sentence. She adds a practical rule: if a source is too hard to read, note it and move on, since a source you can't follow is one you won't be able to use. Later in the book she diagnoses a common draft problem, a pile of evidence offered for a conclusion the writer never actually states. Facts don't argue for themselves. Somebody has to say what they prove.

Clodfelter is a working demonstration. His preface admits his evidence is not all-inclusive: many American sources were still classified, and no historian could get into the Politburo's archives, which would show most clearly what the bombing achieved. Then he argues that waiting for perfect evidence would be a mistake and tells you exactly what he can and can't conclude. That's the third column written into the book itself. And in the epilogue he evaluates his own witnesses. When LeMay said in 1986 that America could have won "in any two-week period you want to mention," Clodfelter didn't just quote him. He checked the claim against the war and found it wanting.

Before, the way a tired student writes it: "General LeMay said the war could have been won in any two-week period, which shows that political restrictions cost America the war." After, with the third column filled in: "LeMay claimed in 1986 that America could have won 'in any two-week period you want to mention,' but the claim ignores the war's nature before 1968: most Communist troops in the South were Viet Cong, who needed only about 34 tons of outside supply a day." The first sentence uses the source as a trophy. The second uses it as evidence and grades it.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: Ctrl+F your draft for "said," "notes," "argues," and "claims." Those verbs tell the reader how much you trust each source, so make sure you chose them on purpose. A general who "notes" something gets your endorsement. A general who "claims" something gets your skepticism. Opinion: in a paper about airpower's record, the postwar memoir is the most quoted and least checked source on the shelf. Give every one of them a third column. Some of them will need two. (S)

Don't Confuse Their Losing With Your Bombing

On the eve of the midterms, electoral-vote.com led with a warning to the party the polls favor, and the warning is about reading results. Democrats, (V) wrote, are nervous precisely because the news looks good: Texas, South Carolina, and Kansas supposedly in play, and a lingering fear that the polls are badly off. The headline quote came from Morgan Jackson, a senior adviser to North Carolina's Roy Cooper: "Don't confuse them losing with us winning." Jackson's point, as EV relays it, is that a big night would reflect Republicans staying home, independents breaking one way, and high gas prices, while the party's brand remains toxic in many places.

Others in the item agreed. An anonymous veteran Democratic pollster called a win "not an affirmation" of the party. Lis Smith, a strategist from Pete Buttigieg's 2020 campaign, noted that candidates winning deep-red seats are doing it by distancing themselves from the national party, an option no 2028 nominee will have, and that wave elections carry along many mediocre candidates. (V)'s own verdict: this is an anti-incumbent election more than a pro-party one, and whoever misreads it does so at their peril.

Then (V) predicted the misreading. Progressives will point to their wins as proof of their path. If the South comes through, moderates will claim it as theirs. And if both wings win, the message will be mixed, which EV expects: "cue the start of the 2028 battle on Wednesday." Every faction will find its own lesson in the same returns. The returns will not be consulted.

Now go to today's book, which is about exactly this. After Linebacker II, Clodfelter writes, many air chiefs treated the whole war as a single thing, with the same nature and the same objectives from 1965 to 1972, and concluded that only political controls had kept bombing from working. LeMay said America could have won in any two-week period. An August 1986 Air Force magazine article blamed "timorous military amateurs" in Washington. Clodfelter's reply is that most air commanders failed to see that the Eleven-Day War "was a unique campaign for very limited ends," one that rode on the first Linebacker, on Nixon and Kissinger's diplomacy, and on Hanoi's fear for an army it insisted on using conventionally. The Air Force drew the lesson it already believed and left the doctrine alone.

Opinion: Jackson's line is Clodfelter's thesis in campaign language. A result tells you what worked against that opponent, in those conditions, at that moment. It doesn't tell you that your doctrine, or your platform, was right all along. We'd extend the warning to whoever wins anything tonight, in either party, since winners are the people least motivated to check their assumptions. (Losers will over-learn instead, which is a different problem and will fill a different book.)

We'd note one difference. EV's strategists are warning about the misreading before the votes are counted. The Air Force waited until after the war and, as of Clodfelter's writing in 1989, still hadn't stopped. (S)

Acquisition × Clodfelter · via Breaking Defense, November 2

The Army Will Concur, We Think: The Robot Industry Asks for a Signal

The Army wants ground robots at scale, and the companies that would build them would like to know what, exactly, it wants. Carley Welch of Breaking Defense interviewed Warren Sponsler, chief operating officer of the National Advanced Mobility Consortium, a nonprofit that links the Pentagon and industry on ground-drone projects. His complaint is "the challenge of understanding what the demand signal is." Companies, he says, "want to know what's next," and some order parts and strain their supply chains before the Army has settled on a contract mechanism or even a capability.

The org chart explains the anxiety. The Army turned its Robotic Combat Vehicle program into the Unmanned Ground Commercial Robotic Vehicle, then dispersed that into Project Sustainment and an Engineer Autonomous Breaching Capability program, which picked four companies in July. Within about 18 months, Breaking Defense reports, the Army changed its unmanned-systems program of record, moved its autonomy acquisition office, and created a separate portfolio acquisition executive for autonomy. Futures and Concepts Command became the Army Futures and Autonomous Systems Command, and the Pentagon has announced an Autonomous Warfare Command that still awaits congressional approval. That is a lot of letterhead for vehicles that mostly exist in demonstrations.

And there are plenty of demonstrations. Sponsler says the Army invites industry to so many that small firms must choose which to attend: "There are so many opportunities right now." He'd like fewer, tied to contracts, the way the Army's xTech competitions link demonstrations to awards. Industry, in short, wants a path to revenue. Sponsler is upbeat about the new commands and thinks they could cut duplication. We're told reorganizations are often described this way at the start.

Now go to today's book, where the most famous demand-signal problem in airpower history comes with a pencil. On February 16, 1965, McGeorge Bundy drafted a message saying the President "has concurred in" sustained bombing of the North. Johnson changed it to say the advisers thought the President "will concur." Bundy wrote back the same day, puzzled, and reported that while Johnson was out of the room, McNamara had said over and over that "he simply has to know what the policy is so that he can make his military plans and give his military orders." Bundy wanted an "absolutely firm and clear internal decision." He got a White Paper that William Bundy later called a lousy job, and a policy that was, in his words, declaring itself through actions.

Opinion: Johnson's vagueness at least had a purpose. Clodfelter shows he wanted no loud signal to Hanoi, Moscow, or the domestic audience he was protecting for the Great Society. The Army's vagueness about ground robots has no enemy audience to manage; it's just churn. The lesson from 1965 still applies, though: when the people who must execute can't tell whether a decision has been made, they hedge, and hedging is expensive. McNamara's planners hedged with sortie rates. Sponsler's companies hedge with inventory.

In 1965 the policy declared itself through actions. In 2026 it declares itself through reorganizations, which are also actions, technically. (S)

Leadership × Clodfelter · via Reuters in Defense News, November 2

Delivery Expected Sometime After the Next War: Patriots, Promises, and Thieu

The State Department has approved a possible sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 missiles to Ukraine, worth an estimated 2.1 billion dollars, Reuters reported Monday in a story Defense News carried. Canada, Germany, and Norway are paying through a U.S. program called JUMPSTART that lets allies buy American weapons for Kyiv, and Ukraine is adding money financed by an EU loan. Reuters called it one of the most significant steps to supply Kyiv with defensive weapons in the current term.

The catch is that the missiles haven't been built. Two people briefed on the decision told Reuters that deliveries aren't expected until a couple of years into the 2030s, which is why Ukraine's Defense Ministry wants the Pentagon to hand over interceptors already in U.S. stocks and replace them later. Lockheed Martin, the prime contractor, plans to triple PAC-3 MSE production capacity by the end of 2030 and grow its Camden, Arkansas, workforce from about 1,200 to roughly 1,850. Zelenskiy said Ukraine had waited nearly a month just for the approval. Meanwhile, on Thursday a Russian drone strike killed at least 14 people on a crowded rail platform in Dnipro.

So the signature is real, the funding is real, and the missiles are a forecast. Reuters also notes that U.S. munitions used against Iran have raised concerns about American stockpiles of air defense and precision weapons. A promise is only as good as the inventory behind it.

Now go to today's book, where Chapter VI is titled "Persuading Enemy and Ally," and the ally took more persuading. Clodfelter writes that Nixon aimed Linebacker II at two audiences: Hanoi's will, and Thieu's doubts. Since any agreement would rest on America's air power deterrent, Nixon counted on the bombing to show continued support for Saigon. Then he sent Haig with a letter calling it his "absolutely final offer" and warning he would go ahead alone if he had to. On January 5 he wrote Thieu again and promised that the United States would "respond with full force should the settlement be violated." Clodfelter judges that Linebacker II gave that promise credibility. He also records how it ended. When Giap's army crossed the DMZ in March 1975, Nixon was no longer President, Congress precluded a military response, and the promise was never tested.

Opinion: here's the leadership lesson, and it's nonpartisan, since every administration makes these promises. An assurance to an ally has three parts: the will to act, the means to act, and the time it takes to show up. Nixon's B-52s supplied the first two in December 1972, in about as loud a form as a promise can take. What they couldn't supply was the third part, a guarantee that lasted beyond the people who made it. Kyiv's Patriot sale has the will and the money but, for now, not the missiles or the time. Leaders who reassure allies should say which of the three they're actually offering.

Thieu got a letter, eleven days of B-52s, and a promise that expired with the administration. Kyiv has a press release and a delivery date in the next decade. Both, we suspect, would have preferred the inventory. (S)

Defense & Conflict × Clodfelter · via CSIS, November 2

Option C Is Always Controllable: CSIS Serves Up the 1964 Menu for Iran

Benjamin Jensen of CSIS published a commentary Monday laying out six U.S. military options in Iran, and it reads like a briefing book with the risks left in, which is rarer than it should be. His starting point is that Washington is negotiating with a regime unlikely to make concessions easily while deploying more forces, and every option assumes the sanctions and blockade continue. (ISW's Iran Update for November 2 is also in this morning's feed; its site blocked us, so we won't guess what it says.)

The menu runs from mild to maximal. Coercive diplomacy means more forces, including another carrier strike group and tankers, while talking, which keeps Gulf allies aboard but could embolden Tehran, which has already tested the blockade with strikes on commercial shipping. A limited counter-force campaign would hit coastal and air defense radars, modeled on Israel's April 2024 strike on an S-300 radar near Isfahan. Limited strikes on high-value nuclear sites would follow the model of Operation Midnight Hammer in June 2025. A large special forces raid would try to remove enriched uranium from underground sites, which could take days and need conventional forces and constant airstrikes around it. A sea control campaign would hunt fast attack craft, drones, missiles, and mines, using up munitions that are already running low; every one spent on Iran, Jensen notes, is one fewer to deter China, Russia, or North Korea. And a counter-regime campaign, modeled on Kosovo in 1999, would face a country 151 times larger with more than 90 million people.

His bottom line: sustained pressure is the lowest-risk option, but Tehran could use the time to rearm, split allies, or launch spoiling attacks, and military success doesn't guarantee lasting political gains. That last sentence could be stitched onto a pillow at SAASS.

Now go to today's book, where the same meeting happened in November 1964. After Bien Hoa, Johnson set up a National Security Council Working Group under William Bundy, which spent three weeks producing Options A, B, and C. A continued current activity with reprisals. B was heavy bombing until Hanoi quit, which some members thought amounted to demanding "unconditional surrender," and which the group rejected for fear of China. C paired current activity with milder bombing that would stop once talks began. The group recommended C, Clodfelter writes, because it "was controllable." Even its best case was sobering: at best, the group thought, Hanoi might feign compliance and wait for another day. And Robert Johnson of the State Department added the line that ties the whole menu together: "The threat ... may be as important as execution."

Opinion: Jensen's lowest-risk option is the Working Group's Option C with sixty years of compound interest. Both rest on the hope that pressure short of all-out force will move a regime whose core goal, unification for Hanoi and regime survival for Tehran, isn't negotiable. Clodfelter's answer is that graduated pressure works only when it threatens something the enemy can't afford to lose, and that the controllable option is controllable mostly for the side that chose it. Jensen's warning that Tehran could rearm during the pause is the 1964 group's "another day," updated.

We'll give CSIS this: the 1964 Working Group buried its doubts in a memo, and Jensen put his in the headline. Progress is slow, but it is measurable. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter I

The Air Force Studied Korea and Learned There Was Nothing to Learn

Chapter I, which runs from the Air Corps Tactical School to the eve of Vietnam, argues that American airmen built a doctrine out of one war and then applied it to every war. The preface sets the frame. It opens with Richard Nixon telling Meet the Press on April 10, 1988, that his greatest mistake as President was failing to bomb and mine North Vietnam in 1969, and Clodfelter promises a Clausewitzian test instead: bombing gets judged only by what it did for war aims. His tool is the split between positive objectives, which require force, and negative objectives, which require restraint. In World War II, unconditional surrender left no negative objectives at all. Before the 3 February 1945 raid, Spaatz told Doolittle to hit oil if the weather was clear and otherwise the center of Berlin. Clouds settled it. The raid killed 25,000 people. In Japan, LeMay's fire raids hit sixty-six cities and killed 330,000 civilians. Nobody in Washington was asking for a lighter touch.

Korea broke the pattern. Truman's overriding negative objective was avoiding a world war, so China stayed a sanctuary and the north side of the Yalu bridges stayed off limits. Interdiction and armed reconnaissance made up 47.7 percent of combat sorties, and Communist defenses destroyed 1,041 FEAF aircraft. The airmen's conclusion was brisk. "Military chiefs tended to view Korea as an aberration." The decade that followed shows what happens to an aberration. America's nuclear arsenal grew from 1,750 weapons in 1954 to 26,500 in 1962. Manual 1-8, the strategic bombing bible dated 1 May 1954, went unrevised until December 1965. LeMay stopped submitting his war plans for JCS review in 1951, a real time saver, and after three years as Chief of Staff, three-fourths of the top Air Force officers in the Pentagon came straight from SAC. Even the fighters went nuclear. The F-105 was built to drop nuclear bombs and could not dogfight, and planners called that irrelevant, which is one way to handle a design flaw.

The one thing to remember is a dam. In March 1953, FEAF's target intelligence chief proposed breaching North Korea's irrigation dams to ruin the rice crop. Weyland, skeptical, approved one raid reluctantly. On 13 May, F-84s hit the Toksan dam, 20 miles north of Pyongyang. The FEAF target committee wanted some deception so the enemy would not read it as a war on rice, so planners also aimed at a rail bridge below the dam. The water then removed any doubt about the theory: five bridges and 6 miles of railroad washed out, and 27 miles of river valley flooded. Weyland later admitted his surprise that it flooded "a hell of a lot of North Korea." The Communists answered with their most intense propaganda campaign of the war. Dulles began delivering Eisenhower's atomic warning in India on 22 May, and three days later the Communist negotiators asked for a recess. On 8 June they accepted the UN position on prisoners. Clodfelter does not credit the dams alone. He pairs them with the atomic threat and the confusion after Stalin's death, and he concludes that the more air power menaced an enemy's essential concerns, the better it worked. The heart of North Korea was agriculture. Doctrine was aimed at its factories.

Opinion: Clodfelter and Crane reach the same verdict from opposite ends. Crane argued that Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded, so it went back to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam. Clodfelter shows the mechanism inside the doctrine itself. Manual 1-8 said modern nations were tightly woven webs where cutting one strand unravels the whole, and his dry gloss is the best line in the chapter: "Most air commanders equated "modern" with "all."" Then came the sermon. In May 1953 an ailing Hoyt Vandenberg told the Air War College to go out and spread the gospel, while admitting that claiming air power was all you need was bunk. His audience kept the gospel and dropped the caveat. A doctrine that removes war aims and the nature of the enemy from the equation is cheaper to teach. It is also wrong as soon as the enemy is a rice farmer.

Seminar question: if Clodfelter is right that bombing works best when it threatens what an enemy cannot afford to lose, what is left for air power in a war whose central negative objective is not threatening the enemy's survival? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter II

Everyone Agreed to Bomb North Vietnam. Nobody Agreed Why.

Chapter II, on late 1963 to July 1965, argues that Rolling Thunder was a compromise: Lyndon Johnson's advisers agreed to bomb the North without ever agreeing on what the bombing was for. The positive objective was fixed early. NSAM 288, signed 17 March 1964, called for an independent, non-Communist South Vietnam, and it stayed the goal until March 1968. The negative objectives were just as firm. Johnson feared Chinese or Soviet intervention and nuclear war, wanted to protect the Great Society, and banned actions that threatened North Vietnam's survival. The result was gradual response. The Joint Chiefs wanted a sharp, sudden blow, and in August 1964 Wheeler offered ninety-four targets in a sixteen-day assault. Johnson was not buying while Saigon kept changing governments. At a 7 September meeting he said he "did not wish to enter the patient in a 10-round bout," when the patient could not last one. After the Viet Cong hit Bien Hoa on 1 November, with the election days away, he ordered no response at all.

Then came the committee. William Bundy's Working Group spent three weeks producing Options A, B, and C. Option B, heavy bombing until Hanoi quit, struck some members as a demand for unconditional surrender, and the group rejected it for fear of China. It recommended Option C, milder bombing that would stop once talks began, chiefly because it was controllable. Clodfelter's verdict on the fall of 1964 is that the move toward bombing was a mood born of frustration more than a conviction. Each adviser wanted something different. McGeorge Bundy wrote that the real targets were in the South, in the minds of South Vietnamese and Viet Cong cadres. McNaughton saw bombing as a substitute for a competent Saigon government. Rusk wanted bargaining leverage. McNamara wanted to break Hanoi's will and talk it out of the war before its country was destroyed. The Viet Cong attack at Pleiku brought Flaming Dart on 8 and 9 February 1965, Johnson ordered Rolling Thunder on 13 February, and a coup attempt and a monsoon pushed the first strike to 2 March. Delays had already eaten so many mission numbers that the opening raid was called Rolling Thunder 5.

The one thing to remember is a pencil edit. On 16 February 1965, McGeorge Bundy drafted a message to Ambassador Taylor saying the advisers had recommended continuing air and naval action and that "the President has concurred in" it. Johnson changed it to say the advisers thought "the President will concur in" it. Bundy wrote back the same day, puzzled. While Johnson was out of the room, he reported, McNamara had said over and over that he simply had to know what the policy was so he could give his military orders. Johnson did not want a loud public signal of a policy change. The State Department's White Paper of 27 February obliged by being muddled enough that William Bundy later called it a lousy job. Clodfelter's dry point is that the lousy job did exactly what the President wanted. The policy, as Bundy put it, was declaring itself through actions, which is a convenient arrangement for anyone who might later be asked to explain it.

Seminar pro tip: expect someone to say Rolling Thunder was an uncoordinated series of fits and starts, the view of many senior officers. Clodfelter rejects that. He calls the gradual escalation a carefully orchestrated attempt to reach political goals, then adds that the orchestration lacked harmony. Those are different charges. One says nobody was conducting; the other says several people were conducting at once. Keep the dates straight too. Johnson ordered the campaign on 13 February, but the first bombs fell on 2 March, against the Xom Bay ammunition depot and the Quang Khe naval base. By 6 April, NSAM 328 had sent Marines toward offensive operations in the South, and by July McNamara wanted sorties to rise only from 2,500 a month to 4,000 while the war's weight shifted to ground troops. The independent air campaign had a run of about five weeks.

Seminar question: Clodfelter says the advisers backed bombing because each thought it would deliver his own missing ingredient for a stable South Vietnam. If a campaign has five purposes, can it ever fail, and how would anyone know? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter III

Ninety-Four Targets in Search of a War

Chapter III, on the bombing from the 1964 planning to Johnson's speech of March 31, 1968, argues that the air chiefs fought Rolling Thunder with the doctrine they brought home from World War II. That doctrine was built for general war with the Soviet Union, and North Vietnam was a guerrilla enemy with a small economy. In August 1964, Curtis LeMay's planners drew up a list of ninety-four targets, eighty-two fixed sites and twelve lines of communication, and estimated that a "severe" campaign could destroy them all in sixteen days. Johnson never bought it. Targets were chosen instead at his Tuesday White House lunches, which had no military representative until Wheeler began attending in late October 1967. Clodfelter then marches through the phases: interdiction in late 1965, oil in 1966, industry and electric power from late 1966 into May 1967, and a wavering objective until Tet. Each phase had its own vital center. The North kept fighting through all of them.

The oil campaign shows the pattern. The Joint Chiefs noted that the North had imported 170,000 metric tons of petroleum in 1965 and figured that wrecking its storage would take 416 sorties and cause forty-four civilian casualties. The raids of June 29, 1966 demolished the Hanoi site and left the Haiphong storage area looking 80 percent destroyed. General Gilbert Meyers called it the most important strike of the war. By the end of July, the Defense Intelligence Agency found 75,000 metric tons of storage left, more than enough for the North's needs. The Jason Summer Study then estimated that only 5 percent of the North's oil requirements went to truck infiltration. McNamara never again recommended intensifying the air war. The belief that bombing put a ceiling on infiltration, shared by civilians and generals alike, survived until Tet, when an estimated 70,000 troops struck thirty-six of the South's forty-four provincial capitals. Clodfelter's verdict is blunt: "air commanders had molded the war to suit their doctrine."

The scene to remember is the National Security Council meeting of June 22, 1966. Wheeler tells Johnson that an oil strike will not stop infiltration but will set another ceiling on it. Johnson asks, "Suppose your dreams are fulfilled. What are the results?" Wheeler says it will start to bite in sixty to ninety days. Johnson complains that everyone tells him what not to do and nobody offers alternatives. Wheeler offers one: mine Haiphong harbor. Will that bring in the Chinese and the Soviets? No, sir. Then the President lands the line: "Are you more sure than MacArthur was?" He ordered the raids anyway, with hand-picked crews, no attacks in marginal weather, and a ban on starting on Sunday, June 26. Weather postponed them twice, the Wall Street Journal printed the details on June 24, Johnson canceled the whole thing, appalled, and then he un-canceled it. The bombs fell on the 29th. Grand strategy, scheduled around the Sabbath and the morning paper.

Author's move. Opinion: Clodfelter's sharpest tool is catching officials as they rewrite their own objectives after the fact. In November 1966 the chiefs' Rolling Thunder 52 targeted eight power plants partly to put psychological pressure on both leaders and population. On May 5, 1967, after the strikes, Wheeler informed the President that the goal had never been to turn the lights off, only to deny power to war-supporting industry. Clodfelter pairs him with General O. P. Weyland in Korea, who described the raids on North Korean irrigation dams as interdiction to wash out rail embankments. He does the same thing to McNamara. The Secretary's report on the April 1965 Honolulu conference said everyone thought the bombing's tempo was about right, and Sharp later called that a distortion of his view. Clodfelter reads the memo as McNamara's own worry of the moment, not a consensus. The habit is worth stealing for seminar: when a commander states an objective, go find the second memo, the one written after the results came in. It is usually more modest.

The Joint Chiefs wrote Rolling Thunder's objective without civilian guidance, and Johnson picked its targets over lunch without a general at the table until late 1967. Which of those two gaps did more to doom the campaign? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter IV

"Bomb It," Said the General. Which Village?

Chapter IV, on Rolling Thunder's restraints and results from 1965 to 1968, sorts the campaign's limits into political, military, and operational controls, and then argues that the controls were only half the problem. The other half was that civilian and military leaders alike never grasped the kind of war the enemy was fighting. B-52s flew only 141 Rolling Thunder missions, most of them near the demilitarized zone. Air commanders needed presidential approval to hit anything within 30 miles of Hanoi's center, 10 miles of Haiphong's, or 30 miles of China, and Johnson halted the bombing entirely on eight occasions. The military limits were homemade: nobody commanded the air war as a whole. The Air Force turned the 2d Air Division into 7th Air Force in 1966, let 13th Air Force keep administrative control, and ended up inventing a 7/13th Air Force in Thailand. One staff officer summed it up: "Command arrangements were a mess." The Navy and Air Force split the North into route packages and competed for sortie counts. By August 1967 the North had roughly two hundred SAM sites and eighty MiGs. Losses over the North rose from 171 aircraft in 1965 to 280 in 1966 and 326 in 1967. In 1966, only 1 percent of 81,000 sorties flew against the Joint Chiefs' fixed targets, with weather a key reason.

The results ledger is grim. The 643,000 tons dropped on the North destroyed 65 percent of its oil storage, 59 percent of its power plants, and 55 percent of its major bridges. It cost the United States $6.60 to inflict a dollar of damage in 1965, and $9.60 in 1966. None of it mattered much, because Communist forces in the South fought about one day in thirty and needed only 34 tons a day from outside the South. Seven trucks could carry that, less than 1 percent of what North Vietnam imported daily. Bombing killed an estimated 52,000 civilians out of 18 million. Clodfelter's conclusion is that only two targets would have touched the North's war-making capacity, people and food, and even those would have done little while the Communists chose when and how often to fight.

The story to remember comes from Lieutenant Colonel William Greenhalgh of 7th Air Force intelligence. Late in 1966, Washington asked why nobody had attacked a missile propellant storage area near Hanoi. The intelligence officers knew of no such facility. Told it was on reconnaissance film, they searched and found nothing. They were then directed to specific coordinates on a single frame, enlarged it, and found a small village with a long storage building, with no roads, railroads, or waterways leading to it. Greenhalgh briefed General John Ryan, commander of Pacific Air Forces. Ryan replied only, "Bomb it." The first strike could not tell which village was the target and bombed its best guess. After the damage photos showed the pilots the right village, the next mission wiped it out. There was no sign of missile propellant. That same year, Johnson approved only twenty-two of the Joint Chiefs' recommended targets, so somebody's picks were getting through.

Seminar pro tip. Expect someone to quote General William Momyer's letter to McConnell: don't get in a fight unless you are prepared to do whatever it takes to win. Sharp, Wheeler, and Moore all echoed it. Clodfelter's answer is ready to borrow. The nature of the war, plus the air commanders' own controls, meant Rolling Thunder could never give more than token support to Johnson's goals. Those own controls included doctrine that prized industry and a refusal to hit the Red River dikes, which Wheeler explained this way: "We tried to be sensible men." As for pointing to 1972 as proof, neither the war nor the objectives were the same, and the 1972 campaigns did not deliver total victory either. One number to keep straight: the 643,000 tons were dropped on the North. American aircraft dropped 2.2 million tons on the South over the same years. Quote the wrong one and you have more than tripled the air war against Hanoi.

If Johnson had removed every political control in 1965, which of the military and operational controls in this chapter would still have kept Rolling Thunder from hurting an enemy that needed 34 tons a day? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter V

Linebacker I: Hanoi Brought Tanks to a Bomb Fight

Chapter V, on 1969 to October 1972, argues that Richard Nixon's Linebacker campaign did what Rolling Thunder never could, and Clodfelter gives most of the credit to the circumstances rather than the bombs. The doctrine and many targets were the same. What changed was the war. Nixon wanted far less than Lyndon Johnson did: an American exit that did not hand the South to an imminent Communist takeover. American troops fell from 139,000 in January 1972 to 69,000 in April. Nixon flew to Peking in February and booked a Moscow summit for May, and both Communist powers turned out to want détente more than a Hanoi victory. A September Harris poll found 55 percent of Americans approved heavy bombing of the North. Moscow and Peking protested in words only. Nixon got a free hand and a summit photo.

Then Hanoi made the mistake of fighting like an army. On 30 March 1972, General Vo Nguyen Giap sent three divisions, backed by two hundred tanks, across the DMZ in what became the Easter Offensive. Nixon told remaining American ground troops not to engage and sent airplanes instead. F-4s in theater rose from 185 to 374. B-52s reached 210, more than half of Strategic Air Command's fleet, and a planner on Guam recalled, "We kept waiting for the northern end of the island to sink." After a 2 May meeting in Paris that lasted all of three hours, Nixon ordered the bombing and mining. The first raid, on 10 May, flew under the name "Rolling Thunder Alpha," because the new name had not yet reached the field. Thirty-two F-4s hit Hanoi's Paul Doumer Bridge with twenty-nine guided bombs. At Son Tay, F-4s dropped three laser-guided bombs on three warehouses and hit all three. Another flight wrecked the generator of the Lang Chi hydroelectric plant and left the dam fifty feet away untouched. The whole campaign dropped 155,548 tons, one-fourth of Rolling Thunder's total, yet overland imports fell from 160,000 to 30,000 tons a month and seaborne imports went from more than 250,000 tons to nearly zero.

The line to remember belongs to the British military authority Sir Robert Thompson, and it is the whole chapter: "You cannot refuel T-54 tanks with gasoline out of water bottles carried on bicycles," he observed. Rolling Thunder had hammered a guerrilla war that needed almost nothing from outside. In 1972 the North fought something close to a World War II blitzkrieg, which runs on oil and ammunition. With the ports mined, Hanoi had stockpiles and roads, and both sat in the open. American intelligence estimated that air power alone had cost the North 120,000 casualties by August. On 15 September, South Vietnamese divisions retook Quang Tri. On 8 October, Le Duc Tho dropped Hanoi's demand for a coalition government in Saigon, and on 23 October Nixon ended Linebacker. Clodfelter calls the Easter Offensive "tailor-made to suit the Air Force's bombing doctrine." The Politburo, in other words, finally attacked in the format the Air Force preferred.

Clodfelter lists four reasons the campaign worked: détente took away the fear of Soviet or Chinese escalation, the war had gone conventional, Henry Kissinger knew how to trade concessions while the bombs fell, and Nixon's goals were small. He adds a paradox. The bombing that bent Hanoi convinced South Vietnam's President Nguyen Van Thieu that more of it could win outright, so Thieu balked at the October draft. Kissinger announced on 26 October that peace was at hand. Seventh Air Force commander General John Vogt later said Nixon had stopped too soon. December would test both men.

Opinion: Put this chapter next to Conrad Crane on Korea. In April 1952, a Far East Air Forces study admitted that rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts and 243 aircraft lost. Twenty years later, interdiction worked. Clodfelter's explanation is consumption. An army that needs little can't be starved, and an army running tanks across a border can. Interdiction depends on what the enemy eats, and the Air Force does not set the menu. Many commanders credited Linebacker's success to reduced political controls. That is the flattering reading. The chapter's evidence says Hanoi's choice of war mattered at least as much as Washington's choice of rules.

If the North had stayed with Truong Chinh's protracted guerrilla strategy in 1972 instead of Le Duan's invasion, what could Linebacker I have accomplished? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter VI

Eleven Days, Fifteen Bombers, and a Largely Cosmetic Peace

Chapter VI, on November 1972 to January 1973, argues that Linebacker II had two targets, and only one of them was in North Vietnam. Nixon aimed the December bombing at Hanoi's will to keep stalling and at Saigon's belief that America might walk away. His clock was Congress, which he was sure would end the war when it met in January. Kissinger made the Paris talks harder by tabling all sixty-nine of Thieu's requested changes, and on 13 December Hanoi's experts slipped seventeen changes of their own into text that was supposedly finished. Kissinger wanted October's fighter raids back. General Alexander Haig wanted B-52s north of the 20th parallel. Nixon sided with Haig and told Admiral Thomas Moorer, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, that he would hold him personally responsible if the military failed. Nixon announced nothing to the public and handed Thieu a written ultimatum. Hanoi got the bombs. Saigon got the letter.

The numbers tell the campaign. On 18 December, 129 B-52s went north, and SAMs shot down three. SAC planned the missions from Offutt Air Force Base, Nebraska, with a forty-two-hour lead time, sent the bombers along the same routes at the same times for three nights, and on the 20th lost six. The losses infuriated Nixon. General John Meyer cut the daily rate to thirty bombers from U-Tapao, varied the routes, and lost none from the 22d to the 24th. After a thirty-six-hour Christmas pause, 120 B-52s hit ten targets in fifteen minutes on 26 December and lost two. The next morning Hanoi agreed to resume talks. In all, B-52s flew 729 sorties and dropped 15,237 tons. The North fired about a thousand SA-2s and downed fifteen B-52s, and by 29 December it had run out of missiles. Hanoi's mayor claimed 1,318 civilians killed, a low toll for that weight of bombs. Congress was not soothed: the House Democratic Caucus voted 154 to 75 to cut off war funding.

The person to remember is Major Robert Clark, who led the third wave out of Andersen on the first night. At the briefing, he said, he was ready, his navigator was terrified, and his gunner was a hawk. Before the raids, Clark called the Andersen Officers' Club desultory. By the second day, he recalled, "you could smell the fear." Crews hung on each other to confirm they were still alive. Sick call at the base clinic jumped from thirty or forty a day to fifty-five or sixty. Clark flew three missions and thought the B-52s brought the North's civilization to a screeching halt. He still refused to call the result peace with honor: "The fact that we retreated nullifies the words." Few after-action reports are that concise.

Clodfelter's verdict is that Linebacker II was a primary reason for the settlement, though not the only one. It wrecked supplies and made the threat of more bombing believable. It also produced the Congressional fury that Nixon then used on Thieu, warning that Congress would cut off Saigon if he refused to sign. Thieu sent his foreign minister to Paris on 20 January. As for what all the drama bought, Clodfelter writes that "the January agreement's differences from the October accord were largely cosmetic." Senator Barry Goldwater hailed the strategic bombing lesson, and many air commanders concluded that a campaign like this in spring 1965 would have won the war. The chapter closes in March 1975, with Giap's army crossing the DMZ and no American response.

Opinion: The lesson here is about where tactics get decided. SAC's planners took a lossless second night as proof that their routing worked and repeated it against a defense that had now seen it twice. The crews who complained in briefings were right, and Nebraska was wrong. Losses dropped once Meyer varied the routes after the 20th, and on 25 December he handed 8th Air Force authority over attack axes and withdrawal routes. The bombers stopped being predictable, and the loss rate fell. Centralized control of a strategic signal makes sense. Centralized control of the flight path, with a forty-two-hour lag, guarantees that the enemy plans faster than you do.

Air commanders claimed a Linebacker II-style campaign in spring 1965 would have won the war; using Clodfelter's own explanation for why the 1972 campaigns worked, what would have been missing in 1965? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Clodfelter, chapter VII

Any Two Weeks You Like: The Air Force Grades Its Own Homework

Chapter VII, the Epilogue, is Clodfelter's closing argument: air power's political payoff depends on the war it is thrown into, and no bomb load buys a guaranteed result. Below the nuclear line, he says, technology gave America lethal weapons and a habit of mistaking lethality for political effect. Johnson's team never agreed on what bombing was for. On the eve of the first Rolling Thunder mission, McGeorge Bundy said it would lift South Vietnamese morale, Maxwell Taylor that it would break Hanoi's will, Dean Rusk that it would buy bargaining leverage, and Robert McNamara that it would signal resolve. More reasons arrived once the bombs did. Johnson's negative goals, avoiding a third world war and keeping the public's eyes off Vietnam, fenced the campaign in. Nixon wanted less: a withdrawal that did not hand the South to an imminent takeover. Only Kissinger had his ear, and détente removed the danger of a wider war. Nixon's bombing worked better because it threatened what Hanoi actually needed.

Do not conclude that an unleashed Rolling Thunder would have won. Before Tet, the Viet Cong made up five-sixths of the Communist army, and together with Northern troops fought an average of one day in thirty. Their outside supply needs came to 34 tons a day, and no amount of bombing could stop that trickle. Eisenhower's nuclear threat in 1953 worked because it promised Communist defeat in Korea. Ruining North Vietnam promised Saigon nothing, since the Viet Cong could simply ignore Hanoi. The air chiefs would not have flattened Hanoi anyway. Their doctrine aimed at industry, their consciences balked at killing civilians, and Clodfelter finds their postwar boasts otherwise not credible. Tough talk is cheaper after the war.

1972 was different. Tet had gutted the Viet Cong, leaving the North Vietnamese Army as the only force that could unify the country, and the Easter Offensive threw it into the open as an all-out conventional assault. For the first time, bombing met Clausewitz's principle of polarity: it struck something essential to a Communist victory. Doctrine and morality, Rolling Thunder's two great military restraints, now suited the war. Laser-guided bombs and the best flying weather of the year helped. The war had finally shown up dressed for the doctrine.

The one thing to remember is Curtis LeMay in July 1986. Asked whether America could have won, the retired general answered, "In any two-week period you want to mention." The B-52s bombed for about seven days, he said, and the white flag practically went up. Harry Summers's On Strategy, a text at both Air Command and Staff College and the Air War College, tells the same story and skips the fact that most Communist troops in the South before 1968 were Viet Cong. Admiral Sharp put it plainly: "Our air power did not fail us; it was the decision makers." Air Force Manual 1-1 still lists ten vital targets, six of them industrial and three military. Unlike the generals after World War I, the post-Vietnam air leaders proposed no sweeping changes. Why fix a doctrine that only lost?

Opinion: this is Korea with better airplanes. Crane's book on Korea showed that Korea's ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded, so it went back to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam. Clodfelter's epilogue catches the same loop starting again, with Linebacker II as the new ambiguous ending. He calls the Eleven-Day War a unique campaign for very limited ends, one that rode on the first Linebacker, Nixon and Kissinger's diplomacy, and Hanoi's fear for an army it insisted on using conventionally. The lesson for a commander is the one Clodfelter puts at the top of the chapter in Clausewitz's words: figure out what kind of war you are in before you decide what your air force has already proved.

His last page is a warning. Political controls were no fluke; the atomic bomb made them a standing feature of modern war. The guerrilla war was the anomaly the Air Force never acknowledged, and it may come back. Until airmen and civilians accept that air power is unlikely to deliver cheapness or victory in a guerrilla war, and that success there may look like stalemate, Clodfelter warns that "the prospect of an aerial Verdun" will endure. If Linebacker II worked because 1972 was a conventional war, what in Air Force Manual 1-1's list of vital targets would have mattered against the Viet Cong of 1966? (S)

Clodfelter: seminar recall

  • What is Clodfelter's test for whether a bombing campaign worked?

    Its contribution to the nation's war aims, Clausewitz's definition of war as a continuation of political activity by other means (preface, e10).

  • Define positive and negative objectives, with Johnson's example of each.

    Positive objectives are reachable only by applying force; negative ones only by limiting it. Johnson's positive aim was an independent, non-Communist South; his negative aim was avoiding Chinese or Soviet intervention. Political controls flow from negative objectives (preface, e10).

  • Why did bombing "work" in 1972, in Clodfelter's preface?

    Nixon's two aims were limited, the Chinese and Soviets gave him a free hand, and Hanoi chose a sustained conventional war needing vast logistical support; that combination did not exist until 1972 (preface, e8–9).

  • What does Clodfelter mean by calling doctrine a "chameleon"?

    Emphasizing the enemy's production and distribution failed in Rolling Thunder, when Communist forces in the South needed few supplies, and worked in Linebacker I, when Hanoi's conventional assault needed massive logistics (preface, e11).

  • How does the epilogue end?

    Until airmen and civilians accept that air power is unlikely to provide "cheapness" or "victory" in a guerrilla war, and that success may equal stalemate, "the prospect of an aerial Verdun will endure" (ch. VII, e229).

Clodfelter: chapter recall

  • What is the difference between "positive" and "negative" objectives in Clodfelter's framework?

    Positive objectives can be achieved only by applying military force; negative objectives can be achieved only by restraining it. World War II's unconditional surrender had no negative objectives, while Truman's goal of avoiding world war limited the bombing in Korea. C preface, e10; ch. I, e19

  • What happened when F-84s hit the Toksan dam on 13 May 1953?

    The flood washed out five bridges and 6 miles of railroad and flooded 27 miles of river valley, and the Communists answered with their most intense propaganda campaign of the war. Clodfelter says the dam raids, with Eisenhower's atomic threat, helped produce serious negotiations. C ch. I, e34, e40

  • What were the Working Group's Options A, B, and C in November 1964, and which did it recommend?

    A continued current activity with reprisals, B was heavy bombing until Hanoi quit, and C paired current activity with milder bombing that would stop once negotiations began. The group recommended C on 21 November because it was controllable and B risked Chinese intervention. C ch. II, e70-71

  • When did Johnson order Rolling Thunder, and when did the first strike fly?

    He ordered it on 13 February 1965, but a coup attempt and monsoon weather delayed the first raids until 2 March, designated Rolling Thunder 5. C ch. II, e75, e80

  • How many targets were on LeMay's 1964 list, and how long did planners think a severe campaign would take?

    Ninety-four targets, eighty-two fixed sites and twelve lines of communication, destroyed in an estimated sixteen days. C ch. III, e93

  • What did the DIA find after the summer 1966 oil raids?

    North Vietnam still had 75,000 metric tons of storage, more than enough for its needs, much of it dispersed. C ch. III, e116

  • What did Johnson ask Wheeler at the end of the June 22, 1966 NSC exchange?

    Whether Wheeler was more sure than MacArthur had been that the Chinese and Soviets would stay out. C ch. III, e115

  • How did Wheeler restate the purpose of the power plant raids in May 1967?

    He said the aim was never to turn off the lights in cities, only to deny power to war-supporting industry, contradicting the chiefs' original psychological aim. C ch. III, e125

  • How many tons a day did Communist forces in the South need from outside sources?

    About 34 tons a day, which seven trucks could carry and which was less than 1 percent of North Vietnam's daily imports. C ch. IV, e152

  • What did it cost the United States to inflict a dollar of damage on North Vietnam?

    $6.60 in 1965 and $9.60 in 1966. C ch. IV, e152

  • What were the only two targets that Clodfelter says would have affected Northern war-making capacity?

    People and food, and even those would have had little effect on a war where the Communists set the tempo of combat. C ch. IV, e158

  • What did 7th Air Force find when it bombed the "missile propellant storage area" Washington asked about in 1966?

    A small village with a long storage building and no sign of missile propellant or anything else of military value. C ch. IV, e140

  • Why does Clodfelter say Linebacker I worked when Rolling Thunder hit many of the same targets?

  • What happened when SAC flew the same B-52 routes three nights running in Linebacker II?

  • Gaddis: where did "flexible response" get its public name?

    From Maxwell Taylor's book The Uncertain Trumpet (213).

  • Crane: what does the ambiguous ending in Korea do to the Air Force afterward?

    It lets the service believe it had succeeded, so it returned to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam (9, vii).

  • Zhang: what did Moscow order Belov's 151st Air Division in 1950, as China prepared to intervene?

    To retrain Chinese pilots first and not to fly missions beyond the Yalu, so the Chinese troops did not get the air cover they were counting on (62).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops actually engaged with allied forces?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how many tons a day did Communist forces in the South need from outside before Tet?

    About 34, which seven trucks could carry (ch. IV, e152).

Writing drills

  • Pick the verb: "LeMay ___ that America could have won in any two-week period." Notes, argues, or claims?

    "Claimed," if your paper follows Clodfelter, who finds the postwar boasts lacking credibility (ch. VII, e225). "Notes" would endorse it. Choose the verb that matches your evaluation (Ruppo's third column, 13–14).

  • State the conclusion: "Linebacker I dropped 155,548 tons. Overland imports fell from 160,000 to 30,000 tons a month."

    Add the point the numbers prove: "Linebacker I worked with a quarter of Rolling Thunder's tonnage because Hanoi's conventional offensive finally depended on imports the bombers could cut" (C ch. V, e184–185). Ruppo warns against evidence for a conclusion left implied (100).

  • Review (Monday, Pollock): find the actor in "It was decided that the oil raids would not begin on a Sunday."

    Johnson decided it: "Johnson ordered that the oil raids not begin on Sunday, June 26" (C ch. III, e115). Name the actor unless one of the legitimate reasons for the passive applies (Pollock, 42–43).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
