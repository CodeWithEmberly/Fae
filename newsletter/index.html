Counterpoint · Randolph against Clodfelter · review before Olsen

Counterpoint: Linebacker Coerced a Schedule. Randolph Asks What Else.

Mark Clodfelter's central claim is that Nixon's 1972 bombing worked as a political instrument because Nixon wanted little and Hanoi had switched to a kind of war that bombing could hurt. Of the December raids he writes that the "Eleven-Day War" was "not the only reason for a settlement, but it was a primary one," and that Nixon's threat of another Linebacker helped persuade the Politburo to sign. His epilogue explains the mechanism. After Tet gutted the Viet Cong, the Easter Offensive was an all-out conventional assault, so for the first time in Vietnam the bombing hit something Hanoi needed in order to win. (Clausewitz called this polarity. Air planners call it a target.)

Stephen Randolph, whom we read last Thursday, keeps two scorecards and gives Hanoi the better one. He grants the damage: in the first two months, Linebacker wiped out North Vietnam's industry and 70 percent of its electrical capacity, and air power caused most of the North Vietnamese army's casualties in the South. Hanoi still kept supplies moving to the front, met its minimum strategic objectives, and set up its 1975 victory. He calls the Easter Offensive "a strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome." If the side being coerced walks away with its minimum aims, what exactly was it coerced into?

Clodfelter's own evidence helps Randolph more than it helps Clodfelter. He concedes that the January agreement's differences from the October accord "were largely cosmetic." He also shows the pressure running toward Saigon. The congressional fury over the Christmas bombing, including a House Democratic Caucus vote of 154 to 75 to cut off war funds, became Nixon's lever on Thieu, whom he warned that Congress would cut off South Vietnam if he refused to sign. Thieu sent his foreign minister to Paris on January 20. So 729 B-52 sorties, 15,237 tons, and fifteen bombers lost bought Hanoi's return to a text it had accepted in October, plus Saigon's signature. (The government most coerced in December 1972 may have been the ally.)

Each author's evidence has limits. Clodfelter can show that the bombing changed the timing: Hanoi agreed to resume talks on December 27, the morning after 120 B-52s hit ten targets in fifteen minutes. By his own "cosmetic" line, he can't show that it changed the terms. Randolph can show that Hanoi's strategic position survived the bombing, and his casualty figures show how badly the bombing hurt along the way. Neither can show what Hanoi would have done in January without the eleven days, which is the question both books need answered.

Opinion: Randolph wins on the outcome and Clodfelter on the mechanism, and the two answers fit together. First order, bombing hurt an army that had come out to fight in the open. Second order, Hanoi traded battlefield gains it could not hold for an American exit it could use, and Washington spent its leverage on Saigon, not Hanoi. Third order, air commanders concluded that a Linebacker-style campaign in spring 1965 would have won the war, which Clodfelter calls a misreading of "a unique campaign for very limited ends." The seminar comment: "Linebacker II coerced a schedule, not a settlement: Clodfelter himself calls January's changes from October largely cosmetic, and Randolph shows Hanoi still met its minimum objectives, so the lesson is that bombing works when the demand is small." Tomorrow's book covers a campaign whose demand was the regime itself. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Olsen (tomorrow's book)

Olsen's Best Evidence for Drones Is a Thirteen-Foot Decoy

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. The crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran. The working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. What follows is our extrapolation from John Andreas Olsen, not Shaun's answer.

Olsen supports the answer at its core. His book argues that bombing a regime's leadership did not change the regime: forty-three days of bombing against several hundred targets, and the Republican Guard still withdrew in good order and the regime put down two revolts. All forty-three of Iraq's top political and military leaders on January 15 were alive after March 1. His diagnosis is a sensing failure. American intelligence focused on order of battle, so the planners knew little about Iraq beyond the locations of buildings and communications. The first night's cleverest move was also unmanned. Thirteen-foot decoy drones drew Iraqi radars into the air battle, anti-radiation missiles followed them in, and Iraqi air-defense radar activity fell by over 90 percent within three days. For the rest of the war the Iraqis were reluctant to turn on their radars or generate electrical power for fear of being targeted. A threat that stayed overhead changed behavior more than the damage did. (For a patrol over a strait, that is the entire business model.)

He cuts against the answer in three places. He credits the strategic campaign with real effects, fog and friction in Iraqi decisions and degraded war-making, so the paper can't treat strike as useless. He warns that air power tempts politicians because it seems to offer "the pleasures of gratification without the burdens of commitment," and a drone orbit is the most painless version of that temptation ever built. And Powell's verdict on Instant Thunder in December 1990, that such strategies "are designed to hope to win," applies to any presence mission with no theory of what makes Tehran change its behavior. One more caution, from 1991: the Tomahawks fired from the Persian Gulf flew over Iran's mountains without Tehran being told, and Olsen reports that nothing was made of it. Nobody should plan on that courtesy twice.

Opinion, extrapolated: Olsen would fund the sensors if they are aimed at how the regime holds power as well as at counting its boats. The paper should name the Iranian behavior the orbit is meant to change and say how Airmen will know it changed. A sensor network that measures nothing in particular is just very expensive weather. (S)

The Readings So Far · Crane, Randolph, Kitfield, and Olsen

Three Wars of Turning Off the Lights

Three books on the shelf describe air forces switching off an enemy's electricity, and in none of them did the enemy's leadership give up because the lights went out. In Crane's Korea, Navy and Air Force fighter-bombers flew 546 and 730 sorties in four days from June 23, 1952, and destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential. American prisoners that summer never saw the end of a propaganda film, because the power never lasted the full showing. In Randolph's 1972, Linebacker eliminated 70 percent of North Vietnam's electrical capacity in two months, and Hanoi kept its supplies moving. In Olsen's 1991, the Coalition hit eleven power plants and seven transformers in the first two days, and Baghdad lost commercial power within hours. (Seventy years of proving that dictators own flashlights.)

Olsen adds a detail the earlier books lack: Warden wanted transformers struck rather than generators, so the damage would not outlast a short war and the United States could help rebuild a postwar Iraq. Electric power then drew only 280 of the war's 35,040 strikes, under 1 percent, while Iraqi ground forces drew 23,430, about two-thirds. That matches Haun's appendix, which we read Monday: the work that decided the war was done against the army in Kuwait.

The news has been pinning itself to this shelf all week. Monday's cheap cruise missiles dropped from C-17s met Haun's Assault Breaker, and the question was who finds the targets. Tuesday's plan for a weapons school for acquisition "mavericks" met Kitfield's Top Gun and the Goldwater-Nichols promotion rules. Olsen turns that last link around. Kitfield credited Goldwater-Nichols for Horner's unified air command, while Olsen notes that the same 1986 law gave unprecedented power to the chairman and the theater commanders, which left the Air Staff, Warden's home, outside the chain of command that wrote the war plans. Opinion: the same law gets opposite reviews because Kitfield watches the theater commander's office and Olsen watches the Air Staff. Bring both pages tomorrow. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 3, Day 3 · precise language

"Render Ineffective" Was the Polite Way to Write "Kill Saddam"

Timothy Pollock, in Storytelling in Your Academic Writing, says jargon keeps outsiders at arm's length and gives insiders power. His example is Crozier's study of a French cigarette factory, where the repair manuals were "accidentally" destroyed in a fire and the mechanics passed the knowledge down orally, only to people they trusted. He cites a 2006 study by Oppenheimer that found readers judged writers who used complex language as less intelligent. Pollock's fix is plain: use the common word when an equivalent exists, use the shorter word, and drop presumptuous words like "obviously" and "merely." He also asks for verbs that carry the meaning, since "the alliance blew up" and "the alliance dissolved" each tell the reader more than "the alliance ended."

Olsen catches the planners doing the opposite. Instant Thunder's objective was first written as "kill Saddam," and later versions said "decapitating," "render ineffective," and "incapacitate leadership." Olsen calls these "mere changes of label." Before: "The campaign sought to achieve leadership incapacitation effects through precision engagement of regime nodes." After: "The planners wanted Saddam dead or cut off from his army and his people, and Warden expected a coup within a week." The second sentence can be checked against what happened. The first can't, which is presumably why somebody liked it.

Opinion: in a strategy paper, jargon hides the theory of victory, and the theory is the part the grader wants to see. "Effects," "nodes," and "leverage" let a writer skip the sentence that says who does what, and why the enemy would care.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: keep a Ctrl+F list of your own fog words (effects, nodes, leverage, degrade, impact, obviously, merely) and search the draft for each. At every hit, ask what physically happened and to whom. If you can't say, neither can the reader. (S)

Defense & Conflict × Olsen · via CSIS, November 10

Two Days of Tomahawks, Ten Years of Reloading

The United States has enough munitions to fight Iran again, and a war with China is another matter, according to a CSIS analysis published yesterday by Mark Cancian and Chris Park. In the thirty-nine-day campaign that ended with the April 8 ceasefire, and in the operations since, U.S. forces spent between a third and two-thirds of key munition inventories, including about a third of the prewar Tomahawk stock. Tomahawk production has averaged 86 missiles a year over the past ten fiscal years, against a goal of 1,000. By day fourteen, the authors note, Secretary Hegseth said less than 1 percent of the munitions U.S. forces fired were long-range. Their summary: "The problem is not money, assuming Congress continues its support, but time." They judge stocks sufficient for Iran and warn that the shift to cheap short-range weapons may not be achievable against a great power like China.

Olsen's chapter on the air campaign shows the same pattern in 1991. The Tomahawk, at $1.1 million each, had never been used in war, and Powell told Glosson in October 1990 that every Tomahawk the Navy had was not worth anything and that a fighter should back up every target they were aimed at. The Coalition launched 282 in the whole war, 64 percent of them in the first two days, and none after February 1. Strikes on leadership targets peaked in those same two days, then declined, and for the last two-thirds of the war the strategic campaign ran at what Olsen calls "a minimum level." The expensive weapons opened the war, and cheaper ones fought the rest of it.

Opinion: thirty-five years apart, the planners lavished attention on the opening salvo and left the reload to the budget process. The pattern makes sense against Iraq or Iran, where the long-range weapons kick the door in and the short-range ones can then work in the open, which is the transition CSIS says may not work against China. Olsen adds a warning the report doesn't. The opening salvo in 1991 was also the part aimed at the regime, and the regime survived it. A force that spends its scarcest weapons in the first forty-eight hours had better be sure the first forty-eight hours decide something.

Powell thought the Tomahawk was worth nothing. The Pentagon now finds it worth a wait of several years. (S)

Writing drills

  • Cut the fog: "Strategic paralysis effects were operationalized against regime centers of gravity."

    "Warden's planners bombed Baghdad's leadership, communications, and power targets, hoping Saddam would lose control and someone would overthrow him within a week" (Olsen, 94). Common words, a named actor, a checkable claim (Pollock, 41).

  • Pick the verb that carries the meaning: "Things changed in the Paris talks in December 1972, then the bombing happened, then talks happened again."

    "On December 13, Hanoi's negotiators slipped seventeen changes into a supposedly finished text; Nixon sent the B-52s north, and on December 27 Hanoi agreed to resume talks" (Clodfelter, ch. VI, e200, e207). "Slipped," "sent," and "agreed" say who acted (Pollock, 43–44).

  • Review (Tuesday, evidence): make it precise: "Tomahawks were used a lot early in Desert Storm."

    "The Coalition fired 282 Tomahawks, 64 percent of them in the first two days and none after February 1" (Olsen, 242). The number replaces "a lot" (Booth et al., 139).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
