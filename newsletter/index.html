Tomorrow's Reading · Thursday, October 29

Zhang Flips the Map: The Korean Air War from North of the Yalu

Tomorrow's seminar trades the view from the B-29 for the view from the MiG, which is the same war with the subtitles switched. Xiaoming Zhang's Red Wings over the Yalu tells the Korean air war from the Communist side, and mostly from the Chinese side, using newly opened Chinese and Russian archives and oral histories from the Chinese air force. He isn't shy about the goal. He says plainly that the book should significantly modify conventional wisdom about the Korean War. An author who announces that in the introduction is either very confident or about to be very interesting. Zhang manages both.

He pulls three threads: China's ideas about airpower and its rush to build an air force from nothing, the odd shape of Soviet help, and how Chinese air operations actually went. Running under all three is an alliance that was less than it looked. Moscow and Beijing were allies, in roughly the way two strangers sharing a cab are traveling companions: each used the war for its own national interests, and the Soviets held back to avoid a direct clash with the United States.

Here's the bridge from yesterday's book. Crane spent his pages showing Washington putting limits on American airmen. Zhang's twist is that the American and Soviet air operations were limited mainly by politics, while the Chinese air force was limited by inexperience. China had no aviation industry and depended on Soviet equipment, and its leaders were veterans of ground war with no real idea of air warfare. Opinion: that's the course's second theme in a single comparison. The Americans had the capability and were told not to use all of it. The Chinese had the permission and had to build the capability, at war, in a hurry, taught by Soviet advisers in a language the students didn't speak.

Read for four things. First, whose numbers to trust. Zhang warns that the kill ratios he reports are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent what really happened, and that the two sides' combat reports rarely match. He's just as blunt about the American side, calling U.S. victory claims probably highly inflated. Second, Stalin. Zhang calls the Soviet offer of air support a cryptic promise, and the October telegram in which Stalin asked for Chinese troops said nothing about air support at all. Stalin, it seems, was a firm believer in reading the fine print, provided he was the one who got to leave it out. Meanwhile the Chinese 13th Army Group was telling Beijing that air support was a requirement, and proposed delaying the intervention if the Soviets couldn't guarantee it. Third, doctrine. Chinese air planners saw fighters as defensive weapons and bombers as aggressive ones, which fit China's defensive outlook and pushed them toward a fighter force. Fourth, the lesson China kept: by Zhang's account, the Korean War experience limited the Chinese air force to an air defense role.

If time is short tonight, read the introduction and the conclusion first, then chapter three on Stalin, Mao, and the promise of air cover. Skim chapters six and seven for the covert Soviet air war and the Chinese air force in combat. Opinion: no seminar in recorded history has turned on page 140 of chapter six. Plenty have turned on the conclusion. (S)

The 628 Paper · through tomorrow's author

What Would Zhang Make of Your 628 Paper? Start with Taiwan

The prompt, for anyone who has been avoiding it: what is the most important potential strategic crisis facing the nation today, and, based on your historical understanding of the Age of Limited War, how should Airmen prepare to face it? One crisis only. The syllabus quotes Highlander on that point: there can be only one. Its examples are an Iranian nuclear test, a Chinese invasion of Taiwan, a Russian invasion of a NATO member, and the dissolution of an alliance. Ten pages, due December 1, which is closer than it appears on the calendar. What follows isn't an answer. It's a guess at how tomorrow's author would read the question.

Zhang would probably start where his own story starts, which is Taiwan. By his account, the Chinese Communists' urgent desire for an air force came primarily from Mao's plan to invade Taiwan. Mao asked Stalin's representative for Soviet air and naval support for the "liberation of Taiwan," and Zhou Enlai wrote of forming an offensive air unit to support the cross-strait campaign. The first operational mission assigned to the new air force was support for that projected campaign. Then Korea happened, and by Zhang's reckoning the war left the Chinese air force in an air-defense role.

Opinion from here, and an extrapolation rather than anything Zhang wrote. His lens suggests three cautions for an Airman writing about a Taiwan crisis. First, an air force carries its founding purpose. Taiwan isn't a new mission for the Chinese air force; it's the reason the service was created. Second, alliances. Zhang shows Moscow and Beijing each using Korea for its own national interests, with Stalin holding back from a direct clash with the United States. Any thesis about a future crisis should ask what each side's partners would actually risk, and the honest answer is rarely "everything." Third, numbers. If the two sides' combat reports in 1950 rarely matched, be wary of any force-ratio argument that rests on one side's figures.

There's a fourth caution, and it cuts closer to home. Zhang argues the Chinese air force of 1950 was limited mainly by inexperience, while the Americans were limited mainly by politics. Opinion: inexperience is the limit that time erodes. Political limits tend to persist. A paper that assumes the adversary is still the 1950 version, while Washington's restraints have somehow vanished, has the history backwards. The same alliance lesson applies if you pick Iran or a NATO crisis instead. Zhang won't write your paper, but he'd ask whose history your thesis quietly assumes. (S)

The Readings So Far · Gaddis and Crane

The Story So Far: Gaddis Set the Budget, Crane Sent the Bill

Two books in, the course already has a plot. John Lewis Gaddis defines strategy as the process by which ends are related to means, and his history of containment swings between two approaches. The symmetrical version meets the adversary wherever it pushes. The asymmetrical version picks its own ground. George Kennan's original version was asymmetrical. He counted only five centers of industrial and military power that mattered: the United States, Great Britain, Germany and central Europe, the Soviet Union, and Japan. Only one was in hostile hands, so the job was to keep the other four from falling, defended as strongpoints rather than along a perimeter.

Truman's people liked that approach for a reason that had little to do with elegance. They were convinced the country had only limited resources, however dangerous the threat. Truman framed it as spending twenty or thirty billion dollars to keep the peace against a hundred billion to fight a war. Dean Acheson told the Senate that recovery was the first objective. The atomic monopoly made the bargain feel safe; James Forrestal called the years before anyone could strike back "our years of opportunity." Kennan's own first rule was that interests come first and threats are measured against them, never the reverse. He then managed to undercut himself in print. His "X" article was anonymous for roughly as long as anything stays secret in Washington: Arthur Krock revealed him as the author, Walter Lippmann took it apart at length, and careless drafting left Kennan agreeing in places more with Lippmann than with his own essay. The article's call for "unalterable counter-force at every point" reads like perimeter defense, from the man who invented the strongpoint.

Conrad Crane picks up where the money and the bomb meet Korea. His subject is "the image and reality of American airpower," and the gap between the two is the whole book. Leaders expected too much, partly because the Air Force didn't admit its limits. FEAF's General Smart put it with a candor his successors have rarely matched: "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." (Neither, to be fair, does anyone with a budget.) Washington drew the lines anyway. Air action was held south of the 38th Parallel until Truman approved strikes in the North on June 29, 1950, with orders to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders. After FEAF bombed Rashin, about twenty miles from the Soviet border, the Joint Chiefs stopped further attacks there in September. In November, the Yalu bridge attacks were limited to the Korean side of the spans.

Interdiction, airpower's all-purpose answer to ground war, did not save the day. Fifth Air Force intelligence estimated that as many as 500,000 soldiers and civilians were working to keep enemy transportation routes open. An April 1952 FEAF study found the rail campaign had not worked despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed, and proposed "air pressure" instead. The June 1952 hydroelectric strikes then knocked out about ninety percent of North Korea's electric power. As for the bomb that made containment feel cheap: Attlee rushed to Washington after Truman's November 30 press conference, Truman sent nuclear weapons toward Okinawa in April 1951, and the HUDSON HARBOR test missions convinced the Air Staff that Korea offered no suitable atomic targets.

Opinion: put the two books side by side and the plot writes itself. Gaddis's planners counted on the bomb to make cheap containment safe. Crane's airmen found the bomb had nothing to hit and the war had limits nobody wrote down. A 1953 FEAF study blamed policy makers for never stating limited-war objectives "compatible with air force capabilities." Kennan would have called that skipping step one. And because the war ended ambiguously, Crane argues, the Air Force could believe it had succeeded, went back to planning for general war, and paid for it in Vietnam. Two books, one lesson: the side that never defines its interest ends up defending its budget. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 1, Day 3

The Implementation of Interdiction Resulted in Sadness: On Precise Language

Helen Sword, who has spent a career diagnosing what ails academic prose, keeps a short list of words that turn up in suspicious quantities in stodgy writing. Two of her categories matter most today: forms of "be," and the little pointing words "it," "this," "that," and "there." A sentence that opens with a bare "this" is almost always pointing back at the sentence before it, and asking the reader to do the remembering. Either fold the two sentences together or put a noun after "this." The vague quantity words belong on the same watch list. "Many" and "most" are trying to sound significant while committing to nothing. How many? Is most fifty-point-one percent? How do you know? These are rude questions, and graders ask them anyway.

Here's a paragraph any of us could have written at eleven last night. "Many sorties were flown against the railways, but this did not stop the enemy. Some people thought interdiction was not working. This led to a new strategy." Three sentences, and not one fact an instructor could check. Now the same paragraph after a trip back to the book. "FEAF's rail campaign produced more than 15,000 rail cuts at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed, yet an April 1952 FEAF study concluded that interdiction had not worked. Fifth Air Force intelligence estimated that as many as 500,000 soldiers and civilians were working to keep enemy transportation routes open. The same study proposed a replacement: air pressure through selective destruction." "Many" became numbers, "some people" became a dated study, and every "this" became the thing it was pointing at.

Today's pro tip comes from Helen Sword's Stylish Academic Writing, and it's about the vague word's best friend, the nominalization. That's her term for the long abstract nouns academics build out of verbs and adjectives by bolting on endings like -ion, -ment, -ness, and -ity. Sword actually counted them across disciplines, and there's good news for this building. In higher education research, 78 percent of the articles she sampled were dense with them. In history, only 16 percent were. Historians, it turns out, are the people who still let somebody do something to someone. Try not to ruin it.

Her fix is one rule you can check in a minute. Make sure at least one sentence in every paragraph has a concrete noun or a human being as its subject, followed immediately by an active verb. "Weyland opposed." "Truman approved." "Stalin hedged." She also suggests limiting prepositional phrases to three in a row, and running a sample of your prose through her free WritersDiet test, which flags the flab. Compare "The implementation of the interdiction campaign resulted in the destruction of rail lines without the achievement of the desired disruption" with "FEAF cut the rails fifteen thousand times, and the trains kept running." The second one has a subject, a verb, and a point. The first one has a committee. Opinion: vague words feel safe because nobody can prove them wrong. That's also why they never prove you right. (S)

Whose Numbers Do You Trust? Michigan, Israel, and Zhang's Kill Ratios

Six days out, Michigan's Senate race appears to have moved four points overnight, which is impressive for a race in which nothing obviously happened overnight. On Tuesday, electoral-vote.com listed a YouGov poll, taken October 2 through 19, with Democrat Abdul El-Sayed at 48 percent and Republican Mike Rogers at 46. Today's table has a Glengariff Group poll, taken October 19 through 22, with El-Sayed at 51 and Rogers at 45. Same race, same month, different pollster. Elsewhere on today's list, Marist has Troy Jackson leading Susan Collins 49 to 47 in Maine, and YouGov has James Talarico ahead of Ken Paxton 50 to 45 in Texas. EV notes that if the three close races all break the same way, Democrats would reach 56 seats, which is the kind of sentence that contains a very large "if."

Then there's Israel, where EV reports that results are, in its words, "predictably ambiguous." With only about half the ballots counted, exit polls have Likud winning the most seats while the opposition bloc out-totals Netanyahu's coalition, roughly 65 to 55. Both sides, naturally, can find a number in there they like.

Opinion: Xiaoming Zhang would recognize the genre. In tomorrow's reading he warns that the kill ratios he reports are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent what actually happened, and that the two sides' combat reports in Korea rarely matched. A poll isn't a propaganda bulletin, but the strategist's problem is the same one. A single number from a single source is a claim, not a fact, and two numbers from two sources is a range, not a trend. The campaign that reads the Glengariff poll as momentum and the YouGov poll as noise has just done what both air forces did over the Yalu, which is believe the scoreboard it keeps itself. The voters, unlike the MiG pilots, will settle it on November 3. (S)

You Can't Win an Air War You Can't Build: 850 Jets, a Million Drones, and Mao's Shopping List

Last week the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin announced a ten-year framework for at least 850 F-35s, about 85 a year, which happens to match the fiscal 2027 request. Congress, which has to pay for it, funded 47 in fiscal 2026. The deal also depends on congressional approval, and the stopgap law running through December 11 generally bars new multiyear buys, so the ten-year plan currently has a shelf life of about six weeks. A week earlier, Defense Secretary Hegseth said the Pentagon wants fifty billion dollars to grow its stockpile of autonomous weapons from thousands of units to millions. The department, in other words, would like both the Ferrari and the swarm of mopeds, and would like Congress to stop asking which one.

Now go to tomorrow's reading. When the People's Republic was founded, Zhang writes, it had no aviation industry and no access to Western aircraft, so its new air force had to depend entirely on Soviet equipment and Soviet training. The founding plan reads like a shopping list: send people to study in the Soviet Union for six to eight months, and buy about one hundred planes. Beijing had a doctrine, a war plan, and a target. What it didn't have was a factory, and everything Zhang describes afterward, from Stalin's cryptic promises to pilots learning in a language they didn't speak, flows from that one missing building.

Opinion: an air force is a production line before it's a doctrine. China in 1950 had the ambition and none of the industry. The United States in 2026 has the industry, two competing ambitions, and a funding line that reads 47. One more number to keep in mind: GAO found the F-35's mission-capable rate fell from 67 percent in fiscal 2021 to 44 percent in fiscal 2025. Lockheed says steady multiyear orders will help, which is the defense-industry equivalent of a contractor assuring you the second bathroom will go faster. Zhang's lesson for the acquisition crowd is simple. The side that can't build what it flies ends up flying on someone else's terms. Mao learned that from Stalin. It would be a shame to learn it from an appropriations committee. (S)

Leadership × Zhang · via Military Times, last week

Petraeus Wants a Revolution. Zhang Has Notes on Who Should Lead It.

Retired General David Petraeus told an aluminum industry gathering in Washington on October 16 (the drone revolution, apparently, will be lightweight) that the United States and NATO need "urgent revolutionary institutional change" to prepare for modern war. He wants it to reach force structure, training, procurement, personnel policy, and bases. He would trade much of the heavily manned legacy force for cheaper, more numerous unmanned systems, and he wants production lines that can push software changes weekly. He called the new Autonomous Warfare Command, which requires each service to stand up its own component, a commendable step. Each service will now get to decide how revolutionary it would like to be, which is a bit like letting the defendants pick the jury.

Zhang has a case study in exactly this kind of revolution, and it's not entirely encouraging. When Beijing decided to build an air force from scratch, Zhou Enlai handed the job to Liu Yalou, an army group commander. Money was no obstacle. Liu had Mao's personal attention and got what he asked for. But Zhang judges that Liu's limited knowledge of an air force's day-to-day workings may have stunted its development in the long run. Liu believed the air force should be tied to ground operations, doubted that aviation could be decisive in war, and declared that the air force should support the ground forces and take their victories as its own. The teachers had problems of their own. The Soviet advisers wore civilian clothes for secrecy, many had little teaching experience, and their students had little education and no Russian.

Opinion, and the leadership lesson: revolutions in military affairs are usually led by people who did extremely well in the previous military affairs. Liu built a new service in the image of the old one, and the funding didn't fix it, because the problem was never money. If the Autonomous Warfare Command's component commanders all come from the platforms the drones are meant to replace, Zhang would recognize the pattern. So would Kennan, who would ask first what interest the revolution serves. Lead change with at least some people who are fluent in the new thing, and pay for good teachers. Otherwise the new service ends up taking the old service's victories as its own, and its old habits too. (S)

Defense & Conflict × Zhang · via Defense News

Up, Up, and Just Outside: The Army Rediscovers the Balloon

The most forward-looking idea in the Pacific this week is a technology the Montgolfier brothers would recognize. Defense News reports that the Army is exploring high-altitude balloons that carry payloads to watch the battlefield, relay communications, and even launch strikes, all aimed at China's anti-access network. Major General Bernard Harrington, who commands the 7th Infantry Division and Multi-Domain Command-Pacific, pitched them at the Association of the U.S. Army's annual conference as cheaper and harder to spot than the alternatives, and launchable from dispersed sites. Balloons can now stay up for weeks or months. Above 60,000 feet, their sensors can see deep into a rival's territory while the balloon itself stays in international airspace. The Army has tested them in Guam and Japan, and the European command's commander would like to launch "200, 300 at a time." The flight paths, he notes, vary by season, which makes this the first weapon system whose deployment schedule is set by the jet stream.

Now go to tomorrow's reading. Zhang's Chinese air force wasn't born to project power. In early 1950, he writes, the Nationalist air force effectively owned the airspace over China and was raiding Shanghai and other coastal cities. Beijing's plan for its first year called for seven fighter regiments for air defense and four bomber regiments for the Taiwan campaign. The new air force's mission list, in order, was to win air superiority over the Nationalists, support the assault on Taiwan, and then defend China's airspace and waters, which is why fighters got priority. Korea, as we've covered, then left that air force in a defensive role for good.

Opinion: put those together and the balloon plan becomes a quiet test of the reflex Zhang describes. An air force forged by watching enemy aircraft over its own coast is being invited to watch American sensors hover just outside it. Note, too, where the Army is careful to park them: in international airspace, on the legal side of the line. Crane's airmen in 1950 were told to hit only the Korean half of the Yalu bridges. Seventy-six years later, the limited-war instinct is the same, and only the altitude has changed. The open question for seminar is whether a line that both sides can see is a limit or just a dare. (S)

Today's practice · from Helen Sword, Stylish Academic Writing

Sword's rule: give every paragraph at least one sentence with a human or concrete subject followed right away by an active verb, and keep prepositional phrases to three in a row or fewer (p. 61). Rewrite this sentence her way, then open the model.

  • One possible rewrite

    "FEAF bet on interdiction, cut the rails more than 15,000 times, lost 243 aircraft doing it, and still couldn't stop the trains" (Crane, 115–116). The human subject and four active verbs carry it, and the nominalizations (prioritization, expenditure, achievement, disruption) are gone.

Crane: seminar recall

  • Who limited airpower in Korea, and how?

    Washington, step by step: air action south of the 38th Parallel until June 29, 1950, then the North with the Soviet and Manchurian borders off-limits (23–24); no more attacks near Rashin after September 1950 (44–45); Yalu bridges hit only on the Korean side (49–50).

  • What was Truman afraid of?

    A wider, possibly global war. Attlee rushed to Washington after the November 30, 1950 press conference (58–59). In April 1951 Truman sent nuclear weapons and SAC bombers toward Okinawa, though the bombers stayed on Guam (70).

  • Did airpower achieve its objectives?

    Partly. Interdiction disappointed (82–83). The April 1952 study found more than 15,000 rail cuts hadn't worked, at a cost of 243 aircraft, and launched air pressure (115–116). The hydroelectric strikes cut about 90 percent of the North's power (119–120). Air superiority was "in jeopardy" in September 1951 (83–84).

  • Why no atomic bomb?

    HUDSON HARBOR found long delays and no suitable targets (71). Weyland opposed atomic use over the Manchurian airfields and the propaganda risk (157–158).

  • What interservice fights did airpower start?

    Weyland said he fought the Navy as much as the Communists, and Smart spent 40 percent of his time on Army and Navy proposals (7). Close air support: about 10 minutes for the Marines versus about 45 for the USAF (61–62).

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did Kennan say mattered, and why did Truman like it?

    Five centers of industrial and military power, defended as strongpoints (29, 57). It fit an administration convinced its resources were limited (57–58).

  • Crane: name two Washington limits on airpower in 1950.

    No more attacks near Rashin after September 1950 (44–45), and the Yalu bridges hit only on the Korean side (49–50).

Writing drills

  • Fix it: "Several restrictions often limited airmen."

    Name them and who imposed them: "The JCS barred strikes near the Soviet and Manchurian borders and limited the Yalu bridge attacks to the Korean side of the spans" (Crane, 23–24, 49–50).

  • Fix it: "Most historians agree this was a failure."

    Which historians, and what failed? "Crane argues that rail interdiction failed despite more than 15,000 cuts" (115–116).

  • Review: "It was decided that interdiction would continue."

    Passive voice with no actor. Sword's rule applies: put a human subject in front of an active verb. Name the decider your source identifies.

    • That led to "strongpoint" defense of particular regions and the access to them, rather than "perimeter" defense of fixed lines. 57
  3. GaddisAnalyzeWhat logic connects Kennan's interests, threats, and means?
    • Because capabilities are finite, interests must be too, so you have to separate the vital from the peripheral. 31
    • Means should serve ends, because indiscriminate methods can corrupt the objective. 31
    • Interests are the standard for judging threats, never the reverse. A threat has no meaning except in relation to your interests. 31

    Comps angle: most failures in limited war start when threats begin defining interests.

  4. GaddisAnalyzeWhy did the "X" article create confusion that lasted for decades?
    • It ran anonymously in Foreign Affairs in 1947, but Arthur Krock revealed Kennan as the author. That made it read like official policy, and Walter Lippmann attacked it at length. 24–25
    • Careless drafting contradicted positions Kennan held inside the government, so in places he agreed more with Lippmann than with his own article. He couldn't clarify publicly until his memoirs twenty years later. 25
    • Its call for "unalterable counter-force at every point" reads like perimeter defense, even as Kennan was moving toward strongpoints. 57
  5. GaddisAnalyzeWhy did strongpoint defense appeal to the Truman administration before 1950?
    • It was consistent with the prevailing perception of means: the conviction that resources were limited no matter how dangerous the threat. 57–58
    • Kennan partly articulated and rationalized moves the administration was already making. He wasn't its sole author. 57
    • Economic aid looked cheaper than war: Truman compared spending $20–30 billion to keep the peace with $100 billion to fight a war. Acheson told Congress that "Recovery is the first objective." 61
    • The atomic monopoly let officials accept risks. Forrestal called the years before an adversary could strike back "our years of opportunity." 61
  6. CraneRecallState Crane's thesis in three sentences.
    • The book examines "the image and reality of American airpower." Leaders and the public expected too much, partly because the USAF did not admit its limits. 6
    • The USAF planned for quick air superiority followed by strategic bombing. It got the MiG-15, a sanctuary across the border, and a forced turn to interdiction and then "air pressure." 7–8
    • The ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded. It drew few lessons and returned to planning for general war, which cost it in Vietnam. 9, vii
  7. CraneRecallTrace three Washington-imposed limits on airpower in 1950.
    • Air action was first limited to south of the 38th Parallel. On 29 June 1950 Truman approved strikes in the North, and the JCS restriction was to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders. 23–24
    • After the bombing of Rashin, about 20 miles from the Soviet border, State Department fears led the JCS to bar further attacks there in September 1950. 44–45
    • In November 1950 the Yalu bridge attacks were restricted to the Korean side of the spans. 49–50
  8. CraneAnalyzeWhy did FEAF shift from interdiction to "air pressure"?
    • Interdiction disappointed. FEAF regretted the name STRANGLE, and Communist repair crews of up to 500,000 kept the railways running. 82–83
    • An April 1952 FEAF study concluded the rail campaign had not worked despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed. It proposed air pressure through selective destruction. 115–116
    • The June 1952 hydroelectric strikes, run jointly by the Navy and Air Force, destroyed about 90 percent of North Korea's electric power. 119–120
  9. CraneAnalyzeWhy wasn't the atomic bomb used in Korea?
    • Allied fear: after a 30 November 1950 press conference on the bomb, Attlee rushed to Washington. 58–59
    • Escalation fear cut both ways. In April 1951 Truman ordered nuclear weapons and more SAC bombers toward Okinawa, fearing global war, but the bombers stayed on Guam. 70
    • Practical limits: the 1951 HUDSON HARBOR tests found long delays and weak target evaluation, and the Air Staff concluded there were no suitable atomic targets. 71
    • Weyland opposed atomic use. The Manchurian airfields might not be neutralized, and the Soviets would learn from the strikes and use them for propaganda. 157–158
  10. CraneRecallWhat were the main interservice fights, and was air superiority ever secure?
    • Weyland said he fought the Navy as much as the Communists. Smart spent 40 percent of his time countering Army and Navy proposals. 7
    • On close air support, Marines delivered bombs in about 10 minutes against about 45 for the USAF. Almond carried his complaints to General Collins. 61–62
    • Air superiority was never secure: in September 1951 FEAF had about 90 F-86s against 500-plus MiGs and warned that superiority was "in jeopardy." 83–84
  11. Gaddis × CraneCompareThe atomic bomb was Truman's deterrent of choice in 1948 and unusable in Korea by 1951. Why?
    • Gaddis: before 1950 the atomic monopoly underwrote the risks of a cheap, economy-first containment. G 61
    • Crane: in Korea, allied fears (Attlee), escalation fears, and a lack of suitable targets made the bomb a threat rather than a tool. C 58–59, 70, 71

    Argue it: a weapon designed for general war has little use in a limited war whose limits exist to prevent general war.

  12. Gaddis × CraneCompareKennan said interests should define threats. Who in Korea failed to define the interest that airpower served?
    • Kennan's standard: interests first, threats judged against them, and means subordinate to ends. G 31
    • A 1953 FEAF study blamed policy makers for never stating limited-war objectives "compatible with air force capabilities." C 155
    • Airmen weren't candid either: "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." C 6

    Argue it: the failure was mutual. Policy makers didn't state the end, and airmen didn't state the limits of their means.

  13. Gaddis × CraneCompareKennan's "X" article and Smart's candor problem: what do they share as leadership failures?
    • Kennan wrote loosely enough that readers took a perimeter strategy from a strongpoint thinker. G 25, 57
    • Smart's Air Force didn't state its limits, so leaders expected more than airpower could deliver. C 6

    Argue it: in both cases, what the author left unsaid shaped policy more than what was said.

  14. Gaddis × CraneSynthesizeWas Korea fought as symmetrical or asymmetrical containment?
    • Commitment: defending South Korea at all reflects the expanded, symmetrical view that follows NSC-68. G ch. 4, 87 · to check
    • Conduct: Washington drew tight geographic limits on airpower (the 38th Parallel, borders, Rashin, the Yalu spans). C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50

    Argue it: a symmetrical commitment fought with asymmetrical restraint, and airpower was caught in the gap between the two.

  15. Gaddis × CraneSynthesizeWhy might the New Look have made Korea's lessons easy for the Air Force to ignore?
    • Crane: the ambiguous ending let the USAF believe it had won, and it returned to planning for general war under the New Look. C 9
    • Gaddis: the New Look is asymmetrical and nuclear-centered. G ch. 5, 125 · to check

    Argue it: a strategy that rewards nuclear general-war forces gives an institution little budget reason to study limited war.

  16. Crane × ZhangCompareAfter ThursdayWhose limits were political, and whose were self-imposed?
    • Crane: Washington imposed political and geographic limits on American airpower. C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50
    • Zhang: US and Soviet operations were limited mainly by politics, while the PLAAF was limited by inexperience. Z 8
    • China had no aviation industry and relied on Soviet equipment, and its leaders were ground-war veterans. Z 6–7
  17. Crane × ZhangCompareAfter ThursdayHow should a strategist treat one side's combat statistics?
    • Zhang warns that his kill ratios are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent reality, and that the two sides' reports rarely match. Z 10–11
    • He calls American victory claims "probably highly inflated" and criticizes the one-sided US official account. Z 5
    • Compare Crane's F-86-versus-MiG numbers. C 83–84
  18. Crane × ZhangSynthesizeAfter ThursdayBoth superpowers feared a direct clash. How did that fear shape the air war on each side of the Yalu?
    • US side: borders off-limits, Rashin halted, Yalu spans restricted, and the 1951 atomic deployment. C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50, 70
    • Communist side: the Soviets held back to avoid a direct clash with the US, and Moscow and Beijing each pursued their own national interests. Z 7
    • Stalin's "cryptic promise" of air support, and his October telegram asking for Chinese troops that didn't mention it. Z 60, 62, 65
  19. Crane × ZhangSynthesizeAfter ThursdayIs airpower inherently defensive? Answer with both books.
    • Zhang: PLAAF planners saw fighters as defensive and bombers as aggressive. The war left the PLAAF in an air-defense role. Z 46, 11
    • But China's first air-war concept came from planning to invade Taiwan. Z 6, 53
    • Crane: FEAF's ambitions were offensive (strategic bombing, then air pressure), but it spent the war fighting to hold air superiority. C 7–8, 83–84
  20. Gaddis × ZhangSynthesizeAfter ThursdayApply Kennan's five-centers logic from the other side. Why did Korea matter to Moscow and Beijing differently?
    • Kennan ranked regions by industrial and military power. Korea wasn't one of his five centers. G 29
    • Zhang: Moscow and Beijing each used the war for their own national interests, and Stalin pushed China in while staying out himself. Z 7, 62

    Argue it: a peripheral theater for one ally can be a vital one for another, and alliances fracture along that line.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
