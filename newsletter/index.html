Seminar 14 · Henriksen, NATO's Gamble · "Operation ALLIED FORCE: Airpower Gets its Day?"

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered

  1. How do alliance politics affect stated military objectives? They shrink the objective to whatever nineteen governments will sign. Clark says the campaign began with one objective, driving the Serbs back to the talks at Rambouillet, and only later grew to halting the ethnic cleansing and then the G-8's five conditions; Henriksen says the Rambouillet aim came from the lack of consensus on anything bigger (chapter 1). Washington imposed target-by-target approval, France demanded to see the target lists, and when several allies balked at Phase Three, Naumann and Solana invented "Phase Two Plus" so Solana could approve Phase Three targets without a council vote (chapter 1). By the end, Henriksen says, targeting aimed inward at allied publics more than outward at Milosevic, and Clark admitted he sacrificed the basic logic of warfare to keep the alliance together (chapter 10).
  2. What are the risks of acting without gaining consensus? The enemy plans around the gaps, and the allies stop trusting you. With no UN authorization and Russia threatening a veto, Milosevic told his Supreme Defense Council that NATO would bomb for about five to seven days before the international community forced it to stop (chapters 5 and 7). Inside the alliance, a U.S.-only operation, Joint Task Force Noble Anvil, ran alongside NATO's campaign; General Rupert Smith called it fundamentally wrong, and Short said that doing it again would leave America without allies (chapter 1). Smith also says American diplomats threatened NATO air power in 1998 without properly consulting NATO, which then had to make good on threats it hadn't chosen (chapter 5).
  3. Did airpower provide NATO with a scalpel or a sledgehammer to intervene in a civil war? Neither, because NATO never agreed which one it wanted. Short wanted the sledgehammer, "incredible speed and incredible violence" on the first night against Belgrade, while the French wanted to save the best targets so Milosevic would always have more to lose (chapters 2 and 10). Against the people actually doing the killing, Naumann told the council, air power could not stop the individual murderer going from village to village with a knife (chapter 10). Henriksen calls attacks on fielded forces a political necessity and probably right (chapter 2), while Lambeth calls them largely ineffective, even though 79 percent of precision munitions hit their targets (Lambeth, chapter 6).
  4. Did NATO "win" this war? Serbia? The Kosovars? Henriksen stops when the bombing starts, so the scorecard comes from Lambeth. After 78 days, Yugoslavia accepted 50,000 peacekeepers under a NATO general with sweeping powers over Kosovo, and the UN put Kosovo under international civilian control (Lambeth, chapter 6); Lambeth credits Russia's shift on June 3, the war crimes indictment, and preparations for a ground invasion alongside the bombing. The Kosovars got the intervention the KLA had courted, though not the independence Holbrooke admits he may have suggested was possible within three to five years (Henriksen, chapter 6). Henriksen's verdict on NATO's side is blunt: coercion may have been impossible earlier, but "the effort was inadequate" (chapter 10).

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. (S)

Today's Reading · Henriksen, NATO's Gamble

NATO Bet Three Nights and Got Seventy-Eight

Dag Henriksen argues that NATO went to war on March 24, 1999, without planning for the war it was starting, and that the airmen share the blame with the politicians. Key leaders expected two to four days of bombing to move Milosevic, because they believed Deliberate Force had done it in Bosnia. The targeting cell at Vicenza was told, less than forty-eight hours out, to pick three nights of good targets. Short ran out of them on night three and canceled a wave of F-117s. (When the plan is three nights long, night four is improvisational theater.)

The book's sharpest move is to turn the airmen's complaint around. Short, Ryan, and the staff at Vicenza blamed the politicians for gradualism, and Henriksen grants Short a real point: hunting Serb units from village to village was not what air power was suited for. Then he notes that when politicians said "we have got to do something," the military had no adequate answer short of "the classic air campaign that we'd all learned at Maxwell." That puts him beside Lambeth on the facts and against him on the blame, and it sends us back to Clodfelter, whose Rolling Thunder had the same gradual shape thirty years earlier. Opinion: Henriksen is mostly writing about credibility. A threat NATO wouldn't carry out was worse than no threat, and NATO made a lot of them. (S)

Counterpoint · Lambeth against Henriksen

Counterpoint: Lambeth Says Leave the Airmen Out of It

Henriksen's central claim is that NATO's coercion of Milosevic was inadequate because politicians and airmen alike were unprepared for a limited, coalition war, and that the airmen deserve part of the blame they spent the war assigning to others.

Benjamin Lambeth, whom we read yesterday, makes the strongest case against the second half of that claim, and he uses many of the same witnesses. He calls Allied Force a suboptimal use of air power "through no fault of allied airmen," and puts the fault on those responsible for the strategy. His evidence is the opening itself. Admiral Ellis, who commanded the U.S. contribution, told Pentagon and NATO officials that luck played the chief role in the campaign's success, that NATO's leaders "called this one absolutely wrong," and that consensus politics produced an "incremental war" with sanctuaries the enemy exploited. Lambeth also gives the bombing a result Henriksen never reaches: punishment "did seem to work against Milosevic," which Lambeth says disconfirms the old adage that air power can beat up an enemy indefinitely without changing his mind. If the airmen's method was wrong, why did the alliance win with it once it finally escalated?

The two books agree more than the airmen would like. Lambeth also blames a questionable reading of Bosnia for NATO's belief that a few days of token bombing would do. He quotes Ralston that gradualism "may be perceived as the only option" when political constraints are pervasive, and he concludes that airmen will have to discipline their natural inclination to bridle at politicians. That is Henriksen's point in a RAND voice.

Each author's evidence has limits. Henriksen's strength is his interviews (Naumann, Kelche, and Norway's defense and foreign ministers), which show the European side Lambeth mostly hears secondhand. His weakness is the calendar: the book ends in the first week of bombing, so it cannot test whether the coercion eventually worked, and it leans on memoirs written after the fact. Clark's account of the five residences, Henriksen admits, comes from Kelche and has not been verified by Clark. Lambeth's strength is the end of the war. His weakness is that his own list of why Milosevic quit includes Russia, the indictment, and a ground invasion plan, so "punishment worked" is a claim about bombing plus everything else.

Opinion: Henriksen has the better of it, and the reason is what happened next. First order, Short's night-one plan was outside what nineteen governments would approve, and Lambeth concedes that gradualism may be the future of coalition war. Second order, an air arm that offers only the Maxwell answer gets ignored when the politics forbid it, and then improvises; Jumper later said planners managed the target list day by day without reference to the effects they wanted. Third order, France's General Kelche built a permanent cell to prepare crisis options, because politicians with no options "opt to wait or do nothing." Lambeth is right that the airmen didn't write the strategy. Henriksen is right that they didn't offer one either. The seminar comment: "Lambeth proves the strategy failed the airmen; Henriksen proves the airmen had nothing ready for the strategy NATO could actually approve." Luck, as Ellis noted, is not a line of effort. (S)

Questions to Ask in Seminar · Henriksen

Four Questions for an Alliance That Couldn't Agree on a Fifth

  1. Whose job was it to tell the politicians there was no plan? Henriksen concludes "NATO had hardly planned for the war the Alliance itself was about to start" (Henriksen, chapter 10, ebook page 341). One answer: the military's. When politicians said they had to do something, Henriksen writes, the military could not give an adequate reply, and General Smith says soldiers are "stupid" to demand precise end states from politicians (ebook pages 337 and 339). The other answer: the politicians'. Clark was asking Solana and Naumann "What really was our aim?" after the bombing started, and the British Defence Committee found the politicians had no clear grasp of their own strategy on March 24 (ebook page 30). The first answer is stronger for seminar, because it is the only one an officer can act on.
  2. Was the French "save the best targets" logic smarter than Short's shock? A French officer told Clark, "We believe we must save the best targets" (Henriksen, chapter 2, ebook page 118). One answer: yes. Schelling's logic, as Henriksen relays it, is that the anticipation of future damage coerces, so destroying the whole target set at once wastes leverage, and Kelche argued for hitting Milosevic's residences one at a time (ebook pages 117 to 118). The other answer: no. Milosevic already expected NATO to quit after five to seven days (ebook page 261), so saving targets fed his theory, and Ellis said incrementalism created sanctuaries the enemy exploited (Lambeth, chapter 6, page 227). The second answer is stronger, because a threat of future pain only works if the target believes there will be a future.
  3. Should Clark have relieved Short? Henriksen writes that "there is a fine line between the CFACC signaling his view" honestly and causing friction (Henriksen, chapter 10, ebook page 332). One answer: no. Giving his professional view is the air commander's job, and when ordered to make the Third Army the main objective, Short said he expected to do poorly and then did his best (ebook pages 331 to 332). The other answer: yes. Short sighed and signaled disapproval in video conferences for the whole war, and General Stöckmann said Vicenza ignored political guidance and NATO principles (ebook page 332). The first answer is stronger, since in Henriksen's account Short refused no order, only to hide his mood.
  4. Should NATO have threatened Milosevic at all in 1998? Henriksen says NATO "coerced itself into issuing ever more threats" (Henriksen, chapter 10, ebook page 333). One answer: no, not until it could act. Naumann's lesson from Determined Falcon was never to threaten what you cannot execute the next day or so, and Judah says that by March 1999 there was no way back short of a humiliating climb down (ebook pages 243 to 244). The other answer: yes. Tony Lake says diplomacy divorced from power has very rarely worked (ebook page 324), and Naumann told Henriksen that faced with people being killed and expelled, you act on a vague hope rather than tolerate it (ebook page 340). The first answer is stronger on Henriksen's evidence, because each empty threat raised the price of the next one. (S)
The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Henriksen · extrapolation

A Threat You Can Carry Out Tomorrow

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran, and his working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. What follows is our extrapolation from Henriksen, and not Shaun's answer.

Henriksen supports the answer at its foundation. He says the airpower community needs more resources and education on coercive strategy at the low end of the intensity spectrum, and he quotes Dennis Drew that the Air Force treats low-intensity conflict as a small conventional war. A patrolled strait after a withdrawal is exactly the low end. Naumann's rule from Determined Falcon helps too: never threaten what you cannot execute the next day or so. Eighty aircraft that could not strike for forty-eight hours told Milosevic everything. A persistent sensor-and-drone presence is, in principle, a threat that is already on station.

He cuts against it in two ways. First, presence is not coercion. Henriksen defines coercive diplomacy as changing an opponent's behavior by manipulating costs and benefits, and Determined Falcon's lesson is that showing aircraft proves only that you own aircraft. If the drones watch and never act, Tehran learns what Milosevic learned. Second, credibility binds the coercer. Once you threaten, Clark says, following through becomes a vital interest, and NATO threatened itself into a corner. A sensor network that reports a mining attempt creates a decision the next morning, and a coalition of reluctant partners has to make it. (Naumann's knife-wielding murderer has a maritime cousin in the small boat.)

Opinion, extrapolated: Henriksen would approve the sensors and ask what they are for. Which behavior in Tehran do they change, and who has agreed in advance to act on what they see? (S)

The Readings So Far · Henriksen, Lambeth, Olsen, Clodfelter, and Fravel

Every Airman's Favorite War Keeps Getting Cited

Desert Storm is the precedent that misled the most people on our shelf. Clark called the Gulf War air power's "persistent reference point," and Henriksen says Warden's Instant Thunder promised victory over Iraq in six to nine days. Olsen showed that only 2 percent of the strikes went at Warden's inner ring, and Lambeth showed the strategic work was done against Iraq's army. By 1999 officials expected Serbia to take two to four days. (Each retelling shaved a few days off the estimate, which is not how wars work.)

Henriksen and Clodfelter are describing the same shape thirty years apart. Clodfelter lists among Rolling Thunder's early aims to signal Hanoi the firmness of American resolve, and Henriksen's France wanted to save the best targets so Milosevic would fear the future. Short told the Senate he was a child of Vietnam. Fravel completes the circle from the other side: the PLA watched this war and concluded air strikes were the likeliest way China would be attacked. The audience took the campaign more seriously than its planners did.

Recent news keeps landing here. Yesterday's budget item paired a dying reconciliation bill with Lambeth's warning about affordability. Last night's page set the Senate Democrats' leadership fight beside Henriksen's NATO ambassador, who worried more about home capitals than about Milosevic. Opinion: caucuses and alliances fight the same first battle, which is with themselves. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 4, Day 2 · using evidence and evaluating sources

Milosevic Said It, According to a Man Who Was in the Room

Every piece of evidence in a paper is really a report of evidence, and the reader deserves to know how many hands it passed through. Booth, Colomb, and Williams make this point in The Craft of Research: you rarely hold the evidence itself, only someone's report of it, and "data you take from a source have invariably been shaped by that source." Their checklist for reliability asks who published it, whether experts reviewed it, whether the author is reputable, whether it is current, and whether a book has notes so you can follow its claims back.

Henriksen is a good model because he shows his chain. Milosevic's famous line that NATO would bomb for five to seven days comes to us through Milo Djukanovic, Montenegro's president, who attended the meeting. Before: "Milosevic expected a week of bombing." After: "According to Djukanovic, who attended, Milosevic told his Supreme Defense Council that NATO would bomb for about five to seven days and then be forced to stop." The second version tells the reader what to trust and how much. Henriksen goes further when he can't confirm something. He says plainly that he could not verify a Belgrade editor's claim about Holbrooke, or Naumann's story about Robertson and Guthrie, and that Kelche's account of Clark's targets is unconfirmed by Clark. On Racak he sets Walker's verdict, reached within minutes with little or no forensic competence, against the Finnish forensic team's findings.

Opinion: a source that admits what it can't prove earns more trust for everything it doesn't flag.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: for each key claim in your paper, count the removes. Write "Milosevic (1), via Djukanovic (2), via Henriksen (3)" in the margin. If a load-bearing claim is four removes out, find a closer source or say so in the sentence. (S)

Leadership × Henriksen · via War on the Rocks, November 16

Short Didn't Resign, and Neither Did the Argument

Quiet resignation is a narrow tool, and officers are being told to use it too broadly, Joshua Braver argues in War on the Rocks. Braver, a visiting senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute and a law professor at Wisconsin, describes the officer's usual two bad options when facing an objectionable order. Disobeying a lawful order risks court-martial under Article 90, with up to five years' confinement in peacetime. Resigning in protest risks undermining civilian control. Quiet resignation, leaving without stating the reason, looks like a third way. He says it has three limits: it doesn't cover illegal orders, which officers should resist; Peter Feaver's case for it depends on flag rank; and resignation is often not legally available. Active-duty service obligations bind at least 26.8 percent of Army officers by his conservative count, officers have no right to resign, and processing takes nine to twelve months. (The order, presumably, will not wait for the paperwork.)

Henriksen gives the case Braver's framework leaves out, the commander who disagrees with a lawful order and stays. When Clark made Serbia's Third Army in Kosovo the primary objective, Short said, "General, that is a high level of effort, high risk and low payoff operation. We will do our best but I do not expect to do very well." Then he flew it. He also sighed through video conferences for seventy-eight days, and SHAPE's chief of staff, General Stöckmann, said Vicenza ignored political guidance. Henriksen's judgment is careful: giving his view is the air commander's job, but there is a fine line between signaling it honestly and causing friction over time. Clark says he never considered relieving him.

Opinion: Short shows the option Braver's article assumes but doesn't discuss, which is dissent inside the chain, documented and argued, followed by execution. It's the hardest option, because you have to stay and keep losing the argument in front of your staff. Short got the dissent right and the tone wrong. Henriksen's other lesson applies to the commander above him: Clark might have spent more time on the disagreement before the war started. Most leadership problems, like most air campaigns, are cheaper to address before night one. (S)

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

    546 Navy and 730 Fifth Air Force sorties destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential in four days (119).

  • Zhang: what did Zhou Enlai tell a Soviet general about China's air force in Korea?

    China could endure losing half a million to a million soldiers, "but we will not sacrifice our newly built air force" (116).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how did January 1973's agreement differ from October's?

    "Largely cosmetic" differences (ch. VI, e217).

  • Randolph: how does he score the Easter Offensive for Hanoi?

    "A strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome" (339).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

  • Haun: how much Iraqi armor and artillery did airstrikes destroy before the ground war?

    About 10 percent; desertions accounted for most of the 25 to 30 percent attrition (272).

  • Kitfield: what was the U.S. Army's 1976 doctrine called, and what did it warn?

    "Active defense," in FM 100-5; it warned the Army to "fight outnumbered and win" (192).

  • Olsen: what share of the air effort went against Warden's inner ring?

    2 percent; 740 of 43,123 strikes hit the "central nervous system" (167, 295).

  • Fravel: who started eight of China's nine strategies since 1949?

    Senior military officers, subject only to the chairman's approval (ebook 429).

  • Lambeth: what role does he forecast for land power against large-scale aggression?

    "To secure a win rather than to achieve it" (320–321).

  • Henriksen: what did key leaders expect from the bombing, and why?

    Two to four days would move Milosevic, because they believed Deliberate Force had done so in Bosnia in 1995 (ebook 13).

Writing drills

  • Show the source: "Racak was a massacre."

    "Helena Ranta, who led the Finnish forensic team, found the bullets came from a small number of weapons and the bodies had not been moved, and Human Rights Watch concluded a massacre had taken place" (Henriksen, ebook 281–282). Named investigators beat an unattributed verdict (Booth et al., 135–136).

  • Flag the unverified claim: "Holbrooke promised Milosevic he would close the Albanian border."

    "According to Braca Grubacic, a Belgrade newsletter editor quoted by Tim Judah, Milosevic believed Holbrooke had promised to close the border; Henriksen could not verify the claim" (Henriksen, ebook 302).

  • Review (Monday, active voice): fix "Targets were approved one by one."

    "Washington introduced a target-by-target approval requirement, and France soon demanded to see the lists" (Henriksen, ebook 48). Name who did it (Sword, 49).

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

Write it yourself. The "Your turn" box matters most. Producing the paragraph teaches more than reading mine.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
