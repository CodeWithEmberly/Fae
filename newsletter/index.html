Tomorrow's Reading · Thursday, October 29

Zhang Flips the Map: The Korean Air War from North of the Yalu

Tomorrow's seminar trades the view from the B-29 for the view from the MiG. Xiaoming Zhang's Red Wings over the Yalu tells the Korean air war from the Communist side, and mostly from the Chinese side, using newly opened Chinese and Russian archives and oral histories from the Chinese air force. He isn't shy about the goal. He says plainly that the book should significantly modify conventional wisdom about the Korean War. An author who announces that in the introduction is either very confident or about to be very interesting. Zhang manages both.

He pulls three threads: China's ideas about airpower and its rush to build an air force from nothing, the odd shape of Soviet help, and how Chinese air operations actually went. Running under all three is an alliance that was less than it looked. Moscow and Beijing were allies, but each used the war for its own national interests, and the Soviets held back to avoid a direct clash with the United States.

Here's the bridge from yesterday's book. Crane spent his pages showing Washington putting limits on American airmen. Zhang's twist is that the American and Soviet air operations were limited mainly by politics, while the Chinese air force was limited by inexperience. China had no aviation industry and depended on Soviet equipment, and its leaders were veterans of ground war with no real idea of air warfare. Opinion: that's the course's second theme in a single comparison. The Americans had the capability and were told not to use all of it. The Chinese had the permission and had to build the capability, at war, in a hurry.

Read for four things. First, whose numbers to trust. Zhang warns that the kill ratios he reports are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent what really happened, and that the two sides' combat reports rarely match. He's just as blunt about the American side, calling U.S. victory claims probably highly inflated. Second, Stalin. Zhang calls the Soviet offer of air support a cryptic promise, and the October telegram in which Stalin asked for Chinese troops said nothing about air support at all. Meanwhile the Chinese 13th Army Group was telling Beijing that air support was a requirement, and proposed delaying the intervention if the Soviets couldn't guarantee it. Third, doctrine. Chinese air planners saw fighters as defensive weapons and bombers as aggressive ones, which fit China's defensive outlook and pushed them toward a fighter force. Fourth, the lesson China kept: by Zhang's account, the Korean War experience limited the Chinese air force to an air defense role.

If time is short tonight, read the introduction and the conclusion first, then chapter three on Stalin, Mao, and the promise of air cover. Skim chapters six and seven for the covert Soviet air war and the Chinese air force in combat. Opinion: seminars are won in the conclusion more often than in chapter five. (S)

The 628 Paper · through tomorrow's author

What Would Zhang Make of Your 628 Paper? Start with Taiwan

The prompt, for anyone who has been avoiding it: what is the most important potential strategic crisis facing the nation today, and, based on your historical understanding of the Age of Limited War, how should Airmen prepare to face it? One crisis only. The syllabus quotes Highlander on that point: there can be only one. Its examples are an Iranian nuclear test, a Chinese invasion of Taiwan, a Russian invasion of a NATO member, and the dissolution of an alliance. Ten pages, due December 1. What follows isn't an answer. It's a guess at how tomorrow's author would read the question.

Zhang would probably start where his own story starts, which is Taiwan. By his account, the Chinese Communists' urgent desire for an air force came primarily from Mao's plan to invade Taiwan. Mao asked Stalin's representative for Soviet air and naval support for the "liberation of Taiwan," and Zhou Enlai wrote of forming an offensive air unit to support the cross-strait campaign. The first operational mission assigned to the new air force was support for that projected campaign. Then Korea happened, and by Zhang's reckoning the war left the Chinese air force in an air-defense role.

Opinion from here, and an extrapolation rather than anything Zhang wrote. His lens suggests three cautions for an Airman writing about a Taiwan crisis. First, an air force carries its founding purpose. Taiwan isn't a new mission for the Chinese air force; it's the reason the service was created. Second, alliances. Zhang shows Moscow and Beijing each using Korea for its own national interests, with Stalin holding back from a direct clash with the United States. Any thesis about a future crisis should ask what each side's partners would actually risk, and the honest answer is rarely "everything." Third, numbers. If the two sides' combat reports in 1950 rarely matched, be wary of any force-ratio argument that rests on one side's figures.

There's a fourth caution, and it cuts closer to home. Zhang argues the Chinese air force of 1950 was limited mainly by inexperience, while the Americans were limited mainly by politics. Opinion: inexperience is the limit that time erodes. Political limits tend to persist. A paper that assumes the adversary is still the 1950 version, while Washington's restraints have somehow vanished, has the history backwards. The same alliance lesson applies if you pick Iran or a NATO crisis instead. Zhang won't write your paper, but he'd ask whose history your thesis quietly assumes. (S)

The Readings So Far · Gaddis and Crane

The Story So Far: Gaddis Set the Budget, Crane Sent the Bill

Two books in, the course already has a plot. John Lewis Gaddis defines strategy as the process by which ends are related to means, and his history of containment swings between two approaches. The symmetrical version meets the adversary wherever it pushes. The asymmetrical version picks its own ground. George Kennan's original version was asymmetrical. He counted only five centers of industrial and military power that mattered: the United States, Great Britain, Germany and central Europe, the Soviet Union, and Japan. Only one was in hostile hands, so the job was to keep the other four from falling, defended as strongpoints rather than along a perimeter.

Truman's people liked that approach for a reason that had little to do with elegance. They were convinced the country had only limited resources, however dangerous the threat. Truman framed it as spending twenty or thirty billion dollars to keep the peace against a hundred billion to fight a war. Dean Acheson told the Senate that recovery was the first objective. The atomic monopoly made the bargain feel safe; James Forrestal called the years before anyone could strike back "our years of opportunity." Kennan's own first rule was that interests come first and threats are measured against them, never the reverse. He then managed to undercut himself in print. Arthur Krock revealed him as the author of the anonymous "X" article, Walter Lippmann took it apart at length, and careless drafting left Kennan agreeing in places more with Lippmann than with his own essay. The article's call for "unalterable counter-force at every point" reads like perimeter defense, from the man who invented the strongpoint.

Conrad Crane picks up where the money and the bomb meet Korea. His subject is "the image and reality of American airpower," and the gap between the two is the whole book. Leaders expected too much, partly because the Air Force didn't admit its limits. FEAF's General Smart put it bluntly: "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Washington drew the lines anyway. Air action was held south of the 38th Parallel until Truman approved strikes in the North on June 29, 1950, with orders to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders. After FEAF bombed Rashin, about twenty miles from the Soviet border, the Joint Chiefs stopped further attacks there in September. In November, the Yalu bridge attacks were limited to the Korean side of the spans.

Interdiction didn't save the day. Fifth Air Force intelligence estimated that as many as 500,000 soldiers and civilians were working to keep enemy transportation routes open. An April 1952 FEAF study found the rail campaign had not worked despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed, and proposed "air pressure" instead. The June 1952 hydroelectric strikes then knocked out about ninety percent of North Korea's electric power. As for the bomb that made containment feel cheap: Attlee rushed to Washington after Truman's November 30 press conference, Truman sent nuclear weapons toward Okinawa in April 1951, and the HUDSON HARBOR test missions convinced the Air Staff that Korea offered no suitable atomic targets.

Opinion: put the two books side by side and the plot writes itself. Gaddis's planners counted on the bomb to make cheap containment safe. Crane's airmen found the bomb had nothing to hit and the war had limits nobody wrote down. A 1953 FEAF study blamed policy makers for never stating limited-war objectives "compatible with air force capabilities." Kennan would have called that skipping step one. And because the war ended ambiguously, Crane argues, the Air Force could believe it had succeeded, went back to planning for general war, and paid for it in Vietnam. Two books, one lesson: the side that never defines its interest ends up defending its budget. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 1, Day 3

"This What?" and "How Many?": Two Questions That Fix Most Drafts

Col Hollon's editing handout has a search list that every SAASS draft should survive: this, these, those, that, many, most, some, several, would, and often. His point about "this" is simple. When it opens a sentence, it almost always points back at the sentence before. Either fold the two sentences together or put a noun after "this." His point about the rest is sharper. Words like "many" and "most" try to sound significant while committing to nothing. How many? Is most fifty-point-one percent? How do you know?

Here's a paragraph any of us could have written at eleven last night. "Many sorties were flown against the railways, but this did not stop the enemy. Some people thought interdiction was not working. This led to a new strategy." Three sentences, and not one fact an instructor could check. Now the same paragraph with the vague words replaced by the book. "FEAF's rail campaign produced more than 15,000 rail cuts at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed, yet an April 1952 FEAF study concluded that interdiction had not worked. Fifth Air Force intelligence estimated that as many as 500,000 soldiers and civilians were working to keep enemy transportation routes open. The same study proposed a replacement: air pressure through selective destruction."

Look at what changed. "Many sorties" became two numbers. "Some people" became a named study with a date. Each "this" became the thing it was pointing at. The paragraph got more persuasive without getting any longer, which is the best trade in writing.

The pro tip: run Hollon's list with Ctrl+F on your next draft, and at every hit ask "which one?" or "how many?" If you can answer, write the answer in. If you can't, you haven't found a style problem. You've found a gap in your research, and the fix is back in the book. Opinion: vague words feel safe because nobody can prove them wrong. That's also why they never prove you right. (S)

Nebraska Is Running the Strongpoint Play

Electoral-vote.com's Senate table on Tuesday had an odd row for Nebraska. A Concord POP poll taken October 15 through 18 had Republican incumbent Pete Ricketts at 41 percent and independent Dan Osborn at 48, and the Democratic column read "No Democrat." EV's whole reaction was one word: "Wow."

Opinion from here. Whatever the reason for the empty column, the effect is Kennan's strongpoint defense in electoral form. A party that can't win a seat under its own label stops contesting it head-on and lets someone else hold the line. Nobody announces that kind of concession. It just shows up as an empty column in a poll table.

Compare Michigan on the same page. YouGov had Democrat Abdul El-Sayed at 48 percent and Republican Mike Rogers at 46, a race EV says has a very small margin for error. That's the symmetrical fight, with both parties all in and matching dollar for dollar. Kennan's rule was to define interests first and judge threats against them. For a party, the interest is the Senate majority, and which seats are worth defending follows from that, not from which ones it fought for last time. One empty row in a poll table can say more about party strategy than a week of press releases. (S)

850 Jets, Millions of Drones, and the Perception of Means

Last week the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin announced a ten-year framework for at least 850 F-35s. That's about 85 a year, which matches the fiscal 2027 request. Congress funded 47 in fiscal 2026. The deal depends on congressional approval and funding, and the stopgap law running through December 11 generally bars new multiyear buys. A week earlier, Defense Secretary Hegseth said the Pentagon wants fifty billion dollars to grow its stockpile of autonomous weapons from thousands of units to millions.

Opinion: put those side by side and the department is betting on both the exquisite fighter and the cheap swarm. That only works if the money grows to cover both. Gaddis found that Truman-era strategy rested on the opposite belief, that resources were limited no matter how dangerous the threat. The gap between 85 requested and 47 funded is the whole debate in two numbers. One more figure: GAO found the F-35's mission-capable rate fell from 67 percent in fiscal 2021 to 44 percent in fiscal 2025, and Lockheed argues steady multiyear orders will help. Buying predictability is a reasonable answer to a readiness problem. It's a weaker answer to a strategy that hasn't chosen between jets and drones. If the money grows, nobody will remember this item. If it doesn't, someone will have to choose, and Congress has already voted once: 47. (S)

Leadership · via Military Times

Change for What? Petraeus, Kennan, and the Order of Operations

Retired General David Petraeus told a Washington audience on October 16 that the United States and NATO need "urgent revolutionary institutional change" to prepare for modern war. He wants it to reach force structure, training, procurement, personnel policy, and bases. He would swap much of the heavily manned legacy force for cheaper, more numerous unmanned systems, and he wants production that can push software changes weekly. He called the new Autonomous Warfare Command a commendable step.

Opinion: he may well be right, and Ukraine is a strong body of evidence. The leadership lesson is in what the report leaves out. By Military Times' account, Petraeus named no specific adversary or interest the change should serve. Kennan's first rule ran the other way: threats only mean something when measured against your interests. For anyone leading change at any echelon, say what the change protects before you say what it replaces. People will put up with a revolution in "how" much longer if they can repeat the "why" from memory. Interest, then threat, then reorganization. Skip the first step and the reorganization becomes the goal. Kennan would add one more: say what the change does not require. He learned that one the hard way. (S)

Crane: seminar recall

  • Who limited airpower in Korea, and how?

    Washington, step by step: air action south of the 38th Parallel until June 29, 1950, then the North with the Soviet and Manchurian borders off-limits (23–24); no more attacks near Rashin after September 1950 (44–45); Yalu bridges hit only on the Korean side (49–50).

  • What was Truman afraid of?

    A wider, possibly global war. Attlee rushed to Washington after the November 30, 1950 press conference (58–59). In April 1951 Truman sent nuclear weapons and SAC bombers toward Okinawa, though the bombers stayed on Guam (70).

  • Did airpower achieve its objectives?

    Partly. Interdiction disappointed (82–83). The April 1952 study found more than 15,000 rail cuts hadn't worked, at a cost of 243 aircraft, and launched air pressure (115–116). The hydroelectric strikes cut about 90 percent of the North's power (119–120). Air superiority was "in jeopardy" in September 1951 (83–84).

  • Why no atomic bomb?

    HUDSON HARBOR found long delays and no suitable targets (71). Weyland opposed atomic use over the Manchurian airfields and the propaganda risk (157–158).

  • What interservice fights did airpower start?

    Weyland said he fought the Navy as much as the Communists, and Smart spent 40 percent of his time on Army and Navy proposals (7). Close air support: about 10 minutes for the Marines versus about 45 for the USAF (61–62).

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did Kennan say mattered, and why did Truman like it?

    Five centers of industrial and military power, defended as strongpoints (29, 57). It fit an administration convinced its resources were limited (57–58).

  • Crane: name two Washington limits on airpower in 1950.

    No more attacks near Rashin after September 1950 (44–45), and the Yalu bridges hit only on the Korean side (49–50).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
