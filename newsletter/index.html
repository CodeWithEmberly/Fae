Counterpoint · Nguyen against Clodfelter

Counterpoint: Eleven Days That Won the Signing and Lost the Enforcement

Nguyen is right and Clodfelter is too generous about Linebacker II: the Christmas bombing bought Nixon a signature and spent the air power that was supposed to enforce it. Clodfelter credits the campaign with real coercion. Nguyen agrees it hurt Hanoi and then shows what it cost Washington afterward. For a course about limited war, what came afterward is the part that counts.

Start with what the bombing changed on paper, which was very little. Clodfelter himself says the January agreement's differences from the October accord were "largely cosmetic." Nguyen reports the same argument from officials and scholars: the bombing only got Washington to accept terms it had rejected. (Eleven days, 3,420 sorties, and the edits fit in a footnote.) Then look at the clock. On January 2, 1973, the House Democratic Caucus voted 154 to 75 to cut off funds for the war, and the Senate caucus followed two days later. Nguyen's verdict is that "rather than break the will of Hanoi, American bombs mobilized the international community" against Washington, and that afterward "Nixon could no longer utilize American air power in Vietnam."

Nixon had staked the peace on that air power. On January 5, he promised Thieu that the United States would "respond with full force" if Hanoi broke the settlement. An American negotiator told Clodfelter that the threat of renewed bombing "was implied in all that we signed with Hanoi." The threat was the enforcement clause, and the bombing had already used it up. Zhou Enlai saw it in January: "The most important [thing] is to let the Americans leave," he told Le Duc Tho, because the situation would change in six months or a year. It took a little longer. North Vietnamese tanks entered Saigon on April 30, 1975.

Clodfelter's best shot is strong, and part of it lands. Linebacker II did not run alone; it followed nine months of bombing. General Tran Van Tra later said of his units, "The troops were no longer capable of fighting." Hanoi offered to resume talks on December 26, and Clodfelter writes that the havoc "prevented Hanoi from achieving an eleventh-hour victory," meaning the chance to lock in October's terms while Thieu balked and Congress came back. Even Nguyen concedes that "Nixon's bombs had done their damage." Judged by Nixon's narrow aim, which was to get a signed agreement and the prisoners home before Congress cut the money, the campaign worked. We concede that in full.

It still loses, because a limited war is judged by the peace that follows, and Linebacker II made that peace unenforceable. First order, Hanoi signed. Second order, the outcry and the funding votes took the bombers off the table, so Hanoi could rebuild and wait. Third order, the Air Force learned the wrong lesson. Clodfelter's own conclusion says air leaders "parade Linebacker II as proof that bombing will work in limited war," when it was a unique campaign for very limited ends. On that point Clodfelter has already joined Nguyen's side, so the remaining argument is about the first week of January.

What would change our mind? Evidence that Hanoi delayed its final offensive because it still feared the B-52s after 1973. Nguyen shows it was waiting for Washington's patience to run out, as Zhou advised, not for the bombers to come back. The line for seminar: "Linebacker II got the signature and spent the enforcement; Zhou told Hanoi to let the Americans leave, and Hanoi did exactly that." In plain terms, coercion that uses up its own credibility only buys the signing, and Shaun's Hormuz hold faces the same clock. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Sekirin · extrapolation

Time Is on the Side of Whoever Copies Faster

Sekirin would tell Shaun that time works for whichever side adapts faster, and a hold does not make that side Washington by default. The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the Iran war without a settlement, and his thesis is that Airmen should prepare now to hold the strait the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Sekirin, not Shaun's answer, and it tests the first point: the enemy plays the clock.

Sekirin's war shows the clock running both ways at the front. Ukraine opened Kursk in August 2024 with no more than 100 drone crews and blinded Russian reconnaissance with interceptors and jammers. Sekirin says that force proved insufficient once Russia brought in its own drone and counter-drone units. By February 2025 Russia had massed drones against the Sumy to Sudzha highway, which he says mimicked what Ukraine had done at Kursk the previous August. Many of the Russian drones ran on fibre-optic cables that jamming cannot touch, and they cut Ukraine's supply road. (Imitation is the sincerest form of flattery, and the cheapest form of R&D.)

He also names the failure that lets the clock turn. Russia first used fibre-optic drones in March 2024, he writes, and Ukraine "was slow to adapt," both in building its own and in countering them, and paid for it with a period of disadvantage. His prescription is to watch the enemy's drone programme closely and keep a technological edge. Russia alone, he notes, produces around one million new drones a year. Tehran, for the record, is the original supplier in this story: Sekirin's Ukrainians built long-range drones to match Iran's Shahed 136.

Opinion, extrapolated: Sekirin supports the hold's footprint and cuts against its assumption about time. A Korea-style hold works against Tehran only if the Americans and Gulf partners out-adapt an Iranian drone industry that will spend the hold learning. Two questions for the draft:

  1. Does the small footprint include a refresh cycle, meaning who updates the drones and countermeasures every few months, and with what money?
  2. Who watches Tehran's drone programme for the next fibre-optic surprise, and is that job inside the hold or outside it? (S)

Seven Trucks, a $500 Drone, and a Very Expensive Dollar

In every limited war on this shelf, the side that needed less had the better arithmetic. Clodfelter's Communist battalions fought about one day in thirty and needed only 34 tons a day from outside the South, a load seven two-and-a-half-ton trucks could carry. Rolling Thunder, meanwhile, cost the United States $6.60 for every $1.00 of damage in 1965 and $9.60 in 1966. (If you were selling bombs at that exchange rate, you would be asked to leave the bazaar.)

Haun shows the same arithmetic on the Trail. The DIA and Seventh Air Force said Commando Hunt I cut supplies by 82 percent. The CIA said the enemy needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day and got them, and Haun rules that both were correct. A campaign can destroy most of what moves and still miss the small amount that matters.

Forty years later, cheapness changed sides. Fuller calls the CIA's drone war "relatively cheap, in U.S. lives as well as cash," and that was the American advantage. In Sekirin's Ukraine, the drones that force a kinetic response cost around $500 each, and the defender is the one paying exquisite prices to stop them. Today's news item from Colombia shows where that leads: the same arithmetic now works for armed groups, who have used drones to kill soldiers on their own bases. Tuesday's item on AI-graded wargames belongs on the same shelf. Gaddis's sortie counts and Schlight's tonnage tables measured the expensive side's effort, never the cheap side's needs.

Our take: before any campaign, ask what the enemy actually needs per day, and what each unit of your effort costs against it. Clodfelter's seven trucks are the oldest lesson in this course. They have since been replaced by a quadcopter, and the bill still lands on the side with the bigger budget. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 5, Day 3 · precise language

Jargon Marks the Spot

A term of art is precise only if you can say concretely what it covers. Helen Sword, in Stylish Academic Writing, finds that stylish academic writers use plenty of concrete nouns and vivid verbs, "especially when discussing abstract concepts." She also quotes Marjorie Garber's warning that jargon becomes a verbal gesture in place of an idea: "Jargon marks the place where thinking has been." Sword's fix is Garber's own goal, language "at once precise and rich."

Drone writing is full of these gestures. "Battlefield transparency." "Counter-UAS capabilities." "The drone wall." Sekirin earns that last one, because he measures it. He writes that an invisible drone wall stands between attacker and defender in Ukraine, and that it is not a line but a zone up to 10 kilometres deep and growing as drone ranges increase. Before: "Drones have created battlefield transparency that makes offensive maneuver difficult." After: "Drones watch and strike a belt up to ten kilometres deep along the front, so any tanks that gather there are found and attacked before they can move."

The same test works on your own draft. "Airpower had a significant impact on Hanoi" names no cause and no effect. "Eleven days of B-52 raids got Hanoi back to the table by December 26" names both, and a reader can check it. (Vague sentences are popular for exactly that reason: nobody can check them.)

An old editor's trick: keep a Ctrl+F list of fog words and search for each before you submit. Start with "significant," "impact," "capabilities," "effective," "various," and "multi-domain." Every hit gets a number, a name, or a cut. Jargon is a promissory note. Pay it off in the same sentence or the reader will assume you can't. (S)

Defense & Conflict × Sekirin · via Breaking Defense, November 23

Colombia Shops for a Drone Shield. Sekirin Hands It a Warning Label.

Colombia's armed groups are now fighting with drones, and the government is shopping for a shield. Wilder Alejandro Sanchez reports in Breaking Defense that drones dropped explosives on a base in Santa Rosa del Sur earlier this month, killing three soldiers, and that a drone and explosives attack on a battalion headquarters in La Jagua de Ibirico killed one soldier and wounded at least twelve. Citing Caracol Radio, he counts at least 93 drone attacks in President Abelardo de la Espriella's first 100 days, and at least 11 of the 26 Army personnel killed in that time died from drone-dropped explosives. The defense minister says his predecessor's "anti-drone shield" hadn't amounted to much. A retired colonel wants air defenses built specifically for drones. Rebecca Bill Chavez of the Inter-American Dialogue says the goal should be "to dismantle those networks, not just shoot down drones once they're airborne."

Sekirin's book explains why the shield keeps disappointing. Drone engineers keep changing frequencies to beat jammers, he writes, and the new class of drones guided by fibre-optic cable needs a kinetic answer, against swarms that cost around $500 apiece. Breaking Defense reports that Colombia's groups have moved from watching troops to coordinated multi-drone strikes, including fibre-optic-guided systems. On the day of a November 8 weapons seizure, troops in Chocó were using jamming rifles. (A jamming rifle against a fibre-optic drone is a strongly worded letter.)

Sekirin's own answer, "fight fire with fire," does not transfer. He means drone and counter-drone forces at a front line, and the colonel himself says "There is no conventional drone warfare in Colombia." The part of Sekirin that does transfer is less famous. The launch sites and command centres of drone swarms, he writes, can rapidly disperse and give off little or no electronic signal. His list of lessons also says to monitor the enemy's drone programme closely. That is Chavez's point in Ukrainian terms: find the people and the supply before the drone is in the air.

Opinion: Chavez has it right, and Sekirin backs her. Buy point defense for the bases, because soldiers are dying, but spend the real money on intelligence against the suppliers. A shield gets bought once, and the network on the other side keeps adapting, so this year's shield is next year's museum piece. In plain terms, money spent finding the suppliers keeps paying off after the shield goes out of date. (S)

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

Writing drills

  • Find the fog word and fix it: "Russia effectively adapted its drone capabilities at Kursk."

    "In February 2025 Russia massed drones within seven kilometres of the Sumy to Sudzha highway, many on fibre-optic cables, and cut Ukraine's supply road (Sekirin, 177)." "Effectively" and "capabilities" become a date, a distance, and a result (Sword, 49).

  • Define the jargon in one clause: "the drone wall."

    "The drone wall, a belt up to ten kilometres deep in which drones watch and strike anything that moves (Sekirin, 54)." A term of art earns its place when the same sentence says what it covers (Sword, 114–115).

  • Review (Tuesday, sources): which of Adler's questions does this fail? "Linebacker II forced Hanoi to sign, as everyone agrees."

    Whether the reporter knows the facts, and what the source is trying to prove. "Everyone" is not a source, and the record disagrees: Clodfelter calls the changes "largely cosmetic" (ebook 217), and Nguyen says the bombs mobilized the world instead of breaking Hanoi (ebook 412) (Adler, 252–254).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
