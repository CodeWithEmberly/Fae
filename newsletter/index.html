Today's Seminar · Thursday, October 29, at noon

Red Wings, Red Ink: Zhang's Scoreboard Goes to Seminar

Today the course looks at a limited war from the far bank of the river for the first time, and the syllabus doesn't pretend that's comfortable. For the People's Republic, it says, Korea meant building a modern air force from the ground up a year after winning a civil war that had lasted decades, which is roughly like opening a restaurant the morning after the kitchen fire. It is still the Chinese air force's only major combat against a modernized military. Then the syllabus asks the questions that will hang over the room at noon:

  1. How do you judge a stalemate?
  2. What do you tell your Party, and even yourself?

Xiaoming Zhang's answer, boiled down: you tell them you won, then spend forty years believing it. The argument behind that is less flattering to everyone. Zhang says American and Soviet air operations were limited mainly by politics, while the Chinese air force was limited mainly by inexperience. China's first class of new pilots graduated in October 1950 with about sixty-three flying hours, against a standard of 150 to two hundred. Stalin's promise of air cover was never defined, so he never technically broke it, a piece of contract drafting that lawyers still admire. The MiG war over the Yalu was mostly a Soviet war, fought in planes with North Korean markings by pilots in Chinese uniforms with Chinese pseudonyms, who were told to speak Chinese on the radio. Few did. By the end, the Chinese air force was averaging fifty MiG sorties a day to the Americans' nine hundred, and even by Chinese reckoning the United States held air superiority for the entire war. That is a generous concession from the side that won.

Bring the syllabus questions, because Zhang answers most of them:

  1. How accurate are one side's statistics?
  2. Does survival count as victory?
  3. What did the alliance do?
  4. Is airpower inherently defensive?

On statistics, everyone graded their own homework. The Chinese claimed about 1.42 kills for every loss. The Soviets claimed 1,309 UN aircraft for 335 MiGs. The Americans claimed eight or ten to one, and some American accounts have since revised that to about two to one. Zhang's explanation is refreshingly unglamorous: American pilots got credit whenever the gun camera showed hits, while the Soviets assumed only 75 percent of filmed hits were actually kills. On survival, Zhang insists the Communist air effort was vital to keeping the supply lines open, even while the bombs fell at a rate of one for every seven meters of railroad. On the alliance, Moscow got a combat-tested MiG and an America pinned down in Asia. Beijing got a debt of three billion yuan, paid off in 1964, and the lesson that Stalin was an unreliable ally. We'll let the class decide who got the better end of that trade. On defense, Chinese planners saw fighters as defensive and bombers as aggressive, and the war left their air force in an air-defense role. That same air force was founded to support an invasion of Taiwan, which is about as offensive a mission as a brand-new service can be handed.

Now the connections. Conrad Crane's subject was the image and reality of American airpower, and Zhang hands us the matching set from the other side. Both air forces left Korea with a flattering self-portrait. Crane's Air Force went back to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam. Zhang's got its bill sooner: in 1954, the Chinese air force failed to spot 70 percent of the Nationalist aircraft intruding on its airspace, flown by an enemy its veterans believed was no real threat. Nobody had told the enemy. And for the Gaddis fans, there's Zhou Enlai telling a Soviet general that China could endure losing half a million to a million soldiers but would not sacrifice its newly built air force. That's ends and means at about the coldest temperature the course has recorded yet.

Opinion: the question worth pressing today is the last one on the syllabus list. Zhang's evidence suggests the Chinese air force went defensive because it was scarce, green, and dependent on a patron who wouldn't fly south of the river. That makes "defensive" a fair description of 1953 and a shaky forecast for anything after it. Taiwan was the founding mission, after all, and planners in Beijing now name it as a likely flashpoint. Both sides left Korea certain they had won the air war. Only one of them found out in 1954 that seven of every ten intruders were getting through unannounced. (S)

Till Debt Do Us Part: Zhang and the Alliance That Sent a Bill

The prompt, once more for the people in the back. One crisis only, ten pages, due December 1, and it asks two things:

  1. What is the most important potential strategic crisis facing the nation?
  2. Based on your historical understanding of the Age of Limited War, how should Airmen prepare to face it?

Yesterday we ran Zhang against a Chinese invasion of Taiwan. Today we try him on the syllabus's least cinematic example, the dissolution of an alliance. Nobody makes a movie about an alliance quietly coming apart. They should; the ending is usually expensive. What follows is our extrapolation from Zhang's evidence. He didn't write it, and it isn't an answer to the paper.

Zhang's book is, among other things, a case study of an alliance that held on paper and leaked everywhere else. Moscow and Beijing each used the war for its own national interests, he writes, and the Soviets held back to avoid a direct clash with the United States. Stalin offered air cover for Chinese troops in July 1950 and never said what it meant, which left him a great deal of room later. When Moscow finally ordered 208 jet fighters to China in October, its pilots were strictly forbidden to fly at the front. When they did fight over the Yalu, they stayed within about thirty miles of Andong and never flew in coordination with Chinese ground operations. Meanwhile, Beijing paid. The Chinese covered the living expenses of the Soviet advisers who trained them, the military equivalent of paying your tutor's rent. Moscow lent three hundred million dollars at one percent. When the shooting stopped, China owed three billion yuan, which it finished paying in 1964, along with the lesson that Stalin had been an unreliable ally. The lesson, at least, came free.

So what would Zhang's lens say to an Airman writing about an alliance in trouble? Opinion, from here on. First, alliances rarely dissolve in a single dramatic moment. They erode in the fine print, in the gap between "we will provide air cover" and the order that keeps the patron's pilots on its own side of the river. A paper about alliance dissolution should look for that gap now, in peacetime, before a crisis forces everyone to read the contract for the first time. Second, the junior partner's real question is what the senior partner will actually fly, where, and at what risk. The syllabus asks how a junior partner in an alliance had to compensate to reach its national goals, and Zhang's answer is that China compensated with concrete, manpower, and a very large invoice.

Third, for the planners: treat an ally's restraint as a planning factor. Stalin wasn't being fickle when he kept his MiGs near Andong. He was being consistent about the one thing he cared about most, which was not fighting the United States directly. An Airman who assumes partners will share every risk has written a hope and filed it as a plan. (We would note that RAND published a study this week titled "Anticipating Allies' Responses to U.S. Retrenchment from Europe." We haven't read it yet, so we'll say only that the title suggests somebody is already doing this homework, which puts them ahead of most of us.)

Zhang won't pick your crisis for you. He would remind you that the Korean War ended at the armistice in 1953, and China's bill for it ended in 1964. Wars get armistices. Invoices get payment schedules, and those run longer. (S)

Three Books, One River: The Yalu from Washington, Andong, and Moscow

Three books in, the course has shown us the same river from three banks. Start with John Lewis Gaddis, who defines strategy as relating ends to means. George Kennan's version counted five centers of industrial and military power that mattered, and Korea did not make the list. Truman's people believed their resources were limited no matter how dangerous the threat, and the atomic monopoly let them feel safe about it. So Gaddis handed us the budget and the map of what mattered, plus a bomb that was supposed to keep the bill small.

Conrad Crane showed what happened when that budget met an actual war. Washington drew the lines, and drew them with a fine pen. Truman approved strikes north of the 38th Parallel on June 29, 1950, with orders to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders, and in November the Yalu bridge attacks were limited to the Korean side of the spans, which takes some doing with a bridge. The bomb turned out to have nothing to hit: the HUDSON HARBOR test missions found no suitable atomic targets in Korea. Crane's subject was the gap between the image and the reality of American airpower, and the Air Force, in the words of FEAF's General Smart, didn't advertise its limitations. Few institutions do.

Now Zhang, and the symmetry is almost too neat. Moscow ordered its first fighter division in China to train Chinese pilots and fly no missions beyond the Yalu. Truman kept his pilots on the south side of the river; Stalin kept his on the north side. Two superpowers, one water line, the same reason. Zhou Enlai's refusal to sacrifice China's new air force, even at the cost of a million soldiers, is Kennan's ends-and-means discipline with a different accent. The scarcest asset gets protected first, whatever the communiqués say.

The news has been doing its part. Yesterday's Michigan Senate polls, YouGov at 48 to 46 and Glengariff at 51 to 45, were the kill-ratio problem with yard signs, and Zhang's own numbers finish the thought: an American claim of eight or ten to one that some American accounts now put nearer two to one. Pollsters disagree about a race in a single week; it took historians decades to disagree that much about a dogfight. Yesterday's Iran war receipt, more than 47 billion dollars with more than 29 billion of it for munitions, sat awkwardly next to Zhang's China, which had no aviation industry at all, and today's item on a missile plant in Japan picks up that thread. The Army's balloons, parked in international airspace above sixty thousand feet, are Crane's Korean side of the spans with better altitude.

Opinion: across all three books, limits get drawn by whoever has the most to lose, and they get drawn where both sides can see them. Tomorrow's book moves the contest somewhere nobody had drawn a line yet. The syllabus says the Space Race showed how the two superpowers competed when a hot war was off the table, which is a polite way of saying the Cold War found a new river and it ran straight up. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 1, Day 4 · the thesis and the argument

They Say Airpower Won; I Say Check the Gun Camera: On the Thesis

Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein built an entire book, They Say / I Say, around one move, and they say plainly it's the single most important template they teach: present your idea as a response to some other person or group. Your thesis, which they call the "I say" moment of your text, should always answer someone else's argument. Skip the "they say," they warn, and readers may understand what you're saying without ever understanding why you're saying it. Anyone who has turned in a paper that was perfectly clear and completely pointless knows the feeling, usually around the time grades come back.

They have a test case for the pointless kind. "William Shakespeare wrote many famous plays and sonnets" is a true sentence, they note, and precisely because nobody would disagree with it, there's no reason to say it. Playing it safe, in their view, produces flat, lifeless writing that never answers the questions "so what?" and "who cares?" Every seminar has its Shakespeare sentence. Here's ours: "The Korean air war was an important part of the Cold War, and both sides used airpower in many ways." It is true, nobody will argue with it, and the grader will be asleep by the comma.

Now give it a "they say." "For decades the dominant American account credited Sabre pilots with a kill ratio of about seven to one. Zhang's Chinese and Russian sources suggest the real ratio was far lower, and that the United States held air superiority anyway, which means the kill ratio was never the measure of success that mattered." Same war, but now one position is picking a fight with another, and the reader has a reason to stay. Graff and Birkenstein also suggest stating your view and the one it answers together, as a unit, near the start, with the summary kept brief so the reader gets a preview and is spared a pile of details. They recommend a move they call planting a naysayer: summarize the likeliest objection to your claim, then answer it. It's cheaper than letting the grader plant one for you. "Although it might be objected that both sides' numbers are propaganda, the more important point is that the side with the worse numbers still couldn't fly south of the river." They especially like answers that agree and disagree at once, which happens to be how most good seminar comments sound, and nearly every diplomatic communiqué.

Pro tip, from an old editor's bag of tricks: read the question, then immediately read your thesis. Those two or three sentences should make sense together, and if they don't, nothing on pages two through ten will save you. Try it on the 628 prompt, which asks for one crisis and how Airmen should prepare. A thesis that names three crises, or none, or prepares nobody for anything, fails the test before the grader reaches the second paragraph. The same editor has a corollary: the thesis is king, and every paragraph should say how it serves the crown. Courtiers who can't explain themselves get cut. Opinion: the thesis is the one sentence in your paper that's allowed to start a fight. Don't waste it on Shakespeare. (S)

We don't know who paid for it, and EV's reader didn't say. We would note only that when a mailer's return address and its message point in opposite directions, the voter is being invited to admire the uniform and skip the question of who's flying.

Five days out, there's a pattern. Campaigns of every stripe are borrowing someone else's face, voice, or label. Some of it is old-fashioned distancing and some of it is new-fashioned software, and all of it rests on the same bet: that the audience will judge the markings on the fuselage before it asks who is in the cockpit.

Today's book has the same costume party, run by the Kremlin with better tailoring. Moscow, Zhang writes, placed security restrictions on its pilots to conceal Soviet participation in Korea. Soviet planes were disguised with North Korean markings. Soviet pilots wore Chinese uniforms when they arrived in China, each had a Chinese pseudonym, and they were expected to speak Chinese on the radio in combat, although few ever did. Then comes the twist that turns theater into strategy: American Air Force leaders knew the MiGs were Soviet, Zhang says, and credited the Chinese anyway, to avoid stirring up public demand for a war with Russia.

The Korean disguise worked because both sides wanted it to work. Call it escalation control in a flight suit. Washington and Moscow each had a reason to keep the fiction alive, so it lasted the whole war, which is a longer run than most official fictions get. The campaign disguises of 2026 run the other way. Their targets object, loudly and with lawyers, which is why the cease-and-desist letters and lawsuit threats share a roundup with the ads.

Opinion: a disguise the other side refuses to accept is just a provocation with a production budget. The Soviet pilots were at least told to try speaking the language, and few managed it. The 2026 versions are fluent in everyone's voice except their own. (S)

Some Assembly Required: Japan Gets a Missile Plant, Two Years and One Constitution Later

At the ASEAN Defense Ministers' Meeting in the Philippines, Japan's defense minister, Shinjiro Koizumi, and Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth finalized an agreement to build a final assembly and inspection facility in Japan for the AIM-120 Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile, the AMRAAM. Koizumi called the missile essential for the F-35 and the deal "groundbreaking." Where the ground will be broken hasn't been announced. The effort began in July 2024, under both ministers' predecessors, and approval took more than two years, partly because Japan's postwar constitution and peace-focused policy restrict exporting lethal weapons and co-producing offensive weapons with allies. The missile's classified guidance and intellectual property needed safeguards too. The Pentagon's readout, we are given to understand, didn't mention the facility at all. It said Hegseth "welcomed Japan's efforts to strengthen its defense industrial capacity," which is one way to describe a missile plant.

The numbers behind the handshake are bigger than the ceremony. In late September the Pentagon and RTX announced a production contract worth up to 20.7 billion dollars, five years plus two option years, covering Lots 41 through 47 and foreign sales to 16 international partners. A February framework agreement calls for at least 1,900 missiles a year. For comparison, the Air Force's fiscal 2022 budget request sought only 186 AMRAAMs, and the Navy asked for none at all. From 2017 to 2022, combined purchases generally stayed at or below 400 a year. Those were simpler times.

Going from about 400 a year to 1,900 is close to a fivefold jump (our arithmetic, not RTX's). The company says it's "building faster than ever before," and we believe it, because it would have to. The article also lists what stands in the way: shortages of subcontractor components and long lead times for solid rocket motors, with new American rocket motor lines expected in 2027. Until then, a missile without a motor is a very expensive dart. Yesterday's Iran receipt put more than 29 billion dollars of the war's cost on replacing munitions already fired. You can see why somebody wanted a second factory, and why that somebody wanted it in the Pacific.

Today's book has the cautionary version. Zhang's China entered the air war with no aviation industry and no access to Western aircraft, so its air force depended on Soviet equipment from the start. The founding deal in August 1949 brought 434 aircraft and 878 Soviet personnel to staff six aviation schools, and the Chinese later complained about paying new prices for old machinery. When the MiG-9s arrived, a jet with short range and poor maneuverability, the Chinese suspected Moscow was dumping its retirees on them. Call it the first used-jet lot with a Politburo. A junior partner that can't build its own weapons fights on its patron's schedule, at its patron's prices, with its patron's leftovers.

Opinion: Japan's plant is Mao's problem solved in reverse. The syllabus asks how a junior partner in an alliance had to compensate for technological dependence to reach its national goals. Beijing compensated with manpower and debt. Tokyo is compensating by building the capacity on its own soil, with the patron's blessing and the patron's classified guidance under lock and key. That's the healthier version of an alliance, even if it took two years, one constitutional reinterpretation debate, and a readout that forgot to mention it.

In 1950, the ally shipped you old MiGs and sent the invoice. In 2026, the ally helps you build the missiles and still leaves them out of the press release. Progress, like a solid rocket motor, arrives on a long lead time. (S)

Leadership × Zhang · via Defense News, October 28

Class A, Grade F: The Navy's Worst Mishap Year in a Decade

The Navy Safety Command's latest numbers are out, and nobody will be framing them for the wardroom. In fiscal 2026 the Navy recorded 16 Class A aviation mishaps and 11 Class A afloat mishaps, the most unplanned catastrophic events involving aircraft and vessels in a decade. A Class A mishap, for the uninitiated, is one that causes 2.5 million dollars or more in damage, destroys an aircraft, or kills or permanently disables someone. It is one of the rare bureaucratic labels that undersells its contents. Several of this year's happened in the Middle East during the war with Iran, which began on February 28.

The list reads like a bad week stretched across a year. On July 1 an MH-60S Sea Hawk from the USS George H.W. Bush made a controlled crash in the Arabian Sea; three crew members were rescued and one went missing and was later presumed dead. The Navy said the crash was not a result of any hostilities. On March 12 a fire broke out in the main laundry room of the USS Gerald R. Ford in the Red Sea, took more than thirty hours to put out, injured three sailors, and wrecked sleeping space for more than six hundred, many of whom slept on floors and tables while the carrier went to Split, Croatia, for repairs. Few port calls are scheduled around the dryers. On September 28 an arresting gear failure aboard the Eisenhower, off Virginia, forced both pilots of an E/A-18G Growler to eject. (The article's body and its web address disagree about how many people were hurt, which is the kind of discrepancy that would get a staff officer a phone call.) And on July 1 an F/A-18F crew landed safely at Fallon, Nevada, after a left engine fire, the closest thing on this list to good news.

The article gives no causes beyond the Navy's statement that it is investigating, and no total cost, so we won't guess. A laundry fire, a helicopter, an arresting cable, and an engine aren't obviously the same problem. Still, twenty-seven Class A events in one year is a number that demands an answer, and the question it asks is whether a force running at wartime tempo is spending its safety margin faster than it can earn it back. Safety margin, unhelpfully, doesn't come with a monthly statement.

Today's book has a force that answered that question the hard way. Zhang's Chinese air force rushed its first class of pilots out of school in October 1950 with about sixty-three flying hours, against a standard of 150 to two hundred. In 1950 it admitted 3.35 serious accidents per ten thousand sorties, and its former chief of staff counted more than a hundred fatal crashes in the first half of 1951. Years later, the service conceded that more training hours would have meant fewer losses and probably more victories in Korea. The tempo had a price, and the receipt came long after the war.

Zhang has a second leadership lesson in the same book. By the Chinese air force's own records, five of its eight regimental commanders killed over Korea died because of their own brashness, a statistic no promotion board wants to read. And when the CPV Air Force's deputy commander, Duan Suquan, warned Peng Dehuai that even a one-to-one ratio against the Sabres was hard and urged leaders to stop exaggerating, the myth won anyway. Myths usually do; they have better publicists.

Opinion: the leader's job is to count accidents as honestly as kills, and to say the number out loud before somebody else has to. The Navy publishing its worst year in a decade is the right instinct, the one Duan had and his superiors ignored. The Chinese air force waited decades to admit what sixty-three hours had cost. Twenty-seven Class A mishaps is a bad number. Not publishing it would have been a worse one. (S)

Kim Takes the New Bridge to Andong, Where the MiGs Used to Live

On Saturday, October 24, Kim Jong Un made an unannounced day trip across the Yalu into the Chinese border city of Dandong, by way of the China-North Korea New Yalu River Bridge. Neither government announced it in advance. South Korea's Yonhap news agency broke the story, citing an unidentified government source, and China's Xinhua later confirmed that Wang Huning, China's top political adviser, met Kim and saw him off at the bridge, which is as close to a press release as the trip got. A South Korean lawmaker, Youn Kun-young, said Kim was understood to have met Premier Li Qiang. The bridge has a story of its own. According to the AFP report, it was finished in 2014 and opened officially only the week before the visit, about twelve years later, because North Korea lacked the roads and customs facilities to connect to it. Twelve years is a long time to wait for the other side to pave a driveway.

Why go? Nobody on the record knows. A former president of the University of North Korean Studies, Yang Moo-Jin, suggested Kim may have wanted to press China on economic commitments and line up Beijing's support before a possible meeting with President Trump, with world leaders preparing for the APEC summit in Shenzhen in November. CSIS thought the trip mattered enough to run an emergency episode of its podcast, The Impossible State, on Tuesday with Victor Cha and Sydney Seiler. In think-tank terms, an emergency podcast is scrambling the alert jets. Its event page frames the trip as Kim's first confirmed visit to China in over a year, possibly aimed at trade and economic ties. We haven't heard the recording, so that is as far as we'll go. ISW's Korean Peninsula Update for October 28 is out as well, though its website declined to let our reader in. We are trying not to take it personally.

On the south side of the line, the conversation is less diplomatic. At a conference in Honolulu on Tuesday, Gen. Xavier Brunson, who commands U.S. Forces Korea, said American and allied electronics are jammed daily on the peninsula, which he called a "violently contested electromagnetic environment." Adversaries jam GPS in the West Sea, he said, and try to blind American servers, sensors, and networks. A system that needs pristine satellite links and unlimited bandwidth, he said, "will not survive 48 hours on the peninsula," which is a tough review for a product demo. His prescription for industry was combined from the start: "We must design combined from the first line of code," with security that tags the data instead of guarding the pipe. He also asked companies, in a pickle metaphor we won't attempt to improve on, "What is your sliced pickle?"

Dandong is familiar ground for anyone reading today's book: under its old name of Andong, it is where Zhang's air war lived. In August 1950, before China had even entered the war, American planes bombed the railroad station and strafed the airfield at Andong, the border city on the Chinese side of the Yalu where the 13th Army Group had its headquarters. Later the Soviets' main job was defending the airfield and the bridges at Andong. Their pilots stayed within about thirty miles of the city and never flew in coordination with Chinese ground operations. And the Chinese pilots they trained learned through interpreters who didn't know aviation terminology, which turned flight school into a game of telephone with jets.

Opinion: Brunson's "combined from the first line of code" is the lesson that 1950's alliance never learned. Moscow and Beijing fought the same war over the same city with different pilots, different rules, and no shared plan with the troops on the ground. Seventy-six years later, the American commander's biggest worry is a coalition network that can't pass a missile track in milliseconds, and the North Korean leader's big diplomatic gesture is driving across a bridge that took a dozen years to open.

The bridges at Andong were once among the most fought-over spans in Asia. The new one waited twelve years for a road on the far side. Some limits are imposed by superpowers. Others are imposed by the paving budget. (S)

Today's seminar: Zhang, Red Wings over the Yalu, chapter by chapter

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 1

An Overnight Air Power, Forty Years in the Oven

Zhang opens with Gen. Hoyt Vandenberg, back from the Far East on November 21, 1951, telling the press that Communist China had become "one of the major air powers of the world." Air Force intelligence counted fourteen hundred Communist planes in Korea, seven hundred of them MiGs. How did China manage that overnight? Zhang's answer is that the night was a very long one. Sun Yat-sen preached "aviation as the nation's salvation" from 1911, read like Douhet and Mitchell, and concluded that no air force means no national defense. His aviation school had ten students and two Curtiss trainers. The Nationalists did better on paper: more than seven hundred pilots and six hundred aircraft by the end of 1936, over a thousand aircraft by 1945. By mid-1947 fewer than three hundred could fly, and a monopoly on airpower did not keep them on the mainland. Paper, as usual, flew better than the airplanes.

The Communist thread is thinner and more stubborn. Forty-three Red Army officers trained in Xinjiang under a Soviet-backed warlord, wearing Nationalist uniforms and pseudonyms, after someone thoughtfully waived the literacy test. They built up about three hundred hours each, and then the warlord switched sides in 1942 and arrested 160 Communist personnel, pilots included. The Northeast Aviation School opened on March 1, 1946, with captured Japanese aircraft and Japanese instructors. Cadets ate cornbread and pickles, burned purified alcohol when gasoline ran out, and tied their coats shut with rope in flight, none of which made the recruiting poster. By July 1949 it had trained 126 pilots. In 1947 the Soviet-trained faction won the argument over whose system to copy, and Mao kept insisting that brave men could beat machines, a theory nobody ran past the machines.

The best scene is the Red Army's first combat aircraft. In February 1930 a Nationalist Vought Corsair got lost and landed in Communist territory in Hubei. The Red Army talked the pilot into joining and renamed the plane the Lenin, a faster rebrand than most program offices manage. In September 1931 it dropped leaflets over Wuhan. Two months later it dropped two mortar shells on the Nationalist garrison at Huangan, the defense line collapsed, and the town fell in three days. Any targeteer would envy that ratio of ordnance to effect. When Jiang Jieshi's next campaign came in 1932, the Red Army took the plane apart and hid it in the mountains. Local people dug it up in 1951, about the time the MiGs were over the Yalu. Two mortar shells and a defecting pilot, and that was the whole Red air force.

Opinion: This chapter is Crane's "image and reality of American airpower" run in reverse. Vandenberg counted airframes and saw a major air power. Zhang shows pilots who had been machine gunners, with eighty to one hundred hours in Japanese trainers, and a chairman who trusted courage over equipment. FEAF's Gen. Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations," and the Air Force kindly extended the same courtesy to China. For seminar:

  1. When Vandenberg said China had become a major air power, was he counting aircraft or air power?
  2. Which one should an Airman count? (S)
Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 2

Built for Taiwan, Shipped to the Yalu, Flight Hours Sold Separately

Zhang's claim in chapter 2 is that the PLAAF was born for one job, invading Taiwan, and built as an arm of the army. On July 10, 1949, Mao wrote that China could send three to four hundred people to the Soviet Union, buy about a hundred planes, and "seize Taiwan next summer." Liu Yalou, the future air force commander, ran the numbers and found the Nationalists still had 150 to two hundred fighters, so he asked for three hundred to 350 fighters instead. Mao approved and told him to be thrifty, a hard order to follow while tripling the shopping list. In Moscow on August 18, Marshal Vershinin offered 434 aircraft, six aviation schools, and 878 Soviet personnel to staff them. The Chinese worried that the Nationalist P-51 could outrun the La-9 in a dive, took the deal anyway, and later complained about paying new prices for old machinery, the eternal lament of the used-car buyer. Liu wrote in 1951 that the air force should support the ground forces and take their victories as its own. Nobody considered an independent service, and fighters were preferred over bombers because fighters were defensive.

The money came from wherever money could be found. Moscow lent $300 million at 1 percent, and Mao put $20 million of the 1950 installment toward the air force and navy. In 1950 the PLAAF asked for 369.1 million renminbi, 13 percent of the defense budget. A 1951 donation drive collected 556.5 million renminbi, enough for 3,710 fighters. The donations were voluntary in the sense that elementary school students gave their pocket money. Meanwhile Liu required approval for a dinner party or a night at the opera, and he publicly criticized a budget for a dairy farm at an aviation school. Comptrollers everywhere should study his methods, though perhaps not out loud.

Now for sixty-three. The standard for a student pilot was 150 to two hundred flying hours. After Beijing decided to intervene in Korea, the six schools graduated their first class early, in October 1950, with an average of about sixty-three hours, all in Yak-18 and Yak-11 trainers. That is well under half the minimum, and the bill came due fast. The PLAAF admitted 3.35 serious accidents per ten thousand sorties in 1950, and its former chief of staff counted more than a hundred fatal crashes in the first half of 1951. PLAAF authorities later conceded that more hours would have meant fewer crashes and probably more victories in Korea. Enthusiasm, it turns out, does not log flight time.

Opinion: The 628 paper prompt lists a Chinese invasion of Taiwan as a candidate crisis, and this chapter shows it was the PLAAF's original design brief. Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means. Mao's end was Taiwan within a year; his means were peasant soldiers, interpreters who did not know aviation terms, and sixty-three hours. Then Korea handed that half-built force a different war, no exchanges or refunds. Seminar question: if the PLAAF was designed as a defensive, army-support force for a Taiwan landing, how well did that design suit the air-superiority fight it actually got over the Yalu? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 3

Stalin Never Broke His Promise. He Just Never Made One.

The big question of chapter 3 is whether Stalin reneged on his promise of air cover for the Chinese in October 1950. Zhang's answer is a lawyer's answer: no, because he never defined it. The promise started on July 5, 1950, when Stalin asked Zhou Enlai to put nine divisions on the Korean border and offered air cover for them. On July 13 he got specific about the hardware, a MiG division of 124 planes, and vague about how it would work with Chinese ground troops, which was the only part the ground troops cared about. Belov's 151st Guards Fighter Aviation Division was in place at Shenyang, Liaoyang, and Anshan by early August, but Moscow ordered it to train Chinese pilots first and never fly beyond the Yalu. Zhou finally pinned Stalin down at his Black Sea villa on October 11. The answer: Soviet air units would need two to two and one-half months to get ready, roughly the delivery window on a nice sofa. On October 14, Moscow ordered 208 jet fighters to China, with pilots "strictly forbidden" to fly at the front. Mao sent his troops across anyway on October 19, umbrella or no umbrella.

Peng Dehuai had a bad week. On October 9 he gathered his army commanders in Shenyang and ordered them ready for battle in ten days. Then he learned his troops had exactly thirty-six antiaircraft guns, Japanese-made 76-mm pieces, and that nobody had issued any orders for coordinating ground and air forces. His commanders kept asking about air cover. Peng could not answer, so he cabled Mao in the middle of the meeting: how many bombers and fighters, when, and "who will be in charge?" Mao's answer, on October 11, was that "the air force cannot set off for the time being," which disposed of all three at once. At the Politburo meeting on October 13, Peng threatened to resign. Then on October 16 he told his division commanders the enemy air force was not "as fearsome as people think." Commanders have given worse pep talks, but not many with less behind them.

Opinion: Crane's chapter 1 has the American side of the same river. On June 29, 1950, Truman cleared strikes north of the 38th Parallel but ordered his pilots to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders, and in November the Yalu bridge attacks stopped at the Korean side of the spans. Stalin's pilots got the mirror-image order: stay on the Chinese side of the Yalu. Both great powers drew their limit at the same water line, for the same reason, and both left the poor guys on the ground to discover it late. It may be the most heavily lawyered river in Asia. FEAF's General Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Stalin didn't advertise his either. He just let Mao assume them away.

One for seminar: the 13th Army Group warned on August 31 that China should delay without guaranteed Soviet air support; since Mao went in anyway, was air support ever really the deciding factor, or just Mao's bargaining chip with Moscow? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 4

The MiGs Took the Shanghai Detour, and the Scorekeepers Are Still Arguing

Chapter 4 argues that Soviet air combat in 1950 was air defense of China wearing a Korean costume. The story starts in Shanghai, where the Nationalist air force hit the city twenty-six times between October 1949 and February 1950; the February 6 raid killed more than fourteen hundred people. Mao, then in Moscow, asked for help. Stalin sent Lt. Gen. Pavel Batitskiy with an air defense corps, including forty-four MiG-15 pilots who swapped into Chinese uniforms and had their identification and party documents removed, the standard paperwork for "we were never here." On April 28, Maj. Kelenichkov scored the first MiG-15 kill in combat, a P-38. By late 1950, eleven Soviet air divisions had arrived in China, mostly MiG-9s, a retired jet with short range and poor maneuverability that the Chinese suspected was being dumped on them, like a regifted fruitcake with engines. Over the Yalu, Zhang finds the Soviet pilots stayed within about thirty miles of Andong and never flew in coordination with Chinese ground operations. In November they flew 384 sorties against 2,525 detected enemy sorties, outflown better than six to one. His verdict: they "seem irrelevant" to the CPV's early success. Historians rarely get ruder than that.

Then there is the custody fight over the first jet-to-jet kill in history. The USAF official history says November 8, 1950, credited to Lt. Russell Brown. A Russian study says November 1, credited to Lt. Semyen Khomich, though the UN side reported no losses from that encounter, which makes it a kill without a victim. The Soviet records show no MiG losses at all on November 8; Russian scholars suspect Brown saw a MiG drop its external tanks and dive away. But Soviet records do confirm that Navy aviator William Amen's cannons sent Sr. Lt. Mikhail Grachev's MiG into a North Korean hillside on November 9. So after all that, Zhang says the honor should probably go to the Navy. The Air Force will want a recount.

Opinion: the scorekeeping problem never went away. On December 23, U.S. pilots claimed six MiGs "definitely destroyed" with no losses; the Soviets claimed three F-86s for the afternoon. For December, Pashkevich's division was credited with forty-one victories against six losses, which the Americans disputed. This is the Michigan Senate race in today's news, YouGov at 48 to 46 and Glengariff at 51 to 45: same race, different pollster, though at least pollsters don't paint stars on their fuselages. Crane said his subject was the image and reality of American airpower. Zhang shows the Soviets had the same gap between image and reality, and kept it in Russian. For the 628 paper, note something else from Shanghai: the Soviets barred their pilots from flying beyond the coastline so they would not get dragged into a Taiwan campaign. A patron in a Taiwan fight drawing a line at the water is not a new idea.

If the Soviets learned their jet tactics against a weak enemy over Shanghai and then struggled over the Yalu, how much should any air force trust lessons from a war against an opponent who can't fight back? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 5

Build It and They Will Bomb It

Chapter 5 covers the year, November 1950 to August 1951, when China tried and failed to get its own air force into the war. The matchup was not close. The Americans had some 1,100 combat aircraft flown largely by World War II veterans with more than a thousand hours. The PLAAF had about two hundred aircraft and pilots with under a hundred hours, and nobody, pilot or commander, had ever fought in the air. Mao approved a plan on December 4 giving priority to direct ground support, because Peng Dehuai demanded it. Reality then filed a plan of its own. The first combat practice at Andong, under Soviet cover, produced exactly one American plane shot down, and even its type is in dispute: Li Han's late-January kill was claimed as an F-84, and the FEAF records an F-80C lost that day. In February the 12th Aviation Regiment, with fifteen hours in MiGs, lost an entire squadron in a few days without firing a shot. Over little more than two months the 4th Aviation Division flew 145 sorties and saw the enemy on only twenty-four. Entry was postponed to September, then to November, and Peng canceled his planned offensive on August 21. By year's end close air support was dropped from the plan entirely, which did make the plan shorter.

Then there are the airfields. On March 1, Mao told Stalin that China was "unable to find a single operational airfield" in Korea, and that it could not build any without air cover it did not have. Stalin's solution to not being able to build one airfield was to build four concrete fields for Soviet MiGs, all at once. Zhou Enlai worked out that a jet airfield in China took twenty-four thousand man-days, so four in Korea would take ninety-six thousand. The final bill was some two million man-days, about twenty times Zhou's arithmetic, plus thirty thousand tons of cement and thirty-six thousand pieces of pierced-steel planking. American bombers attacked the sites seventy-two times, so at least someone was following the project closely. When the last field, at Sunchon, was finished in late July, the Chinese discovered the Soviets could not use any of them, because they were too far from bases in China to be supported. That is a lot of concrete for a parking lot.

Opinion: Gaddis says strategy is relating ends to means, and Zhou gave the bluntest version of it in an argument with a Soviet general. China could endure losing half a million to a million soldiers, he said, "but we will not sacrifice our newly built air force." Soldiers were cheap and replaceable; pilots and jets were neither. That explains every delay in the chapter better than bad weather does. Stalin, meanwhile, warned that Chinese fliers kept out of combat would be only "paper pilots," which is rich from a leader who fretted about the danger of basing his own air units in Korea. Both patrons and clients were guarding their scarcest asset and calling it prudence.

Seminar question: was the PLAAF's caution a failure of nerve, or the correct reading of what a brand-new air force could afford to lose? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 6

The Chinese Pilots Who Spoke Russian

Chapter 6 makes a simple, uncomfortable claim: the MiG war over the Yalu was mostly a Soviet war. American records claim about 950 enemy aircraft destroyed in the air, more than 850 of them MiGs, against 147 air-to-air losses. The Soviets claimed more than a thousand UN aircraft against some three hundred of their own. Zhang says the gap cannot be reconciled, and spends the chapter showing why, which is more than either side's accountants ever tried. Moscow ran the war in three phases. Elite divisions under Col. Ivan Kozhedub, a top World War II ace, and Maj. Gen. Georgiy Lobov did the damage in 1951; on April 12, thirty-six MiGs hit forty B-29s, and Gen. Stratemeyer suspended B-29 operations around Sinuiju. Then Moscow swapped whole divisions for green ones, and losses climbed, a personnel policy that works fine until it meets Sabres. The constraints were political. Soviet MiGs wore North Korean markings, and pilots arrived in Chinese uniforms with Chinese pseudonyms and orders to speak Chinese on the radio. Few did, since nobody learns Mandarin mid-dogfight. They carried no bombs or rockets, could not fly over enemy territory or the sea, and fought purely on defense while the Americans picked every fight. Kozhedub summed it up as "two regiments against all of imperialism." The 64th IAK finally claimed 1,106 planes by fighters and 212 by flak, for 335 MiGs and 120 pilots lost.

Remember Black Tuesday, October 23, 1951, over Namsi. Soviet pilots reported meeting twenty-one B-29s and more than two hundred fighters, and claimed twelve bombers and four F-84s for no losses. The FEAF had sent nine B-29s, and one of them turned back with a mechanical problem. So the Soviets claimed four more bombers than actually showed up, a feat of marksmanship unmatched in the history of aerial warfare. The Americans admitted three bombers and one Thunderjet, though four of five badly damaged B-29s crash-landed and two were written off, and they claimed nine MiGs. One American author tidied this up by assuming the dead MiGs were Chinese or North Korean. Zhang points out that no North Korean MiG unit flew before 1952, and the only operational Chinese division had gone to Shenyang for rest three days earlier. Whatever the count, the Americans suspended daylight B-29 operations within days, which is a scoreboard of its own.

Opinion: Crane quotes FEAF's Gen. Smart saying "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Zhang shows that both superpowers made a joint policy of it. USAF leaders knew the MiGs that mauled their bombers were Soviet, Zhang writes, yet credited the Chinese to avoid stirring up public demand for war with Russia. Gen. Vandenberg even announced that China had become a major air power almost overnight. Its pilots just happened to have a Russian accent. It was the polite fiction that kept a limited war limited. And the dueling kill tallies are this week's Michigan Senate polls with jet engines: same race, different pollster, wildly different numbers.

Seminar questions, given that Washington and Moscow both knew Soviets were flying the MiGs and both kept quiet:

  1. Was that shared secrecy the most effective escalation control of the war?
  2. Would it survive in a Taiwan crisis today? (S)
Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 7

Same Bombers, Same Route, Same Time: What Could Go Wrong?

From September 1951 to May 1952, China's air force went to Korea as a student, and the tuition was steep. Operation Strangle opened on August 18, and the Chinese General Staff answered with four decisions: more railway repair troops, more bridge materials, every available ferry, and more antiaircraft guns, plus, at last, the air force. From September 12 to October 19, the 4th Aviation Division flew 508 sorties, claimed 20 kills, and lost 14 planes, while FEAF records show no losses in those fights. Air forces, Zhang notes, tend to be honest about their own losses, if no one else's. By the end of May 1952, 447 Chinese pilots had flown 11,100 sorties, and only 15 percent involved actual combat. Historian Xu Yan puts jet proficiency at 300 hours; Chinese pilots arrived with 50 to 60, which got them about a sixth to a fifth of the way there. Peng Dehuai never got his air support. U.S. aircraft bombed Chinese airfield construction in North Korea 119 times, and in late December 1951 the PLAAF settled for its Chinese bases, leashing its MiGs to about 100 miles from home.

The scene to remember is Taehwa-do Island. On November 6, 1951, nine Tu-2 bombers dropped 81 bombs on the South Korean outpost there and met no UN fighters. So the 8th Division planned the next raid with the same number of planes, the same route, and the same takeoff time, and publicized it for days, in case anyone had missed the pattern. On November 30, Army intelligence tipped off the 4th Fighter Wing, and Col. Ben Preston brought 31 Sabres. Gao Yueming took off a minute early, his squadron leader turned early, and the bombers reached the rendezvous five minutes ahead of schedule, with only propeller-driven La-11s for escort. The MiGs were still taking off. As Zhang puts it, "In modern air combat, five minutes is a lifetime." The crews mistook the incoming Sabres for friendly MiGs heading home. China lost four Tu-2s, three La-11s, and a MiG, and the surviving bombs churned up sand on the beach. The USAF claimed eight bombers. Inflation was the one thing both sides had in surplus.

Opinion: Read this next to Crane and you get both ends of the same pipe. Crane's April 1952 FEAF study found rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts and 243 aircraft lost, and Crane counts up to 500,000 soldiers and civilians keeping the routes open. Zhang shows the order behind that. Beijing's answer to Strangle was repair crews, bridge timber, ferries, and flak, with fighters fifth on the list, and the shovels arguably had the better war. The sanctuary cut both ways. Truman kept FEAF clear of the Manchurian border, so the MiGs lived safely at Andong, and FEAF kept bombing the Chinese airfields going up in North Korea, so the MiGs never moved closer. Each side owned its own bank of the Yalu, and the river did most of the limiting.

Seminar question: The PLAAF treated Korea as a training ground, rotating divisions through Andong after five to seven big battles; was that smart force development for a limited war, or a steady way to feed rookies to the Sabres? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 8

Stalin Dies in March, Bombers Take the Bow in May

Chapter 8 runs from June 1952 to the armistice and asks what the PLAAF learned. Mostly air defense, Zhang says, and he doubts even that paid off. Command was so centralized that the CPV Air Force commander gave orders straight to flying group leaders, a management style best described as hands-on. His deputy, Duan Suquan, blamed the December 13, 1951 disaster on PLAAF chief Liu Yalou, who was visiting and ordered the MiGs down below an approaching Sabre formation. Visiting brass are always so helpful. Liu later pushed four-plane formations. More than 1,070 air force personnel rotated through for experience. Political officers ran morale: red stars painted on the fuselage for each kill, a wingman court-martialed for breaking off after his leader was killed, and five of eight regimental commanders lost to their own brashness. Beijing kept its bombers leashed. Zhou Enlai canceled a night raid on Kimpo minutes before takeoff on February 8, 1952; Peng scrubbed the June 1952 raids on Kimpo and Suwon for fear of reprisal; and Stalin warned that "the air force belongs to the state." Message received. By May 1953 the scoreboard was lopsided: 22,639 USAF sorties against 1,164 for the CPV Air Force, or better than nineteen to one.

The image to keep is the last day of the war. Communist air forces did not rise to meet FEAF's final push. Ground crews from three Chinese air divisions at Andong were busy taking MiGs and Il-10s apart and crating them, racing to move North Korean air units south of the Yalu before the truce took effect. When the cease-fire came, about one hundred crated planes sat on trains and river barges. Zhang calls it the first evidence of the Communists violating the truce, which at least showed promptness. Three years of fighting for the sky, and the final Communist air operation was a freight shipment. The cease-fire line, he adds, ran almost where the war had started on June 25, 1950, making the whole thing a very expensive round trip.

Opinion: Zhang's timeline is a hard rebuttal to the airpower story Crane describes. Stalin died March 5, 1953. A Soviet resolution on March 19 made ending the war a priority, and on March 28 Beijing accepted the UN's prisoner proposal. The big dam strikes came in May, two months after Moscow had decided to quit, yet Western studies credit them with helping force the deal, which is like the rooster taking credit for the sunrise. Sabre pilots also claimed 163 MiGs for three losses in May and June. Zhang concludes that Stalin's death, not air pressure, broke the logjam. That fits Crane's point that the ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded, and both authors end up in Vietnam, which is rarely a happy ending for an airpower argument. Even the Supung strike of June 23, 1952 gets two versions: Crane counts about 90 percent of North Korea's power gone, while the Soviet commander says monsoon storms kept the MiGs grounded. Either way, the lights went out.

Seminar question: If Zhang is right that Stalin's death, not the May 1953 bombing, ended the war, what could an air planner in 1953 have measured to tell the difference? (S)

Today's Seminar · Zhang, chapter 9

Everybody Won the Air War, Which Is How You Know Nobody Counted Right

Zhang opens his conclusion with the scoreboard, and then spends the chapter telling you not to trust it. By the armistice on July 27, 1953, the PLAAF had grown to 27 air divisions and more than 3,000 aircraft. It had flown 26,491 sorties, admitted losing 231 planes, and claimed about 1.42 kills for every loss. The Soviets claimed 1,309 UN aircraft for 335 MiGs, with a ratio of 7.9 to 1 through December 1951 that slid to 1.9 to 1 by 1953. American pilots claimed 8 or 10 to 1. The North Koreans, wisely, mostly stayed home: one Russian source has them flying 12 sorties a month while the Soviets flew 2,500. Zhang's verdict is that the Americans overcounted, because a gun-camera hit earned credit even if nobody saw the MiG go down, while the Soviets figured only 75 percent of filmed hits were real kills. Some American accounts have since cut the ratio to 2 to 1, a markdown any clearance rack would envy.

Kill ratios, Zhang then argues, do not explain who won. Even by Chinese reckoning, the USAF held air superiority the whole war. The UN flew 1.04 million sorties, and the Chinese counted roughly 190,000 bombs dropped on railroads, one for every seven meters of track, which is less a bombing campaign than a landscaping project. The PLAAF averaged 50 MiG sorties a day against 900 for the USAF. Chinese ground forces could not mount large daytime operations for three years. Yet Zhang insists the Communist air effort was essential to keeping supply lines open. Both sides kept it limited. The USAF never hit bases in northeast China, Washington kept Soviet participation from the American public, and Beijing never sent its air force south of the 38th Parallel.

The lessons Beijing drew were mostly the convenient ones. Mao, whose own son died in an air raid, was gratified that bombing killed fewer of his soldiers than he had feared, and he never let airpower change his strategy. The PLAAF filed 3,653 combat reports by 1955 and concluded that the human factor was still decisive, a finding that took a lot of paperwork to reach. It took on Soviet defensive doctrine, put army officers in command, and kept its aircraft busy defending things. Moscow got a tested MiG and a pinned-down America. Beijing got a debt of 3 billion yuan, finally paid in 1964, and the lesson that Stalin was an unreliable ally, which it paid for in installments.

The one thing to remember is the myth. Zhang Jihui and Han Dechai became household names in China for shooting down American aces George Davis and Harold Fischer, and PLAAF writers love to quote Vandenberg's line that China had become "one of the major air powers of the world." It is always nice when the other side writes your press release. Veterans remember an unbroken string of victories. The bill came in 1954, when the PLAAF failed to spot 70 percent of intruding Nationalist aircraft. Korea veteran Lin Hu recalled that pilots who believed they had beaten the Americans saw the Nationalists as little threat. Zhang calls it self-delusion that lasted more than forty years. In fairness, at least one man tried: the CPV Air Force deputy commander, Duan Suquan, warned Peng Dehuai that even a 1 to 1 ratio against Sabres was hard and told leaders to stop exaggerating. The myth won anyway, as myths with good press usually do.

Opinion: this is the chapter where Zhang closes the loop on his introduction, which took aim at Futrell's 7 to 1 kill ratio. Put it next to Crane, whose subject was "the image and reality of American airpower" and whose FEAF commander said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Both air forces left Korea with a flattering image, and both paid for it later. Crane's Air Force went back to planning for general war and paid in Vietnam, while Zhang's PLAAF could not police its own coast. A 10 to 1 claim and a 2 to 1 revision are the Michigan Senate poll problem: same race, different pollster, very different headline. And Crane's 1952 FEAF study saying rail interdiction failed reads differently once you know the other side counted one bomb per seven meters and still kept the trains running.

Zhang ends looking forward. After the 1991 Gulf War, the PLAAF decided it must play a major rather than supporting role, and by the late 1990s it was finally turning its back on Korea's defensive legacy, only about four decades behind schedule. Chinese planners now name Taiwan, the borders, and the South China Sea as likely flashpoints. Zhang's warning is that Washington paid scant attention to Chinese strategic thinking in 1950, and that the will of the Chinese people, more than China's military capability, will likely drive Beijing's decisions. If you are writing the 628 paper on a Taiwan invasion, that is your epigraph, free of charge. If Zhang is right that Beijing intervened in 1950 with almost no air force at all, how much should American planners discount China's capability gaps when they estimate whether it would fight over Taiwan? (S)

Preview: McDougall, …The Heavens and the Earth, chapter by chapter (seminar Friday)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, introduction

Governments Take Up Inventing, and Never Put It Down

McDougall opens a political history of the Space Age with a fish that crawled out of a river 360 million years ago, which is one way to tell the reader you plan to take the long view. His claim is simpler than the fish. For most of history, states reacted to new tools; inventing them was somebody else's job. The world wars made state-run research a duty, and World War II produced four "war babies" that shaped the next half century: British radar, the American atomic bomb, the German ballistic missile, and the American electronic computer. After the war the money dried up again, and most of the West decided that state direction of science defeated itself. Then, in the ten years up to 1965, that belief collapsed. McDougall calls it a saltation, an evolutionary leap, which is what you get for starting with a fish. Western governments quintupled their R&D funding for national goals. His word for the result is technocracy: technological change made into an institution that serves the state. The trigger was Sputnik, a predictable step in engineering that arrived at an awkward moment. American leadership rested on two premises, better institutions and better weapons, and the Soviet satellite seemed to knock out both with one launch. Eisenhower fought a holding action. Kennedy, four months into office, asked Congress for the moon, and he meant it literally. Lyndon Johnson put the new creed in one line: "first in space means first, period; second in space is second in everything."

The thing to remember is the trap McDougall springs on the last page. A state can turn off its research machine. It cannot stop the lone problem solver from thinking, and it cannot stop foreign rivals from making new challenges. The only thing that could end technological change would be a Big Brother powerful enough to control every nation and every subject's thoughts, and nobody is taking applications. Until that arrives, everyone keeps racing, and the race threatens to "erode the very values that make one's society worth defending." McDougall calls this "the dilemma of the Space Age and the moral of our story." So the fish, it turns out, was the setup for a warning.

Opinion: this is Gaddis with the sign flipped. Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means, and he shows Truman's people working from the belief that resources were limited. McDougall describes a Washington that, after 1957, mostly stopped believing that, and a state whose means grew until they looked like ends. Budget officers everywhere will recognize the condition. The 628 paper prompt asks how Airmen should prepare for the next big crisis, whether that is Taiwan, an Iranian bomb, or a Russian move on NATO. McDougall's answer to anyone preparing is to keep asking what the preparation is costing the thing being defended. Not exactly a slogan for a recruiting poster.

Seminar question: if McDougall is right that no state can opt out of the technology race while rivals stay in it, what limits can a democracy put on its own R&D state without giving up the race? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 1

Stalin Built the Launch Pad, Then Locked Up the Designers

Part I opens in 1881, with Tsar Alexander II killed by a bomb from the People's Will. The bomb maker, Nikolai Kibalchich, spent his last days in prison designing a rocket-propelled aircraft, which is one way to use a deadline. McDougall's point is that rocketry and revolution grew up together in Russia. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky hated gravity the way other people hate the tax man, and in 1903 he published the principles of rocket motion. The old regime gave him exactly one grant, 470 rubles, so the tsars can't be accused of overspending. In 1919 Lenin drew his lesson from the Great War: master the highest technology, or be crushed. A 1924 rocket bureau made the Soviet Union the first government to back spaceflight. Stalin delivered money and terror together. In the Industrial Party affair, eight leading technologists were accused of heading a plot of over 2,000 engineers, a conspiracy big enough to need its own org chart. The Academy of Sciences went from 1,000 people in 1927 to over 16,000 by 1940, and R&D spending grew sixfold between 1927 and 1932. Civilian research still ran badly, since a failed experiment could get you branded a "wrecker," which does wonders for risk-taking. McDougall finds one exception: "That sector was defense." By 1939 Soviet factories turned out 700 to 800 planes a month.

The scene to remember is the sharaga, the prison design bureau. Sergei Korolev, born in 1907, got the deputy directorship of the main rocket institute from Marshal Tukhachevsky, whose name became a synonym for death after 1937. (Not the reference you want on a résumé.) Korolev had just been moved to the aircraft designer Tupolev's bureau, in time to follow Tupolev into prison in 1938, and he is said to have spent months in the Kolyma mines. The samizdat author Ozerov later described walking into the dining room of Tupolev's prison bureau and finding the elite of Soviet aircraft design, every one of them a prisoner. He concluded that "this meant a catastrophe for Soviet aviation!" McDougall replies: "In fact, it did not." The prisoners designed fighters and dive bombers, and Georgy Langemak, reprieved from a death sentence, designed the Katyusha rocket launcher. It was the most exclusive engineering faculty in the world, and admission came with an arrest warrant.

Opinion: Conrad Crane's book is about "the image and reality of American airpower," and this chapter is the Soviet edition. In 1933 Stalin declared August 18 Aviation Day. His "Falcons" chased altitude and distance records, and one pilot wrote that Stalin was their father, a family it was unwise to disappoint. McDougall notes that the campaign also pulled attention away from the show trials, so the air show doubled as a smokescreen. Then in 1938 the Messerschmitt 109E arrived in Spain and swept the more numerous Soviet planes from the sky, and Stalin arrested his chief designers. FEAF's General Smart said Americans "certainly don't advertise our limitations." Stalin went further: he advertised the records and jailed the limitations.

Seminar question: if terror did not stop Soviet military innovation, should we still assume that an authoritarian rival will out-think itself in a technology race over Taiwan or anywhere else? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 2

The Americans Got the Germans. The Russians Got to Orbit.

McDougall argues that the German V-2 held few theoretical surprises for Soviet rocketeers. Its main effect was to push both victors into early rivalry and make German engineers the choicest loot of the war. The Americans won the loot. Von Braun led 525 people south, his brother Magnus surrendered to a puzzled American private on May 2, 1945, and Stalin fumed that the Americans got the engineers. The Soviets shipped their second-string Germans east on twelve hours' notice in October 1946, which is how Moscow said "welcome aboard." The real accelerator was Hiroshima. Stalin told his munitions commissar and the physicist Kurchatov, "The equilibrium has been destroyed," and demanded the bomb fast. Kurchatov guessed five years. The Soviet bomb came in August 1949, a year early, so at least his estimating erred on the safe side. The 1946 Soviet R&D budget was 6,300 million rubles, rising to 9,000 million by 1950, when the U.S. government spent $1,083 million. McDougall judges Soviet military research several times the American effort, perhaps six times as a share of GNP. By 1947 Stalin wanted Eugen Sänger's rocket planes, able to cross oceans, which he said would give him leverage over Truman. Stalin always did have an eye for leverage. Korolev's ICBM design won approval in 1954. His R-7, twenty engines clustered for 1.1 million pounds of thrust, flew on August 3, 1957, and Moscow announced a proven ICBM on August 27. In the Part I conclusion, McDougall weighs Kendall Bailes's eight traits of Soviet R&D and finds only terror peculiar to Soviet defense work, and terror was not decisive. "What slowed Soviet rocketry in the 1930s and 1940s was not terror, but war."

The scene to remember is a cocktail party. Three evenings after Moscow announced the satellite's radio frequency, scientists from the International Geophysical Year were drinking vodka at the Soviet Embassy in Washington. A Russian émigré needled his former countrymen: "Poor Tsiolkovsky is turning in his grave," since the old prophet's hundredth birthday had passed without a Russian satellite. A Soviet guest said the émigré would eat his words before he got back to Moscow in two days. Shortly after, the New York Times reached Walter Sullivan by phone, and Lloyd Berkner clapped for silence to announce that Radio Moscow had a satellite in orbit. The émigré's words were served well ahead of schedule. Khrushchev, told the news, congratulated the engineers and calmly went to bed. Beating America into space, apparently, was not worth staying up for.

Opinion: two earlier books meet here. Gaddis has Truman leaning on the atomic monopoly and Forrestal speaking of "years of opportunity." McDougall shows that in Moscow those were years of a crash program, and Kurchatov beat his own deadline. Opportunity, it turns out, was available to both sides. Zhang argues that Stalin's death in March 1953, more than air pressure, broke the Korean armistice deadlock. McDougall agrees in passing: with Stalin gone, Malenkov, Mikoyan and others approved a negotiated end to the war. Interdiction, take note.

Seminar question: if the Soviet rocket program ran on fear of an American monopoly, did American nuclear superiority after 1945 buy security, or did it set the pace of the race that produced Sputnik? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 3

The Superpower That Forgot to Write a War Plan

Part II turns to the United States, which McDougall says never embraced state-driven technology the way Moscow did. From 1945 to 1960 Truman and Eisenhower looked for a way to deter the Soviets without turning America into a garrison state. Their answer, nuclear deterrence, chained American defense to fast technological change, and their thrift and caution helped make the country second into space. American science, chapter 3 says, grew up an orphan, and a poorly fed one. The National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics began in 1915 as a rider on a naval bill, with a $5,000 budget and twelve unpaid appointees, and its peacetime peak was $3.1 million in 1940. At the end of the 1930s all American R&D came to $264 million a year, against 1,651 million rubles in the 1941 Soviet plan. Robert Goddard collected eighty-three patents and was still dismissed as a crackpot, and von Kármán picked the word "jet" for his lab because people still doubted rockets. Branding, even then, beat physics. Then came the war. Vannevar Bush's OSRD contracted research out to universities and firms, and federal R&D rose from $74 million in 1940 to $1.59 billion in 1945. When Congress finally created the National Science Foundation in 1950, the Senate gave it $225,000, while the military already held 62 percent of federal R&D by 1948. Science got the pocket change; the generals had the wallet. After the 1947 unification act, a new directive gave "strategic" missiles to the new Air Force and "tactical" ones to the Army, just as the budget ax fell on everybody. It was a fine time to receive a mission.

The scene to remember is a confession. In 1947 the President's Air Policy Commission under Thomas Finletter held more than 350 meetings and asked to see the war plan. After a Pentagon runaround, Truman ordered Eisenhower, Vandenberg, Leahy, and Nimitz to testify. They dodged until Eisenhower told his colleagues they owed the civilians the truth: "there is no war plan." Over 350 meetings, and the answer fit on an index card. The force behind the missing plan was just as thin. Spaatz figured there were a dozen or so warheads in the arsenal. In August 1946 only six of the twenty-six B-29 groups had aircraft, and they were based in the Southwest, where they "threatened no one but Canada and Mexico." Truman apparently asked about the size of the arsenal only in April 1947, and was shocked. We would have been too.

Opinion: Gaddis says Truman's people believed resources were limited, and has Truman weighing $20–30 billion to keep the peace against $100 billion to fight a war. McDougall shows what that belief did to strategy. A $14.4 billion ceiling on the FY 1950 defense budget left the Joint Chiefs unable to keep even a foothold in Europe, so the bomb won on price. In July 1949 Truman concluded that without international control, "we must be strongest in atomic weapons." Deterrence was a budget decision before it was a doctrine. Crane's Air Force, which went back to planning for general war after Korea, was born here, in the accounting office.

Seminar question: if the budget ceiling, more than any theory, pushed Truman into air-atomic deterrence, what spending choices today are already writing American strategy for a Taiwan crisis? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 4

RAND Called Sputnik Seven Years Early, and Washington Filed It

Chapter 4 explains why the richest country on earth left its rockets on the shelf in the late 1940s. Truman's war on inflation won, and the rockets were among the casualties. Missile R&D for FY 1947 was cut to $22 million, and eleven programs died at once, including Convair's MX-774, a 5,000-mile ballistic missile. No fuel could yet throw a five-ton warhead around the world, nobody knew how a warhead would survive reentry, and SAC had bombers and bases near Soviet borders. Vannevar Bush doubted anyone could build an accurate ICBM, while admitting he was "not much of a prophet." On that point, at least, he was right. The navy's satellite program was cancelled on June 22, 1948. Fear revived both. The Soviet bomb brought Truman's H-bomb go-ahead on January 31, 1950, then NSC-68 and Korea, and defense spending tripled. The H-bomb solved the payload problem. BRAVO's 15 megatons in March 1954 came in a package a rocket could carry, so a smaller, less accurate missile would do. At 15 megatons, close counts. Trevor Gardner went around the Air Force bureaucracy, Bernard Schriever got the Western Development Division, and on June 21, 1954, Atlas became the Air Force's highest priority. Nothing focuses the mind like a Soviet bomb.

The one thing to remember is a pair of RAND reports. On May 2, 1946, RAND told the Army Air Forces that a satellite needed 17,000 miles per hour, would circle the earth every hour and a half, and would cost about $150 million and five years. It also predicted a satellite "would inflame the imagination of mankind." Nobody has asked for a refund on that one. Then came the October 1950 report, which McDougall calls the "birth certificate of American space policy." It saw that satellites were reconnaissance tools, that a closed Soviet Union would treat spying from orbit as an attack on its secrecy, and that nobody knew whether national airspace had a ceiling. The fix was a polite bait-and-switch: launch an "experimental" satellite on an equatorial orbit that never crosses Soviet territory, test "freedom of space," then send up the real work satellite. RAND even guessed the Soviets would have little reason to build a satellite of their own, the one call it badly missed, and a big one to miss. The report went to the Air Force on October 4, 1950, seven years to the day before Sputnik 1.

Opinion: this chapter is Gaddis's point that Truman's people believed resources were limited, applied to rockets. The same budget logic that kept containment cheap left the ICBM waiting for an H-bomb to justify it. The more current hook is RAND's question about whether airspace has an upward limit, and the Army is now floating an answer. It is exploring balloons above 60,000 feet, in international airspace, to watch, relay, and strike along China's anti-access network. The vertical boundary RAND worried about in 1950 is still the legal ground an Airman would be fighting on.

McDougall says the lag in hardware and the lead in ideas left America second into orbit but with a program better suited to its needs, so did Truman's austerity cost the country the satellite race or buy it the right strategy? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 5

Second Place, On Purpose

Chapter 5 argues that Eisenhower lost the race to orbit because he was running a different race. The federal budget had grown from under $4 billion in 1932 to $85.5 billion in 1952, 57.2 percent of it for the Pentagon. His New Look, NSC-162/2 of October 1953, planned to cut military spending 30 percent over four years, a plan the Pentagon presumably did not applaud. Cutting safely required hard intelligence on the Soviets, so the Skunk Works built the U-2 in eighty days. (Vanguard's planners might have taken notes.) By 1955 Washington had two goals, getting into space first and winning the legal right of overflight, and three candidates: the army's Orbiter, the IGY science satellite, and the Air Force's WS-117L spy satellite. The NSC put protecting the spy satellite first. On August 3, 1955, the Stewart committee voted three to two for the navy's Viking over von Braun's Redstone, with one member home sick who later said he favored the army. History occasionally turns on a sick day. Quarles let the vote stand. McDougall calls the choice ideal politically, if not technically, and then the technical part arrived: Vanguard's estimate climbed from $20 million to $63 million by June 1956. When John Hagen called it the Naval Research Lab satellite in front of Soviet visitors, a junior staffer corrected him: "The National Academy's satellite, Dr. Hagen." Message discipline, at least, was never Vanguard's problem.

The one thing to remember is the dummy upper stages. On September 20, 1956, the army's Jupiter-C flew 682 miles high and came down 3,355 miles from Cape Canaveral. With a live upper stage it could have reached orbit, so the army sent inspectors to the Cape to make sure every Jupiter upper stage was a dummy and von Braun could not launch a satellite "accidentally." The army, in other words, posted guards against success. A year later, on October 4, 1957, Defense Secretary Neil McElroy was having cocktails in Huntsville when word came that the Soviets had a satellite up. Von Braun begged for sixty days, and Medaris corrected him to ninety. Four days later Quarles admitted to Eisenhower that the Redstone could have orbited a satellite a year or more earlier. Then he looked on the bright side. The Russians, he said, had "done us a good turn" by establishing freedom of international space, and Ike, ever on message, asked about a reconnaissance vehicle.

Opinion: Eisenhower ranked his ends and paid for the ranking, which is Gaddis's definition of strategy in action. It also echoes Zhang's account of Korea, where Soviet pilots flew MiGs in Chinese uniforms and USAF leaders credited the Chinese to avoid stirring public demand for war with Russia. In both cases a secret protected the policy. Eisenhower's reasons stayed classified and only the result was public, which is the worst split a politician can draw. McDougall's Part II conclusion calls Sputnik "the greatest defeat Eisenhower could have suffered," because it handed his critics a weapon against his whole philosophy of government. The spy satellite got its legal precedent. The President got a political beating.

Was quietly letting the Soviets go first a sound trade for the freedom of space, or did Eisenhower misjudge what a public defeat would cost the policies he was trying to protect? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 6

Four Feet Off the Pad, Two Parts Vodka

Part III opens with Eisenhower at Thanksgiving 1959, holding a $92.2 billion budget with a billion-dollar surplus and asking his Cabinet what the true problem facing Western civilization was. McDougall casts him as Janus, in the vanguard of the Space Age and its rearguard at once. Chapter 6 argues that the early uproar over Sputnik was a "media riot" with no grassroots movement behind it. A Gallup poll of October 11 to 14 found half the sample calling Sputnik a serious blow to prestige, 46 percent saying it was not, and 61 percent expecting satellites to be used for good. Not exactly torches and pitchforks. The press, as ignorant of space as everyone else, taught the public like a rookie professor one chapter ahead in the textbook. Life told readers, "Let us not pretend that Sputnik is anything but a defeat for the United States." The administration then pitched in. Wilson called it a neat scientific trick, and Sherman Adams said the goal was not a high score in a celestial basketball game. Meanwhile a memo to Lyndon Johnson said the issue could unify his party and make him President, so somebody, at least, saw an opportunity. Sputnik II went up on November 3 with a 1,121-pound payload and a dog named Laika, and the Senate approved an inquiry the next day. Eisenhower refused the Gaither Report's call for a fallout shelter program costing upward of $25 billion. Johnson opened his hearings on November 25 by calling Sputnik a "technological Pearl Harbor," and closed them on January 23 with seventeen recommendations.

The one thing to remember is Vanguard TV-3. The White House insisted that a test flight be billed as a real attempt to orbit a satellite, so reporters from around the world gathered at Cape Canaveral. Shortly before noon on December 6, 1957, the rocket rose four feet, burst into flame, and settled back to earth. The nose cone fell clear, and the little satellite lay nearby, chirping innocently. The press renamed it Kaputnik, Stayputnik, and Flopnik, and Americans drank the Sputnik Cocktail, "two parts vodka, one part sour grapes." At the UN, Soviet delegates asked whether the United States would like aid for underdeveloped countries. Moscow, it turned out, did comedy too. Of all the things Eisenhower could have publicized that autumn, he publicized a test.

Opinion: this is Crane's subject, "the image and reality of American airpower," moved to orbit. By McDougall's account, the reality favored the United States. The Killian panel's late-1950s window of superiority was real, and LeMay called Sputnik a hunk of iron, which was accurate and helped nobody. The image went the other way, because the reality was mostly classified. FEAF's Gen. Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Eisenhower's problem was that he could not advertise his strengths either. For an Airman, a classified advantage does nothing for public confidence, and an adversary's demonstration on the front page does a great deal.

The polls show the public was not panicking at first. That leaves two questions:

  1. Who owns the "media riot"?
  2. Can a government whose best facts are secret ever win one? (S)
Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 7

Goldstone Has the Bird, and Ike Wants No Hullabaloo

Chapter 7 argues that Eisenhower answered Sputnik with four initiatives, each built to be temporary, limited, or self-limiting, and that each one opened a door he could not fully close. In his 1958 State of the Union he said the Soviets were waging total cold war, with trade, science, education, and ideas all pressed into service. His first post-Sputnik budget still rose only 1.5 percent, so total cold war came with a spending cap. NSF grants rose to $55 million from $38 million. The National Defense Education Act was written to run seven years only, but a Democratic rider added $60 million for students not going to college, and McDougall sees no stopping point after that. The sunset clause lost the race to the rider. Pentagon reorganization, law on August 6, 1958, was about control: letting every service list its needs produced something like $100 billion, against Ike's FY 1960 ceiling of $44 billion. Wish lists are like that. The NACA had been shrinking and in 1954 got half its request, but the PSAC chose it for its long cooperation with the military. Neglect, it turns out, was no disqualification. The bill that became NASA split space into an open civilian program and a closed military one, and aimed to keep the United States "a leader, not the leader." Schriever told Congress the military side was 75 to 90 percent of the game, and McCormack privately put it at 60 percent, so the only argument was over the size of the generals' majority. On July 7 Johnson offered to make the President chairman of the new space council, and Ike said that might do it. NASA opened on October 1, 1958.

The one thing to remember is the night of January 31, 1958. The army and navy were racing for the first American satellite. Vanguard slipped from the 18th to the 23rd to the 26th and then lost its turn to a faulty engine. Jet stream winds held the army's Jupiter-C, and January 31 was its last chance before Vanguard got another crack. At 10:48 p.m. it lit up the Banana River swamps. Then came an hour of waiting. Medaris fought the press and his nerves, and Army Secretary Brucker complained from Washington about running short of coffee and cigarettes, the true hardships of high command. Finally a slip of paper: "Goldstone has the bird." Explorer 1 was in orbit. Eisenhower, at Augusta National, said that was wonderful and then, true to form: "Let's not make too great a hullabaloo over this." The only man in America who wanted less hullabaloo was the one who had just won some.

Opinion: this is Gaddis's symmetrical versus asymmetrical containment, fought over budgets instead of borders. Total cold war means every field is a yardstick, which makes it a symmetrical contest by definition. Eisenhower answered with sufficiency, picking where to compete and capping how much, which is the asymmetrical instinct. McDougall's Part III opening says Ike's emergency measures became permanent anyway. Anyone writing the 628 paper on preparing for a crisis should take note: institutions created in a panic usually stay.

By splitting space between an open NASA and a closed Pentagon program, did the 1958 Space Act settle the civil-military question or, as McDougall says, only start the fight? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 8

Space for Peace, Some Restrictions Apply

McDougall's claim is that Eisenhower's space strategy had two faces, and the second one ran the show. In public, the United States would champion "space for peace," cooperation, and the rule of law. In private, it would keep its freedom to fly whatever military missions the nuclear deterrent needed, above all spy satellites. Ike wrote to Bulganin on January 12, 1958, proposing that outer space be used for peaceful purposes only. Moscow answered by asking, among other things, that American foreign bases be liquidated. Shockingly, that went nowhere. The real policy was NSC 5814/1, "Preliminary U.S. Policy on Outer Space," which Ike approved in mid-August 1958. It split missiles off from space, so no UN space deal could freeze missiles, and it called reconnaissance satellites critical to national security. It also declined to say where outer space began. The United States preferred a functional definition (if it is in orbit, it is in space), the kind only a lawyer could love. On November 24, 1958, the General Assembly voted 54 to 9, with 18 abstentions, for the Western resolution creating the committee that became COPUOS. The Soviets boycotted it, which is one way to lose a vote twice. Washington also won on wording: space would serve "peaceful" purposes, not "nonmilitary" ones, and the Pentagon's programs fit comfortably in the gap between the two words.

The one thing to remember is a question asked by Eisenhower's Operations Coordinating Board in 1958. With the UN useless without Soviet consent, the board wondered about developing "a cover for such reconnaissance satellites." By October it had its answer, and the answer was rhetoric. The United States should wrap its military program in "space for peace," call it vital to deterrence and therefore peaceful, and build a world climate that tolerated certain classified programs. Few ad agencies have aimed higher. Meanwhile the hardware struggled. Discoverer 1 tumbled in orbit, and Discoverer 2's capsule came down somewhere in northern Norway and was never found, which is not the reconnaissance anyone ordered. A consultant warned that "Satellites are our last chance." Staffer Sol Horwitz told Lyndon Johnson not to worry about Moscow's protests: "The Russians will scream on any occasion they think it desirable to scream." As Kremlinology goes, that one held up. McDougall's bottom line is that space was about spying because the USSR was secretive, and that competition, the thing the idealists deplored, paid for spaceflight.

Opinion: The hook is overhead. The newsletter reported on Oct. 27 that the Army is exploring high-altitude balloons above 60,000 feet, in international airspace, to watch, relay, and strike along China's anti-access network. That is the 1958 problem all over again, with more helium:

  1. Who may fly over whom?
  2. Where does sovereign air end?

Ike's answer was never to draw the line and to let practice set the law. The balloon program depends on the same open sky his lawyers fought to keep. Crane's Gen. Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Ike didn't advertise his capabilities either, and he called that peace with a perfectly straight face.

  1. Was "peaceful but not nonmilitary" an honest distinction or a lawyer's dodge?
  2. Should the United States still rely on it when China and Russia put military systems in orbit? (S)
Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 9

Ike Builds a Sparrow, Then Feeds It Steak

McDougall argues that the conventional picture of a tight-fisted Eisenhower misses half the story. Ike founded NASA, protected it from the military, handed it the big missions, and tied it to national prestige. The first pick for administrator, Hugh Dryden, sank himself by telling Congress that putting a man in orbit in a Redstone nose cone was like the "circus stunt of shooting a young lady from a cannon." Using the word circus in front of Congress is always a gamble. T. Keith Glennan got the job instead, with Dryden landing softly as deputy, and both were sworn in on August 19, 1958. To give NASA muscle, the administration raided the Army, politely and in installments. Glennan bid for JPL in October 1958. In September 1959 the Pentagon gave military space operations to the Air Force, the Army's Saturn ran short of money, and the von Braun team moved to NASA effective July 1, 1960. Ike also gave manned spaceflight to NASA in August 1958, in part because the Air Force looked ready to grab it and run. The Air Force pitch, "Man-In-Space-Soonest," promised eventual moon landings for $1.5 billion, all in, a quote that deserved a warranty card. Glennan and Dryden named Project Mercury on November 26.

The one thing to remember is a dinner. On December 10, 1959, the Greenewalt Committee, chaired by DuPont's president, met at NASA and then went to the White House. Its "science group" wanted about $100 million a year. Its "space race group" wanted $2 billion a year, and you can guess which group had read the room. After dinner, in the White House basement, Vice President Nixon spoke for forty-five minutes without notes. Politics, he said, outranked science, which the science group presumably took well. The real motive was prestige, and the years that mattered would be 1963 to 1966, when the question would be "how many more Soviet successes can we stand." He said he would trade space for medical programs or foreign aid if Congress would buy them, but it wouldn't. The NSC agreed in spirit, in its own dialect: NSC 5918, approved January 12, 1960, made it an objective to achieve and demonstrate overall American superiority in space, without needing to lead in every phase. The NASA budget for FY 1961 came to $964 million after Congress added $50 million. For an agency told to be sensible, it ate well.

Opinion: Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means. By that test, Glennan saw the problem in May 1959, when he praised ARPA for treating space as just another environment and admitted that NASA could not do the same. "It had no objective." So NASA borrowed one. Its budget testimony leaned on the Soviet challenge, and McDougall says plainly that NASA needed the Soviets. An agency whose budget grows with the threat develops a certain fondness for the threat. Keep that in mind when reading the newest Iran war estimate: over $47 billion, over $29 billion of it for replacing munitions.

If NASA's budget depended on the Soviet challenge, did Eisenhower build an institution that had every reason to keep the race going? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 10

Everyone Wanted More Government Except the Government

McDougall's claim is that by 1960 a new consensus had formed against Eisenhower, and it was bipartisan, intellectual, and expensive. Civilian strategists like Brodie and Kahn claimed strategy as their turf, and Kahn wanted the national budget raised 10 to 20 percent. Retired generals joined in, as retired generals do. Maxwell Taylor sold "flexible response," Medaris wanted the civilian staff of the Secretary of Defense cut by 90 percent (growth, apparently, had exceptions), and Gavin called space the theater of tomorrow's strategy. Walt Rostow offered "take-off" economics. The Rockefeller panels called for growth held at 4 percent or higher, and the Seaborg Report wanted science funding raised as fast as possible. Ike was the odd man out: the executive branch was the only voice calling for less of itself, a sentence nobody has needed to write much since. Then came 1960. Rostow's January 2 campaign memo told the Democrats to hit the missile gap. CIA worst cases ran as high as 100 Soviet ICBMs by 1960 and 400 in 1961, but U-2 photos seemed to show none deployed at all, a generous margin of error even by intelligence standards. Kennedy raised the "space gap" over twenty times in the campaign, and Nixon answered about half as often. After the election, a panel priced a manned lunar landing at an extra $26 to $58 billion. Ike said he was "not about to hock his jewels" and vetoed Apollo. He approved the Saint satellite interceptor, so a few jewels did leave the safe.

The one thing to remember is three press conferences, a week apart, in which Ike declined to panic on schedule. A reporter asked if American prestige was at stake in space. Ike doubted it. Pressed, he said: "Not particularly, no." A week later, asked about urgency, he said, "What you want is enough," and added that a deterrent gains nothing once it is fully adequate. A week after that, asked about Khrushchev saying the Soviet flag on the moon proved communism superior, he called the remark crazy and said Americans should not get hysterical. He was right on the facts and could not prove it, because the proof was in classified U-2 photographs. On May 1, 1960, a Soviet missile shot down Francis Gary Powers. Ike's people finally had evidence they could point to, and Ike lost the Paris summit. Some trade.

Opinion: Crane quotes FEAF's Gen. Smart in Korea: "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Ike had the opposite problem. He couldn't advertise his strengths, and silence looked like weakness. Gaddis's Truman people believed resources were limited. Ike believed it too, and "enough" was his whole strategy in two syllables. The Farewell Address that ends this Part, with its warnings about a "military-industrial complex" and a "scientific-technological elite," reads to me like eight years of saying "enough," compressed into one speech. The conclusion's numbers show what a hard sell that was: federal R&D went from $6.2 billion in 1955 to $14.3 billion in FY 1961, on his watch. So much for enough.

Is "enough" a usable standard for deterrence when the other side gets to define the contest as prestige? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 11

Rockets Off the Line Like Sausages, Allegedly

Part IV turns to Moscow, and McDougall's opening claim is that Khrushchev was a true believer who staked his whole program on a rocket force that was mostly stage set. In 1958 he called an extraordinary party congress for January 1959 to adopt a Seven Year Plan that would carry the USSR to full communism. Ambitious deadlines were a house specialty. Sputnik became the proof. Within weeks the party line had four themes: the satellites proved the Soviet ICBM was real, proved socialism superior, served only peace, and showed Khrushchev's personal genius. (The fourth was not optional.) Khrushchev sneered that the Americans were planning a satellite of eleven kilograms, while Sputnik II weighed 508. He told the party congress that ICBMs were coming off the line "like sausages." McDougall says flatly that the missile gap was a myth and that the USSR trailed in every meaningful category of missile technology. The sausage factory was mostly signage. The bluff still worked on allies. In June 1955 only 6 percent of West Europeans thought the West militarily weaker. By 1960 Britons judged the Soviets stronger than the United States by 59 to 15 percent. An April 1960 poll found only one Frenchman in fourteen expecting the United States to prevail over twenty years. Advertising works, even when the product doesn't.

The one thing to remember is the Nedelin disaster. In September 1960 Khrushchev sailed into New York with model spaceships in his luggage, presumably to celebrate a Mars shot. Two Mars rockets fizzled on October 10 and 14, which made the luggage awkward. On the evening of the twenty-third, with the launch window closing, a third failed to ignite. Marshal Nedelin, commander of the new Strategic Rocket Forces, ordered technicians out of their blockhouses to look at it. The rocket exploded. Estimates of the dead run from scores to hundreds. Moscow announced that Nedelin had died in a plane crash. An official biography in 1979 upgraded that to dying "tragically in the performance of his official duties," the Soviet idea of a prompt correction. On December 2 a capsule carrying two dogs burned up on reentry, and the next day Korolev had a heart attack. He went back to work, and on April 12, 1961, Gagarin orbited the earth. The capsule came down near the towns of Marx and Engels. McDougall calls it an appropriate spot, and it is hard to argue.

Opinion: We have seen this movie before, in Zhang. Over the Yalu, Soviet pilots flew in Chinese uniforms, and both sides inflated their kill claims. Moscow's habit was the same in 1960: hide the real thing, advertise the version that helps. Korolev stayed a nonperson, and the launch site was placed on maps near Baikonur, about 200 miles from where it really was, which is cartography as performance art. Khrushchev's memoirs admit he chased "maximum political advantage," a rare burst of candor for the genre. For the Dec. 1 paper, that is a useful warning about Taiwan scenarios. A rival's claimed capability and its real one can differ by a lot, and allies' opinion polls can follow the claim.

  1. If Soviet space leadership was largely bluff, why did it move European opinion so far?
  2. What does that say about deterrence that rests on perception? (S)
Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 12

Khrushchev Goes All In Holding a Pair of Twos

McDougall opens chapter 12 with six words that carry the whole thing: "For it was all a bluff." While Khrushchev mocked American massive retaliation, the USSR had yet to deploy a single ICBM, which takes a certain confidence. The SS-6 that lofted Sputnik was huge because Soviet rocketry was primitive: clusters of small RD-107 engines, a range of only about 4,340 miles, unstorable fuel, and radio guidance. By 1958 the Kremlin decided against deploying it, which made the world's most famous missile a very good launch vehicle. The first confirmed count of deployed Soviet ICBMs is 150, in 1965. Meanwhile about thirty-five SS-6s went into space shots, which had more publicity value than a baby missile force had military value. The Americans led roughly 194 to 72 in ICBMs by late 1962. The logic was simple: if the enemy thinks you stronger than you are, you can run a diplomacy of intimidation. The bill came anyway. The Americans did not blink over Berlin, and Mao took the boasts at face value and demanded to know why Moscow was so timid, the hazard of convincing an ally too well. The bluff also froze space law, because cooperation meant showing the Americans the Semyorka booster, or in Khrushchev's own words, "both giving away our strength and revealing our weakness."

The scene to remember is the U-2. In May 1960 the Soviets shot down Powers's spy plane, and two days later Khrushchev named Nedelin to command the Rocket Troops and bragged of Soviet superiority in delivery systems. To show that the U-2 had found no missile bases, he displayed film allegedly from Powers's flight: airfields and factories, no ICBMs. McDougall's deadpan comment: "there were none to photograph." Khrushchev said the pictures proved the spy flights went over regions with no rocket bases. Technically, every region qualified. The real threat to the bluff, McDougall argues, was the spy satellite, and from late 1959 through 1963 the Soviet chorus against satellite reconnaissance droned on at the UN with the passion of a man guarding an empty safe. The act ran from Sputnik to the aftermath of the Cuban missile crisis, seven years without an intermission.

Opinion: readers of Crane have met this playbook before, on a smaller stage. Crane's subject in Korea was "the image and reality of American airpower," and FEAF's General Smart admitted "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Khrushchev ran the same rule at the level of national strategy, and it worked for as long as nobody could look. Zhang adds that USAF leaders credited Soviet MiG pilots to the Chinese to avoid stirring public demand for war with Russia, so Washington was in the image business too. Both superpowers learned early that a carefully managed image can buy time. Khrushchev just bought seven years of it on credit, and the U-2 and Discoverer came to collect.

If the bluff could survive only as long as the Soviet Union stayed closed, did American reconnaissance do more to end the missile gap than any American missile? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 13

Moscow Learns to Love the Spy Satellite

Chapter 13 argues that Sputnik handed the Kremlin a crisis of success. If Sputnik was a technological Pearl Harbor, the Soviets, like the Japanese, "found they had awakened a sleeping giant." Khrushchev used the ICBM as a weapon against his own army, which is not the target the designers had in mind. He sacked Marshal Zhukov in October 1957, and in January 1960 he justified a one-third cut in the standing army by telling the Supreme Soviet that almost the entire air force was being replaced with rockets. It was false, and it made enemies of nearly every branch of the military, a rare feat of joint operations. McDougall compares it to Eisenhower's New Look, with one catch: soldiers came cheap in the USSR and high technology did not. Within a year Khrushchev retreated. The defense budget shot up 29 percent in a single year, and the army cuts were restored. Then came the second irony. Marshal Sokolovsky's Military Strategy appeared in the summer of 1962, just as the Soviets were finally ready to deploy ICBMs, and they woke up to find that "it was the space program that represented a bluff." For all the Luniks and Vostoks, they were at least five years behind in the military satellites a real missile force needs.

Remember a satellite with a deliberately boring name. At the start of 1962 the United States had launched sixty-three payloads, thirty-four of them military; the USSR had launched fifteen, none presumptively military. On April 6, 1962, a Soviet satellite went up from Tyuratam, orbited for three days, and parachuted back to earth inside the USSR with film. It was "labeled, blankly, Kosmos IV." The country that had spent years calling American spy satellites illegal now had its own, and by 1965 it was launching more than twice as many as the United States. Converts are always the most zealous. It even built a secret military launch site at Plesetsk, which the Kremlin never publicly acknowledged. Once both sides were spying, there was little left to argue about. In December 1963 the Soviets ended their three-year tirade against American spy satellites, and a UN declaration of legal principles for space passed that month. The old RAND hope of open skies had come true, by mutual hypocrisy.

Opinion: Khrushchev's January 1960 speech is the Soviet version of a story from Crane. After Korea, the ambiguous ending let the Air Force think it had succeeded, and it went back to planning for general war and paid for it in Vietnam. McDougall notes the American Air Force accepted the deterrence mission partly because manned bombers still called for human character, while the USSR had no bomber phase at all. Khrushchev bet everything on one weapon and lost the bet in a year. The service that declares the other forces obsolete usually gets a front-row seat for their comeback.

Khrushchev tried to replace an army with rockets and reversed himself within a year. Is there any American case of a one-weapon strategy that survived contact with a real crisis? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 14

Three Cosmonauts, No Space Suits, No Khrushchev

Chapter 14 and the Part IV conclusion argue that Khrushchev staked his rule on the scientific-technical revolution, and that it devoured him, as revolutions will. The 1961 Party Program named the conquest of space as one of its pillars. The money was real: Soviet R&D grew 17 to 18 percent a year through 1960, rose from 1.2 to 2.8 percent of GNP between 1955 and 1964, and paid for ten new scientific cities and a doubling of science graduate students, 48,000 in 1965 against 21,400 in 1955. The politics were a disaster. Khrushchev alienated the army, heavy industry, the ideologues, the KGB, the provincial Party, and even the scientists, who resented his patronage of Lysenko. Losing the people you are paying takes real talent. By October 1964, Medvedev wrote, not a single power group of any size would have backed him. McDougall's summary: "So Space Age communism fizzled like a capitalist rocket." As his projects failed, Khrushchev leaned harder on space spectaculars. In August 1961 Titov flew a full day in orbit and called Khrushchev the cosmonauts' "space father," and a week later Soviet troops sealed off East Berlin, a reminder of what the father did on the ground. In June 1963 Tereshkova flew, a sport parachutist with no astronautical training, which mostly proved the test pilots had been passengers too.

The story to remember is Voskhod I. By late 1963 the Americans had sketched out the two-man Gemini, so Khrushchev ordered a three-man capsule launched before the next anniversary of the revolution, a deadline no engineer picked. The only hardware available was the one-man Vostok. Korolev's team stripped it of everything but life support and crammed in three seats. There was no room for space suits, which was advertised as proof of routine spaceflight. Safety systems were dropped for weight, which was advertised as proof of reliable rockets. A bigger parachute had to bring down the whole capsule, test monkeys died in the impact, so a still heavier parachute went in and the cosmonauts went on diets like wrestlers making weight. Voskhod I flew on October 12, 1964, and Pravda headlined "Sorry Apollo!" It came down after a single day. Khrushchev's own landing came next: on October 13 he was relieved of all his Party posts. Korolev, who finally got to propose his own sensible plans, died on the operating table in January 1966 at fifty-eight.

Opinion: here is a Korea hook nobody expects. Zhang argues that Stalin's death in March 1953, not air pressure, broke the armistice deadlock. Airpower students may want to sit down for this next part. McDougall reports that Soviet scholars dated the American scientific-technical revolution from that same year, 1953, because the Korean War had locked in large-scale government R&D and the fusion of science, industry, and government. In Moscow's reading, the most lasting thing the Korean War produced was a permanent American research machine. Khrushchev then tried to out-build that machine with a system that could not take the strain.

McDougall says a dictator facing bureaucratic resistance has three weapons, the Party, personality, and terror, and that Khrushchev threw away all three. Did the space program make him stronger, or only more visible when he failed? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 15

Ends: The Moon. Means: To Be Determined.

Part V opens in a Boston bar. In the 1950s the maître d' at Locke-Ober's introduced Jack and Bobby Kennedy to MIT's Charles Stark Draper, hoping to sell them on rocketry, and the brothers dismissed the whole business. Even after Sputnik, Senator Kennedy could not be convinced rockets were anything but a waste of money. Yet on May 25, 1961, he asked Congress to land a man on the moon before the decade was out, in a speech where space made up only a fifth of the text. Few conversions have come with a bigger price tag. McDougall's argument is that the moon decision was an act of image-making by a technocratic team. The Kennedy men thought of space "in terms of ends" rather than means. Jerome Wiesner's science panel warned against a prestige race for man in space, and Kennedy made Wiesner his science adviser and set the report aside, a promotion and a filing in one motion. An Air Force bid to take over manned spaceflight went nowhere. Then Gagarin flew. Kennedy asked his advisers, "Is there any place we can catch them?" Lyndon Johnson's survey answered yes, with NASA estimating at least $11.4 billion extra over ten years, and McNamara signed on partly because Apollo would keep a troubled aerospace industry busy outside his own budget. Every defense secretary loves a program someone else pays for. The final message asked Congress for upward of $20 billion.

The person to remember is James Webb, the man who did not want the job. Webb was a lawyer, Marine reserve pilot, and oilman who had served Truman as Budget Director and axed the early ICBM and satellite programs, an unusual résumé for the space job. When he arrived in Washington in January 1961, he told Hugh Dryden he did not think the job was for him. Dryden agreed. Webb sent a friend to plead with Johnson, and the friend got chased out of the office. The publisher of the Washington Post sent him to Clark Clifford, who had recommended him in the first place. Washington guards its exits well. On Monday he went to the Oval Office, where Kennedy said he wanted someone who understood policy, and Webb said yes because he had never said no to a President. Even then he would not propose the moon himself. He wanted NASA to be asked to take the risk by an anxious nation, and five years later he reminded Johnson that LBJ had "to almost drive me" to make the recommendation. Webb kept receipts.

Opinion: this chapter is Gaddis with the sign flipped. Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means, and he notes that Truman's people believed resources were limited. McDougall's Kennedy team, armed with the new economics and a pay-any-price inaugural, decided the goal first and assumed the economy would grow to cover it. That can work when the goal is a moon landing, since the moon does not shoot back. It is a riskier habit when the goal is a war, and the November 2 Vietnam decision exercise is a good place to test it.

If the moon decision settled the end and never seriously asked about the means, was it a strategy at all, or just a very expensive slogan? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 16

Buy the Missiles Now, Find the Strategy Later

McDougall's charge is that Kennedy's men, sure of their own brilliance and eager to look nothing like Eisenhower, made decisions in their first four months that set the space program for a decade and America's strategic posture for more than two. A December 1960 Weapons Systems Evaluation Group study called for about forty Polaris submarines and about 900 Minutemen. SAC, never knowingly undersold, asked for 10,000 ICBMs. McNamara told Kennedy that 950 Minutemen was the lowest number he could take to Congress and "not get murdered." The country ended up with 41 Polaris boats and exactly 1,000 Minutemen, round numbers being the last refuge of the budgeteer. The strategy that supposedly justified all this, the "no-cities" counterforce doctrine McNamara unveiled at Ann Arbor in June 1962, was going obsolete by late 1963. Its replacement, assured destruction, needed no new missiles and no new space weapons, awkward for whoever had just bought them. Then the whiz kids brought their planning-programming-budget system and systems analysis to space, decided that space was merely a place, blessed passive satellites as stabilizing, and branded orbital weapons destabilizing. Dyna-Soar, the Air Force spaceplane, died in December 1963 after seven years and $400 million. The Manned Orbiting Laboratory was announced the next day, so the mourning period was brief.

Remember how the missile gap died. On February 6, 1961, McNamara admitted at an off-the-record briefing that each side seemed to have about the same small number of missiles. Republicans then sarcastically congratulated Kennedy on closing the gap in eighteen days, which may be the fastest defense program in history. Then the cameras went up. Samos 2 orbited on January 31, 1961, and in June the CIA's estimate of Soviet missiles dropped, without explanation, from 120 to 60, the intelligence version of whistling and studying the ceiling. Discoverer 25 flew on June 16, 1961, missed its recovery area, and plunged into the sea, and navy frogmen fished the film out. The count fell to at most fourteen. McDougall's line: "The first satellite sparked the missile gap; the first reconnaissance satellites snuffed it out." By November the New York Times reported some 233 American missiles able to reach the Soviet Union against fifty or fewer pointed the other way. There was a gap after all; it just ran in our favor. The buildup went ahead anyway, and in 1967 McNamara conceded the force was more than the country needed.

Opinion: this is Gaddis's definition of strategy, relating ends to means, run backwards. The Kennedy team bought the means first and then changed the ends twice, like buying the boat and then looking for a lake. It is also a cousin of Crane's FEAF commander, Gen. Smart, who said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." Khrushchev, McDougall notes, was still blustering and bragging while American satellites counted his missiles, so the Soviets were following the same rule, loudly. The difference from Korea is that this time the reconnaissance actually worked, and Washington built 1,000 Minutemen anyway. The Air Force, meanwhile, got the budget and lost the authority, a trade it would keep making.

Seminar question: if the satellites proved by late 1961 that there was no gap, was the 1,000-Minuteman force a hedge against Soviet surprise or the purchase that made the Soviet buildup of the late 1960s inevitable? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 17

Space for All Mankind, Terms and Conditions Apply

McDougall's argument is that American space diplomacy in the 1960s preached sharing and practiced self-interest, and that this was mostly the right call. He calls the result a "benign hypocrisy": cooperation in science, competition in engineering, agreements where the United States was safely ahead and laissez-faire where the race was still on. He traces it on four axes. On spy satellites, McGeorge Bundy imposed a blackout in January 1961, and the Air Force was forbidden even to mention satellite observation in public, a gag order on a service not famous for modesty. NSAM 156 of May 1962 kept the blackout while backing bans on nuclear weapons in space and refusing any other limit on military uses. On cooperation with Moscow, the grand talk shrank to a June 1962 memorandum covering weather data and a test on the Echo 2 satellite, so superpower friendship began, fittingly, with the forecast. Webb said the effort to project an image of America leading and sharing mattered more than cooperation itself, which was at least honest. On Europe, the line was cooperation in science and decided aloofness in engineering. On comsats, the Communications Satellite Act needed the Senate's first cloture vote since 1927 to beat a liberal filibuster, and Kennedy signed it on August 31, 1962. By July 1964 INTELSAT gave the Comsat Corporation a 61 percent share, the Europeans 30.5 percent, and the United States a veto. That is sharing, with a controlling interest.

Remember Telstar. In July 1962 a satellite built by AT&T and launched by NASA, a mere thirty-five pounds and three feet across, sent the first live television between continents. It was the first satellite of any kind built with private money. It made exactly the impression the administration wanted the whole comsat effort to make, and a British band even named a hit record after it, which is more than NSAM 156 ever got. Six months later Telstar was dead, knocked out by radiation from American high-altitude nuclear tests. The symbol of space uniting mankind was taken out by its own country's bombs, which is a fair summary of the decade.

Opinion: McDougall's sharpest line is about the blackout. The Soviets knew what the satellites were doing, foreign experts knew, and the world's opinion makers assumed the worst anyway. "Only the American public was being fooled." That should sound familiar from Zhang, where Soviet pilots flew MiGs over the Yalu in Chinese uniforms and USAF leaders credited the Chinese to avoid stirring up public demand for war with Russia. Both governments were in on it. The voters back home got the costume. The same pattern holds for Crane's "image and reality of American airpower": the image was built for a domestic audience that was the only one buying it.

Seminar question: if keeping secrets that the adversary already knows only fools your own public, when is that a legitimate tool of limited war and when does it erode the consent a democracy needs to keep fighting? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 18

Houston, We Have a Constituency

McDougall's argument is that James Webb turned Apollo from a race with the Russians into a model for running the whole country. Webb, a Carolina lawyer who had been a budget-cutter for Truman, learned to think big in the Oklahoma oil business and came to believe bigness was the point. NASA grew around the moon goal into a crescent of installations along the Gulf from Houston to Cape Canaveral, built while Texans and Oklahomans ran the committees that paid for them. Pure coincidence, surely. Webb spread the money on purpose: 90 percent of NASA's funds went to private firms, and he steered grants to second-rank universities to grow expertise in more regions, and please more congressmen. By FY 1964 NASA's budget passed $5 billion, five times what it had been three years before, and the federal government bought over 78 percent of all American R&D. Webb told audiences that the viability of representative government and free enterprise was being tested in space, which is a heavy payload for one rocket. He made universities promise to help their regions use space knowledge, and he had harsh words for professors who dragged their feet. McDougall's verdict is that technocracy spread across the land with almost no philosophical debate. Apparently nobody had a grant for that.

Remember how the Manned Spacecraft Center found its way to Texas, if "found" is the word. On May 23, 1961, two days before Kennedy asked for the moon, Webb wrote Lyndon Johnson that the moon message would go up on Wednesday and, without pausing for a new paragraph, got down to business. Congressman Albert Thomas, who ran the Appropriations Committee, was very interested in Rice University, which had set aside some 3,800 acres for research. Former administrator Keith Glennan, asked by his own governor to help pitch Ohio, laughed that 25 states were doing the same and said "I'll lay you a year's salary that that Center is going to Houston." Nobody took the bet, which shows good judgment. On the command module contract, Martin won the evaluation and North American got the award. A North American lobbyist explained that the winner was five times as large as the loser, so there were five times as many happy people. "That's democracy in its finest form." Arithmetic has rarely been put to finer use.

Opinion: Gaddis says Truman's people believed resources were limited, and Webb spent the late 1940s cutting budgets for exactly that Truman. Fifteen years later the same man treated money as the cure for everything from science to regional poverty and, by 1965, was asking whether America could force the Communists to cooperate by building so much power there was no alternative. There is no zealot like a reformed budget-cutter. That is the full arc from Kennan's strongpoints to an unlimited perimeter, run through a space agency. Even von Braun had to remark that "space is bigger than Texas." Webb did not appear to agree.

Seminar question: when a national project wins political support by spreading its contracts to as many districts as possible, does that make it more durable in a long contest, or does it guarantee the program outlives the strategy that justified it? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 19

Everyone Hated Apollo, So Everyone Funded It

McDougall's argument, sharpened in the Part V conclusion folded into this chapter, is that the great Apollo debate of 1962 to 1964 was loud and settled nothing important, because nearly every side had already accepted technocracy. Scientists complained: one straw poll found non-NASA scientists opposed to Apollo by 110 to 3, the kind of margin usually seen only in rigged elections. Eisenhower asked in August 1962 why the great hurry, and in June 1963 called spending $40 billion to reach the moon "just nuts." Goldwater, who had lectured the Air War College in 1960 on the vanity of chasing world opinion, wanted the money moved to military space. Liberals like the sociologist Amitai Etzioni wanted it moved to social programs. Nobody, you will notice, proposed giving it back. McDougall concludes that in the Kennedy years American political resistance to technocracy withdrew, and the danger he names is "a technocracy of politicians" setting a national agenda and ordering up the hardware and management to deliver it. Rule by technicians was never the threat; the technicians just filled the order. By mid-October 1964, 77 percent of Americans wanted Apollo continued at its pace or faster. So much for the great debate.

Remember Kennedy's offer to go to the moon together. With the NASA budget under attack and a British astronomer reporting that the Soviets had no manned lunar program, Kennedy proposed at the UN on September 20, 1963, that the two countries do the big things together. He got no answer, which in diplomacy is still an answer. A month later Khrushchev said the Soviets were not planning a lunar flight, quoted a joke about people who cannot bear earth flying to the moon, and added, "But we are all right on earth." The offer still had an effect, just not on Moscow. The Senate kept its half-billion-dollar cut, which slipped the first landing from 1967–68 to 1968–69, but beat Fulbright's further 10 percent cut 36 to 49 and passed a rider forbidding a joint lunar landing without the consent of Congress. Offering to share the moon persuaded Congress to keep it. Nine days after the rider passed, Kennedy was dead.

Opinion: Lyndon Johnson then took the technocratic faith further than anyone. In St. Louis in 1964 he listed eight ways to fight Communism, with science and space in third place and the Great Society in fifth, all on the same list as the defense establishment. Most strategists would settle for one that works. McDougall puts the contrast plainly: for Eisenhower, courage meant resisting dangerous tools, and for Johnson it meant daring to pick them up in a good cause, in cities, in space, and in Third World jungles. Gaddis's Truman figured $20–30 billion would keep the peace, and Ike thought $40 billion for the moon was nuts. Inflation, it seems, hits grand strategy too. Monday's Vietnam decision exercise and Clodfelter's Limits of Airpower are where Johnson's faith gets its test, and civilian space spending had already peaked in FY 1965. As LBJ put it, some people's feet got tired.

Seminar question: if the Apollo fight of 1963 was really an argument over which technocratic goal to fund, did the same habit of mind shape how Johnson's team chose to fight in Vietnam? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 20

Space for Peace, Orbit for Spies

McDougall opens Part VI with Apollo 8 circling the moon on Christmas Eve, 1968, then names two ironies. First, the means, technocracy, came to matter more than the end, though it would fail on most of the new national agenda. Going to the moon was an engineering problem. Ending poverty was not. Second, the moon was never what space was about. Space was about spy satellites, comsats, and national advantage, and Chapter 20 shows the rules being written to keep it that way. Johnson called for a space treaty in April 1966, after the Pentagon and Joint Chiefs had made sure it would not outlaw military activity. Peace, with the fine print pre-cleared. Talks stalled over a Soviet demand for tracking stations on other countries' soil until the Soviet negotiator folded on October 4, the Sputnik anniversary, which shows either sentiment or a sense of humor. The Senate ratified the Outer Space Treaty 88 to 0 on April 25, 1967. It denuclearized space and demilitarized the moon but left orbit open to military use. McDougall reads that as a low-key American win, locked in while only two powers mattered in space.

Then came the bust. Nixon picked the cheapest of three plans from Spiro Agnew's Space Task Group, then deferred even its space station. The shuttle got through in 1972 largely because aerospace layoffs in California, Texas, and Florida had an electoral logic, the one kind of logic that never needs a systems analyst. Even before the first landing, McDougall writes, "the American hare had stopped again to take a nap." The tortoises kept running. France orbited Asterix-1 from the Sahara in 1965, Europe flew Ariane on Christmas Eve, 1979, and in 1973 the USSR launched 124 spacecraft to 23 American.

Remember the Senate hearing. Ambassador Arthur Goldberg, jet-lagged from an eleven-day Asian tour, tried to explain what the treaty meant by space for the benefit of all countries and produced a sentence the transcript had to flag twice with [sic]. Senator Gore was unimpressed: "I can almost use the word fuzzy." Six days later Goldberg called it a freedom-of-the-seas clause, nothing more. Then on April 12 General Earle Wheeler said the Joint Chiefs backed the treaty, but it required intensified efforts to detect orbiting nuclear weapons. Senator John S. Cooper asked if that meant more money for military space R&D. "That is correct, Senator." A peace treaty whose top military witness asked for a raise passed without a single no vote.

Opinion: Goldberg's freedom-of-the-seas answer is the whole regime in one phrase, and Americans still think that way about contested high ground. The Army is now exploring balloons above 60,000 feet, in international airspace, to watch, relay, and strike along China's anti-access network. That is the 1967 bargain one layer down: keep the commons open, ban only the apocalyptic, and let the militaries sort out the rest. From the moon to balloons is a long way down, but the logic made the trip intact. Seminar question: Was the treaty's vagueness, which McDougall suggests may have been a boon, a wise way to keep a new domain open, or did it simply license the orbital competition Wheeler asked Congress to fund? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 21

Guns, Butter, and No Idea Why

Chapter 21 is McDougall's indictment of American technocracy, and he lets the critics run up the bill. The government spent $146 billion on R&D from Sputnik to the moon landing with no national plan and no goals to measure progress against, which is one way to make sure you never fall short. By 1960 it steered 35 percent of the national R&D effort into aerospace, an industry producing 4 percent of manufacturing value-added, while core industries with 60 percent of value-added got 8 percent. Wolfgang Panofsky put the spin-off argument away in one line: "If you want the by-product, you should develop the by-product." By 1963 Washington paid 88 percent of Caltech's budget, which made Caltech something like a very bright federal agency with a campus. The cures came from the same mentality as the disease. Armed forces procurement regulations swelled from 100 pages to 1,200 by 1967, a growth rate the aerospace industry itself might envy. McDougall's verdict is that the United States got "the worst of both worlds," a market bent by state intervention and a state unable to manage the change it set loose. It had to fight a total Cold War like the USSR while keeping democratic institutions like France or Japan, and the machine broke down.

Then he goes after systems analysis itself. A satellite is a closed system, a billiard-ball universe where every effect has a known cause. As the system grows to include the space program, the state, society, and the international system, the analysis drowns in contingency. Systems theorist C. West Churchman offered a test: a system's real objective is whatever its managers will knowingly sacrifice other goals to achieve. American technocracy promised growth and clean air, excellence and mass education, guns and butter, and declined to sacrifice any of them. Hence McDougall's best line: "The objective of American technocracy was to avoid defining its objective." Every system needs something outside itself to say its goals are worth pursuing, a Guarantor of Decisions, or G.O.D. Few acronyms in the defense world have aimed higher. Analysis cannot supply one, and it would never have endorsed a cathedral or a moonship in the first place.

The image to keep is McDougall's chess game. Picture a leisurely game by mail, where you gloat that if he uncovers his pawn your knight takes it, and if he doesn't your queen goes after his bishop. Now picture writing your orders twelve moves ahead, while players on bigger boards decide knights will not be funded next year, upgrade bishops to move like queens, and players on still bigger boards decide to switch everyone to parcheesi. Anyone who has lived through a budget cycle will recognize the board, and possibly the parcheesi.

Opinion: Churchman's test is Gaddis with a price tag. Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means, and Kennan wanted interests defined first and threats measured against them. Truman's people believed resources were limited, so they had to choose. McDougall's technocrats assumed more R&D would breed more wealth to fund more R&D, which let them skip the choosing. A strategy that will not name what it gives up is a wish list with a seal on it. Seminar questions, by Churchman's test:

  1. Has the United States ever had a real objective for airpower or space since Sputnik?
  2. If so, what did it knowingly sacrifice to get it? (S)
Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 22

The Orphan with the Gun

McDougall ends the book by stepping out of the archives and into philosophy, and he warns us he is doing it, which is more notice than most philosophers give. His thesis, stated one last time: the rivalry that began with the Communist victory of 1917 and peaked with Sputnik caused a leap in the politics of technology, turning the state into the main promoter and manager of technical progress. He borrows Alexander Gerschenkron's rule that the more backward a country, the more the state must force change, and adds the twist that in an age of perpetual innovation every country is permanently backward. Nobody gets to be caught up anymore. So wartime methods became normal, peacetime values were suspended, and the line between state and society blurred. Nations justify the pace by security, yet that progress may erode the very values that make a society worth defending.

Then he surveys how people tried to make sense of it. One camp simply surrendered to the machines. Dean Wooldridge, whose firm did systems integration on the Atlas, argued that man is essentially a complex machine, and systems theorist C. West Churchman concluded that the real question was how to design a god, presumably to spec. Religion offered three answers. The atheist Soviets said space probes were the last nails in God's coffin. Liberal clergy, like Bishop James Pike, traded preaching for counseling, which McDougall likens to the Wizard of Oz: behind the curtain stood the same old social engineer. The orthodox, from Pius XII to Wernher von Braun, called technology a neutral tool; von Braun thought the Ten Commandments adequate without amendments, a rare case of a rocket engineer declining an upgrade. McDougall dismisses the first, finds the second has stripped religion of what makes it religion, and calls the third naive, because technology has a logic of its own and pulls attention away from love, death, and justice.

Then he lets the air out of human power. Mount St. Helens erupted with the force of hundreds of megatons. Migrating plovers navigate from arctic to antarctic and back more precisely than a Minuteman, and without a single contractor. The nearest planet that might sustain life is at least five light-years away, so a moon voyage is a toe poked onto our own front porch. Oppenheimer thought he had become Death, the destroyer of worlds; McDougall says he was no such thing, which must have been a comfort somewhere. The enlightened view had it backward, with pride in our works and contempt for ourselves as stardust. McDougall flips it: spaceflight "will teach us to have contempt for our works and value for ourselves." That, he says, is true humility.

The image to keep comes from Bob Dylan. We stand with our technology, McDougall writes, like Dylan's orphan with his gun, fatherless and dangerous, and about as impressive before the cosmos as a fire in the sun. He predicts that science will soon reach hard limits. Uhuru, a modest satellite launched in 1970, found 161 X-ray sources, and physicists will keep splitting particles until there is nothing left to split. At that point science may become a branch of moral philosophy again, and the orthodox message becomes a guide: God made us, is disappointed in us, but loves us anyway. "Technology is our subcreation." We will be disappointed in it too, and we must love it anyway. Frankenstein's creature, he notes, ran amok only after people turned on it in hatred and fear. Program managers, take note.

The prescription is to avoid both mellonolatry, worship of the future, and misoneism, hatred of change, two words that will send most readers to a dictionary and few back out with any affection for them. We cannot banish the will to power, rooted as it is in human nature and the international imperative. We must compete for power without making power an end in itself, and "pursue knowledge without mistaking knowledge for truth." For a book about rockets, it ends on Dante and Plato, with fishes peering out of the sea at the true heaven. Try getting that ending past a program review.

Opinion: seminar has already met the opposite creed. In Crane's Korea, FEAF's General Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations," and the war's ambiguous ending let the Air Force think it had succeeded and go back to planning for general war, a bill paid in Vietnam. McDougall's advice is to confess the mortality of our works without turning on them or on ourselves. That is a good frame to carry into Clodfelter's The Limits of Airpower next week, a title General Smart would not have advertised, and a fair test for the 628 paper: preparing Airmen for a crisis means valuing the Airmen more than the kit. Seminar question: McDougall says the security-driven race for progress may erode the values that make a society worth defending, so is that a real risk for the airpower enterprise, or a luxury no Cold War service could afford? (S)

Zhang: seminar questions (from the syllabus)

  • How accurate are one side's official statistics?

    Not very. PLAAF claimed about 1.42 per loss (201); Soviets 1,309 for 335 MiGs (202); Americans 8 or 10 to 1, revised by some to about 2 to 1, partly because U.S. gun-camera hits earned credit while the Soviets counted only 75 percent of filmed hits as kills (202–203).

  • Does mere survival constitute victory?

    Zhang: the USAF held air superiority throughout, even by Chinese reckoning (203), yet the Communist air effort was crucial to keeping supply lines open despite one bomb per seven meters of track (204). The myth of victory cost the PLAAF in 1954, when it missed 70 percent of Nationalist intruders (212).

  • Did Korea bolster or hinder the Communist alliance?

    Both. Moscow got a tested MiG and a pinned-down America (207–208); Beijing got a 3 billion yuan debt paid off in 1964 and the lesson that Stalin was unreliable (208). Each side used the war for its own interests (7).

  • What were the sources of China's air limitations?

    Inexperience above all (8): about sixty-three flying hours (42), no aviation industry (7). Political limits too: Soviet restrictions on its pilots (139), Stalin's "the air force belongs to the state" (188), and Beijing's refusal to fly south of the 38th Parallel (211).

  • Is airpower inherently defensive?

    Chinese planners treated fighters as defensive and bombers as aggressive (46), and Korea left the PLAAF in an air-defense role (11, 210). But it was founded to support a Taiwan invasion (32), and after 1991 it decided it must play a major role (213).

  • Why did U.S. Air Force leaders publicly credit the Chinese air force for MiG victories in late 1951?

    They knew the MiGs that beat their bombers were flown by Soviets, but credited the Chinese to avoid stirring up public sentiment for a war with Russia. Z 133

  • Why did the second Chinese bomber raid on Taehwa-do (November 30, 1951) turn into a massacre?

    The PLAAF repeated the November 6 plan exactly and publicized it, U.S. intelligence warned the Sabres, and the Tu-2s arrived five minutes early, before their MiG cover, losing four bombers, three La-11s, and a MiG. Z 158-162

  • What does Zhang say finally broke the armistice deadlock in 1953?

McDougall: chapter recall (for Friday)

  • What does McDougall call the leap in state-funded R&D in the ten years up to 1965, and what happened to Western R&D budgets?

    A "saltation," an evolutionary leap in the state's relation to new knowledge, sparked by Sputnik; Western governments quintupled R&D funding for national goals. M 5–6

  • What was a sharaga, and what does McDougall conclude about its effect on Soviet aviation and rocketry?

    A prison design bureau where arrested engineers like Tupolev and Korolev kept working under guard; McDougall concludes it was no catastrophe, since the prisoners kept producing military designs such as the Katyusha. M 37–40

  • According to McDougall's Part I conclusion, what slowed Soviet rocketry in the 1930s and 1940s, and what put it back on schedule?

    War, not terror, slowed it; the German inheritance after 1945 put the Soviets back on schedule. M 65

  • What did Eisenhower admit to the Finletter Commission in 1947?

    That there was no war plan; the new global power had no operative plan for war with the Soviet Union, and only a dozen or so atomic warheads. M 93–94

  • What did RAND's October 1950 satellite report recommend for the first American satellite, and why?

    An "experimental" satellite on an equatorial orbit that never crossed Soviet territory, to establish "freedom of space" before reconnaissance satellites flew over the USSR. It went to the Air Force on October 4, 1950, seven years to the day before Sputnik 1. M 109–110

  • Why did the Eisenhower administration pick Vanguard over von Braun's Redstone for the first U.S. satellite?

    Protecting the secret reconnaissance satellite program and establishing "freedom of space" mattered more than being first, and a civilian-looking science rocket was less likely to draw a Soviet legal challenge than a military missile built by the Peenemünde team. M 121–124

  • What happened to Vanguard TV-3 on December 6, 1957?

    Billed by the White House as a real satellite attempt, it rose about four feet, exploded in flame, and fell back, leaving its little satellite chirping on the ground; the press called it Kaputnik and Flopnik. M 151–154

  • How did the 1958 Space Act divide American space activity?

    It created a civilian NASA for open, scientific space work and left military applications to the Defense Department, two parallel programs, aiming to keep the U.S. "a leader, not the leader" in space. M 171–172

  • What did NSC 5814/1 decide about missiles and the definition of outer space?

    It treated missile policy separately from space, so a UN space deal would not freeze missiles, and it avoided a fixed boundary, favoring a functional definition (anything in orbit is in space). M 179–180

  • How did NASA acquire the von Braun rocket team?

    After the Pentagon gave military space operations to the Air Force in September 1959 and the Army's Saturn ran into funding trouble, the ABMA team was transferred to NASA, effective July 1, 1960, and became the Marshall Space Flight Center. M 197–198

  • What did Eisenhower do about Apollo and Saint after the 1960 election?

    Faced with a panel estimate of an extra $26 to $58 billion for a lunar landing, he refused to "hock his jewels" and vetoed Apollo, but he approved development and testing of the Saint satellite interceptor. M 225–228

  • What was the Nedelin disaster and how did Moscow explain it?

    On October 23, 1960, a Mars-bound rocket exploded on the pad after Marshal Nedelin ordered technicians to inspect it, killing scores to hundreds; Moscow said Nedelin died in a plane crash. M 241–244

  • How many ICBMs had the Soviets actually deployed during Khrushchev's missile bluff, and what did they do with the SS-6s instead?

    None at first; the first confirmed total was 150 SS-7s in 1965, while about thirty-five SS-6s went into space launches, which had more publicity value than a small missile force had military value. M 249–252

  • What did Sokolovsky's 1962 Military Strategy reveal about the Soviet space program, and what was Kosmos IV?

    Just as the Soviets were ready to deploy ICBMs, they found the space program itself was the bluff, at least five years behind in military satellites; Kosmos IV, launched April 6, 1962, was their first recoverable spy satellite. M 269–272

  • What was Voskhod I, and what happened on the day after it launched?

    A one-man Vostok stripped and crammed with three cosmonauts without space suits, launched October 12, 1964, to beat the two-man Gemini; on October 13 it came home early and Khrushchev was relieved of all his posts. M 291–294

  • Why did James Webb insist that NASA be asked to go to the moon rather than propose it himself?

    He feared that if NASA appeared to initiate an expensive, risky project it would be abandoned when the mood changed, so he wanted the nation, through Johnson and Kennedy, to give NASA the task; he later wrote that LBJ had to almost drive him to recommend it. M 319–320

  • What finally killed the "missile gap," and what did the U.S. build anyway?

    Reconnaissance satellites (Samos 2 and Discoverer 25 in 1961) cut the Soviet count to at most fourteen missiles, yet the Kennedy team still built 41 Polaris boats and exactly 1,000 Minutemen. M 327–330

  • What does McDougall mean by "benign hypocrisy" in U.S. space diplomacy?

    The U.S. preached space for all mankind but cooperated in science while guarding engineering know-how, signing agreements only where it was safely ahead; McDougall judges this self-interest necessary. M 345–346

  • What did James Webb think NASA was ultimately for, beyond beating the Soviets to the moon?

    A model of government-industry-university management for social change: spreading R&D money across regions and universities to drive growth, education, and regional uplift. M 381–386

  • What happened to Kennedy's September 1963 UN offer of a joint moon mission?

    Moscow ignored it and Khrushchev said the USSR was not planning a lunar flight; in Washington the Senate defeated a further 10 percent NASA cut 36 to 49 and passed a rider barring a joint landing without congressional consent. M 395–396

  • What did the 1967 Outer Space Treaty ban, and what did it leave alone?

    It banned weapons of mass destruction in orbit and all weapons on the moon, but it left orbital space open to military use; the Senate ratified it 88 to 0 on April 25, 1967. M 417–419

  • What is McDougall's "G.O.D.," and why can't systems analysis provide one?

    A Guarantor of Decisions (or Destiny): something outside a system that confirms its goals are worth pursuing. Analysis can optimize toward a goal but cannot justify the goal, and American technocracy avoided defining its objective at all. M 447–448

    The enlightened view took pride in man's works and held man himself to be insignificant dust; McDougall predicts spaceflight will teach the reverse, contempt for our works and value for ourselves, which he calls true humility. M 455–458

  2. GaddisRecallWhat were Kennan's five vital centers, and what did he want done about them?
    • The United States, Great Britain, Germany and central Europe, the Soviet Union, and Japan, which he presented to National War College students. 29
    • Only one was in hostile hands, so the US aim was to keep the other four from falling under Soviet control. 29
    • That led to "strongpoint" defense of particular regions and the access to them, rather than "perimeter" defense of fixed lines. 57
  3. GaddisAnalyzeWhat logic connects Kennan's interests, threats, and means?
    • Because capabilities are finite, interests must be too, so you have to separate the vital from the peripheral. 31
    • Means should serve ends, because indiscriminate methods can corrupt the objective. 31
    • Interests are the standard for judging threats, never the reverse. A threat has no meaning except in relation to your interests. 31

    Comps angle: most failures in limited war start when threats begin defining interests.

  4. GaddisAnalyzeWhy did the "X" article create confusion that lasted for decades?
    • It ran anonymously in Foreign Affairs in 1947, but Arthur Krock revealed Kennan as the author. That made it read like official policy, and Walter Lippmann attacked it at length. 24–25
    • Careless drafting contradicted positions Kennan held inside the government, so in places he agreed more with Lippmann than with his own article. He couldn't clarify publicly until his memoirs twenty years later. 25
    • Its call for "unalterable counter-force at every point" reads like perimeter defense, even as Kennan was moving toward strongpoints. 57
  5. GaddisAnalyzeWhy did strongpoint defense appeal to the Truman administration before 1950?
    • It was consistent with the prevailing perception of means: the conviction that resources were limited no matter how dangerous the threat. 57–58
    • Kennan partly articulated and rationalized moves the administration was already making. He wasn't its sole author. 57
    • Economic aid looked cheaper than war: Truman compared spending $20–30 billion to keep the peace with $100 billion to fight a war. Acheson told Congress that "Recovery is the first objective." 61
    • The atomic monopoly let officials accept risks. Forrestal called the years before an adversary could strike back "our years of opportunity." 61
  6. CraneRecallState Crane's thesis in three sentences.
    • The book examines "the image and reality of American airpower." Leaders and the public expected too much, partly because the USAF did not admit its limits. 6
    • The USAF planned for quick air superiority followed by strategic bombing. It got the MiG-15, a sanctuary across the border, and a forced turn to interdiction and then "air pressure." 7–8
    • The ambiguous ending let the Air Force believe it had succeeded. It drew few lessons and returned to planning for general war, which cost it in Vietnam. 9, vii
  7. CraneRecallTrace three Washington-imposed limits on airpower in 1950.
    • Air action was first limited to south of the 38th Parallel. On 29 June 1950 Truman approved strikes in the North, and the JCS restriction was to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders. 23–24
    • After the bombing of Rashin, about 20 miles from the Soviet border, State Department fears led the JCS to bar further attacks there in September 1950. 44–45
    • In November 1950 the Yalu bridge attacks were restricted to the Korean side of the spans. 49–50
  8. CraneAnalyzeWhy did FEAF shift from interdiction to "air pressure"?
    • Interdiction disappointed. FEAF regretted the name STRANGLE, and Communist repair crews of up to 500,000 kept the railways running. 82–83
    • An April 1952 FEAF study concluded the rail campaign had not worked despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, at a cost of 243 aircraft destroyed. It proposed air pressure through selective destruction. 115–116
    • The June 1952 hydroelectric strikes, run jointly by the Navy and Air Force, destroyed about 90 percent of North Korea's electric power. 119–120
  9. CraneAnalyzeWhy wasn't the atomic bomb used in Korea?
    • Allied fear: after a 30 November 1950 press conference on the bomb, Attlee rushed to Washington. 58–59
    • Escalation fear cut both ways. In April 1951 Truman ordered nuclear weapons and more SAC bombers toward Okinawa, fearing global war, but the bombers stayed on Guam. 70
    • Practical limits: the 1951 HUDSON HARBOR tests found long delays and weak target evaluation, and the Air Staff concluded there were no suitable atomic targets. 71
    • Weyland opposed atomic use. The Manchurian airfields might not be neutralized, and the Soviets would learn from the strikes and use them for propaganda. 157–158
  10. CraneRecallWhat were the main interservice fights, and was air superiority ever secure?
    • Weyland said he fought the Navy as much as the Communists. Smart spent 40 percent of his time countering Army and Navy proposals. 7
    • On close air support, Marines delivered bombs in about 10 minutes against about 45 for the USAF. Almond carried his complaints to General Collins. 61–62
    • Air superiority was never secure: in September 1951 FEAF had about 90 F-86s against 500-plus MiGs and warned that superiority was "in jeopardy." 83–84
  11. Gaddis × CraneCompareThe atomic bomb was Truman's deterrent of choice in 1948 and unusable in Korea by 1951. Why?
    • Gaddis: before 1950 the atomic monopoly underwrote the risks of a cheap, economy-first containment. G 61
    • Crane: in Korea, allied fears (Attlee), escalation fears, and a lack of suitable targets made the bomb a threat rather than a tool. C 58–59, 70, 71

    Argue it: a weapon designed for general war has little use in a limited war whose limits exist to prevent general war.

  12. Gaddis × CraneCompareKennan said interests should define threats. Who in Korea failed to define the interest that airpower served?
    • Kennan's standard: interests first, threats judged against them, and means subordinate to ends. G 31
    • A 1953 FEAF study blamed policy makers for never stating limited-war objectives "compatible with air force capabilities." C 155
    • Airmen weren't candid either: "we certainly don't advertise our limitations." C 6

    Argue it: the failure was mutual. Policy makers didn't state the end, and airmen didn't state the limits of their means.

  13. Gaddis × CraneCompareKennan's "X" article and Smart's candor problem: what do they share as leadership failures?
    • Kennan wrote loosely enough that readers took a perimeter strategy from a strongpoint thinker. G 25, 57
    • Smart's Air Force didn't state its limits, so leaders expected more than airpower could deliver. C 6

    Argue it: in both cases, what the author left unsaid shaped policy more than what was said.

  14. Gaddis × CraneSynthesizeWas Korea fought as symmetrical or asymmetrical containment?
    • Commitment: defending South Korea at all reflects the expanded, symmetrical view that follows NSC-68. G ch. 4, 87 · to check
    • Conduct: Washington drew tight geographic limits on airpower (the 38th Parallel, borders, Rashin, the Yalu spans). C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50

    Argue it: a symmetrical commitment fought with asymmetrical restraint, and airpower was caught in the gap between the two.

  15. Gaddis × CraneSynthesizeWhy might the New Look have made Korea's lessons easy for the Air Force to ignore?
    • Crane: the ambiguous ending let the USAF believe it had won, and it returned to planning for general war under the New Look. C 9
    • Gaddis: the New Look is asymmetrical and nuclear-centered. G ch. 5, 125 · to check

    Argue it: a strategy that rewards nuclear general-war forces gives an institution little budget reason to study limited war.

  16. Crane × ZhangCompareSeminar 29 OctWhose limits were political, and whose were self-imposed?
    • Crane: Washington imposed political and geographic limits on American airpower. C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50
    • Zhang: US and Soviet operations were limited mainly by politics, while the PLAAF was limited by inexperience. Z 8
    • China had no aviation industry and relied on Soviet equipment, and its leaders were ground-war veterans. Z 6–7
  17. Crane × ZhangCompareSeminar 29 OctHow should a strategist treat one side's combat statistics?
    • Zhang warns that his kill ratios are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent reality, and that the two sides' reports rarely match. Z 10–11
    • He calls American victory claims "probably highly inflated" and criticizes the one-sided US official account. Z 5
    • Compare Crane's F-86-versus-MiG numbers. C 83–84
  18. Crane × ZhangSynthesizeSeminar 29 OctBoth superpowers feared a direct clash. How did that fear shape the air war on each side of the Yalu?
    • US side: borders off-limits, Rashin halted, Yalu spans restricted, and the 1951 atomic deployment. C 23–24, 44–45, 49–50, 70
    • Communist side: the Soviets held back to avoid a direct clash with the US, and Moscow and Beijing each pursued their own national interests. Z 7
    • Stalin's "cryptic promise" of air support, and his October telegram asking for Chinese troops that didn't mention it. Z 60, 62, 65
  19. Crane × ZhangSynthesizeSeminar 29 OctIs airpower inherently defensive? Answer with both books.
    • Zhang: PLAAF planners saw fighters as defensive and bombers as aggressive. The war left the PLAAF in an air-defense role. Z 46, 11
    • But China's first air-war concept came from planning to invade Taiwan. Z 6, 53
    • Crane: FEAF's ambitions were offensive (strategic bombing, then air pressure), but it spent the war fighting to hold air superiority. C 7–8, 83–84
  20. Gaddis × ZhangSynthesizeSeminar 29 OctApply Kennan's five-centers logic from the other side. Why did Korea matter to Moscow and Beijing differently?
    • Kennan ranked regions by industrial and military power. Korea wasn't one of his five centers. G 29
    • Zhang: Moscow and Beijing each used the war for their own national interests, and Stalin pushed China in while staying out himself. Z 7, 62

    Argue it: a peripheral theater for one ally can be a vital one for another, and alliances fracture along that line.

  21. ZhangRecallWhat did each air force claim in Korea, and why does Zhang trust none of the scoreboards?
    • PLAAF: 26,491 sorties, 231 losses admitted, about 1.42 kills per loss. Z 201
    • Soviets: 1,309 UN aircraft for 335 MiGs, sliding from 7.9 to 1 to 1.9 to 1 by 1953. Americans: 8 or 10 to 1. Z 202
    • U.S. gun-camera hits earned credit even with no confirmed crash; Soviets counted only 75 percent of filmed hits; some U.S. accounts now say about 2 to 1. Z 203

    Comps angle: kill ratios didn't decide the war; sortie rates (50 a day against 900) and air superiority did. Z 203–204

  22. ZhangAnalyzeWhy did the Chinese air force never give Peng Dehuai the ground support he demanded?
    • No usable airfields in Korea: two million man-days built fields the Soviets couldn't use, and UN bombers hit them 72 times. Z 111–113
    • Zhou would lose half a million to a million soldiers but not the new air force. Z 116
    • By late December 1951 the PLAAF settled for Chinese bases and a leash of about 100 miles; bombers were held back for fear of reprisal, and Stalin warned that "the air force belongs to the state." Z 171, 185, 188
  23. ZhangAnalyzeWhat lessons did the PLAAF draw from Korea, and what did they cost?
    • 3,653 combat reports concluded the human factor was still decisive. Z 205–206
    • It adopted Soviet defensive doctrine and put army officers in command. Z 210
    • In 1954 it failed to spot 70 percent of Nationalist intruders; veterans who believed they had beaten the Americans saw the Nationalists as little threat. Z 212
  24. ZhangRecallHow did Moscow hide its air war, and why did Washington go along?
    • North Korean markings, Chinese uniforms and pseudonyms, Chinese on the radio (few managed it), no flights over enemy territory or the sea. Z 139
    • USAF leaders knew the MiGs were Soviet and credited the Chinese to avoid public demand for war with Russia. Z 133

    Argue it: the shared fiction was escalation control that both sides chose.

  25. Crane × ZhangCompareBoth air forces left Korea with a flattering image. What did each pay for it?
    • Crane: the ambiguous ending let the USAF believe it had succeeded; it returned to general-war planning and paid in Vietnam. C 9, vii
    • Zhang: the PLAAF's victory myth fed self-delusion for more than forty years and a 70 percent failure to spot intruders in 1954. Z 212–213

    Argue it: an air force that can't admit a stalemate can't learn from one.

  26. Gaddis × ZhangSynthesizeRead Zhou Enlai's refusal to risk the air force through Gaddis's definition of strategy.
    • Gaddis: strategy relates ends to means; means must serve ends. G viii, 31
    • Zhang: Zhou would lose up to a million soldiers but not the new air force; pilots and jets were the scarce means. Z 116
    • The PLAAF had about 200 aircraft and pilots with under 100 hours against some 1,100 American combat aircraft. Z 100

    Argue it: Beijing protected the means it couldn't replace and spent the one it could.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
