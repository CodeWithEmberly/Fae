Seminar 12 · Fravel, Active Defense · "Unintended Effects: History of PRC Military Strategy"

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered

  1. Are the Chinese correct to view airpower within its larger defensive view of strategy? For a weaker state, yes, and Fravel shows the view loosening as China gets stronger. Active defense means waiting for the enemy's blow and then counterattacking, a civil-war doctrine for the weaker side that sits under all nine guidelines (chapter 2). After Kosovo the PLA judged air strikes the most likely way China would be attacked, and it answered with counter-air raid research, early warning, mobility, and surface-to-air missiles; it built countermeasures rather than copying NATO's air campaign (chapter 7). The loosening shows in the force: by the 2004 adjustment, ground attack and support aircraft made up almost half the air force, and Fravel himself says China's growing power raises serious questions about what active defense will mean.
  2. Is it context dependent? Yes, and Fravel says outright that the meaning of active defense varies from one guideline to the next (chapter 2). For four decades the context was an invasion, first an American amphibious assault and then a Soviet ground attack; for the last thirty years it has been local conflicts over limited aims on China's periphery (conclusion). Doctrine lagged the context: in the early 1990s it still aimed at a Soviet ground attack by defending fixed positions, which Fravel calls more or less what Iraq had failed to do (chapter 6). The 2017 base in Djibouti hints that the next context is overseas.
  3. How sensitive are Western powers to more subtle changes in an adversary's strategy? Less than they think, on Fravel's evidence. Guidelines are never announced, and the 2004 revision first surfaced in a footnote in the last entry of Jiang Zemin's Selected Works (chapter 1). Western scholars, Fravel included by his own admission, mislabeled the early-1980s strategy as "people's war under modern conditions," a phrase the CMC never used (introduction). He dated the 2014 change by counting a phrase in the Liberation Army Daily: 94 appearances, 81 of them after mid-August 2014 (chapter 7). His verdict is still that China's intentions are readable, for anyone willing to do that much reading.
  4. As this book is now eight years old, has anything changed within the PRC? Fravel named the gauges to watch, and several have moved. He flagged the 2018 slide in relations with Washington, new overseas missions after Djibouti, and technologies such as artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and robotics as possible triggers for a new strategy (conclusion). His last warning was that Xi's anticorruption drive, which had caught Zhou Yongkang, Xu Caihou, and Guo Boxiong, could open a split that blocks change. This week War on the Rocks, citing a February CSIS report, counted at least 100 senior military officials purged since 2022, including Generals Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli; the book can't tell us whether that is unity being enforced or a split opening.
  5. Has the United States or its allies experienced a similar reflection point when observing a foreign war? Yes, and from the same war. After the 1973 Yom Kippur War, Don Starry walked the Golan Heights, and he and William DePuy concluded that the Army's industrial-mobilization model of war was bankrupt and that the Army would have to win the first battle of the next war (Kitfield, chapter 14). The PLA sent a seven-person delegation to Egypt and Syria in January 1974, and its report named anti-tank and antiair operations as the war's defining features (Fravel, chapter 5). The American reformers moved within a few years; the PLA waited until 1980 for a united party. Fravel doesn't study the American case, so the comparison is ours.

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. (S)

Today's Reading · Fravel, Active Defense

China's Strategy Waits for Two Signals, and One of Them Is the Politburo

M. Taylor Fravel's argument fits in one sentence: China changes its military strategy when a war somewhere else shows that warfare has changed and the party at home is united enough to let its generals answer. Senior officers started and led eight of the nine strategies since 1949, and the party chairman only approved them. The ninth, Mao's 1964 return to luring the enemy in deep, was a weapon in a domestic fight. (Mao's enemies list that year was drawn up mostly in Beijing.)

For this course, Fravel is the second-order effect of the air wars we have already read. Korea was one of the wars behind the 1956 strategy. Olsen's Desert Storm, which Chinese analysts in late 1990 expected to bog down with air power in a marginal role, became the 1993 strategy. Kosovo's lesson, as the PLA read it, was how to survive air strikes. Each time the PLA asked what a war meant for China, the answer was rarely "build the same thing."

Opinion: airmen grade a campaign by what it did to the enemy in front of them. Fravel grades it by what the audience learned, and the audience was patient. The Gulf War lessons waited almost two years for a party congress, which is a long time to sit on a homework assignment. (S)

Counterpoint · Zhang against Fravel

Counterpoint: Zhang Says the Air Force Copied Moscow After All

Fravel's central claim is that China's military strategy changed when its own officers read foreign wars and a united party let them act, and that the rival theory, that late modernizers copy the leading military, fails even in its easiest case, 1950s China. He picks that case on purpose. China then had Soviet weapons, Soviet factories, Soviet advisors, and Soviet field regulations, and it still rejected the Soviet strategy of striking first. Peng Dehuai told Marshal Zhukov in May 1955 that Germany and Japan had struck first and lost anyway.

Xiaoming Zhang, whom we read on October 29, makes the strongest case against him, from inside the air force. At the Northeast Aviation School in 1947, Soviet-trained cadres argued that the new air force should follow the Soviet model; they won, Soviet teaching materials were adopted, and by late 1949 the Chinese were "receptive to Soviet doctrine." In Korea, lacking combat experience of its own, the air force relied heavily on the Soviet air force as its model. Soviet pilots lectured on fighting F-86s, and Chinese pilots memorized the Soviet ace Kozhedub's rule about checking behind you before you attack. Then the twist: in December 1952, Peng Dehuai himself scolded the air force for being unwilling to study Soviet experience, saying China could not build a strong air force quickly without the Soviet Union. (The same Peng, two and a half years before he lectured Zhukov.)

Fravel's evidence can't refute Zhang's, because the two books are counting different things. Fravel concedes the Soviet influence "cannot be dismissed" and notes that Soviet advisors trained Chinese personnel directly, "especially in the navy and the air force." His question is narrower: whether China copied Soviet strategy. He answers that by the middle of the decade Chinese strategists had moved from euphoria to asking "how" to learn, because China's conditions and war history differed and it lacked the industry to field a Soviet-style mechanized army. China also translated American field manuals alongside Soviet ones, which looks more like comparison shopping. Zhang's evidence has the mirror-image limit: it shows imitation in the classroom and the cockpit, and nothing about who wrote the 1956 plan. His sources are party histories, official chronologies, selected works, biographies, and memoirs; the PLA's archives are closed, the documents he uses were selected for publication, and he warns that this may bias some of his conclusions. Party historians have every reason to celebrate independent Chinese thinking (that inference is ours). Zhang's sources are memoirs and his own interviews with former pilots, which see the cockpit and not the CMC.

Opinion: Fravel wins on national strategy, and Zhang wins on the air force, which is the part of the PLA airmen will actually meet. First order, the air force took Soviet training, tactics, and aircraft while the national strategy stayed Chinese. Second order, the air arm grew up defensive: Fravel describes an almost exclusive reliance on interceptors for territorial defense until the 2000s, when ground attack and support aircraft reached almost half the force. Third order, when the PLA studied Kosovo, it chose countermeasures over imitation. The seminar comment: "Fravel is right that China never copied Moscow's strategy, but Zhang shows the air force copied Moscow's tactics and training, so emulation worked at the level airmen fly and stopped at the level the CMC writes." Emulation, it turns out, has an altitude limit. (S)

Questions to Ask in Seminar · Fravel

Four Questions That Start a Fight

  1. Does the 2014 reform break Fravel's model? He writes that "Xi Jinping's intervention was likely essential for the military reforms to be launched" (Fravel, conclusion, ebook page 430). One answer: yes. No significant shift in warfare occurred between 2004 and 2014, and the new strategy and the reorganization were announced together in November 2013, which Fravel says strongly suggests the guideline existed mainly to frame the reforms (ebook page 371); that is a leader driving change. The other answer: no. Fravel argues the idea was almost certainly not Xi's alone, since joint operations had been a PLA goal for two decades (ebook page 430), and Xi's complaint that "the command system for joint operations is unsound" repeats an old PLA grievance (ebook page 371). The first answer is stronger, because Fravel's defense rests on "likely" and "almost certainly," and his own chapter calls the evidence preliminary.
  2. Is the most attentive audience for an American air campaign the enemy, or the next enemy? After Kosovo, the PLA "studied the Serbian response with equal care and attention" (Fravel, chapter 7, ebook page 359). One answer: the next enemy. The PLA concluded that air strikes were the most likely way China would be attacked and built countermeasures, the "three defenses" of early warning and counter-reconnaissance, mobility, and surface-to-air missiles (ebook pages 355 and 358–359), so every American campaign trains its next opponent. The other answer: China learned to attack, too. By 2004, ground attack and support aircraft were almost half the air force (ebook page 351), and the 2003 Iraq studies counted precision munitions rising from eight to sixty-eight percent of those used. The first answer is stronger for Kosovo, where Fravel finds "little evidence for emulation" (ebook page 358).
  3. Is there really a "Leninist advantage" in changing strategy? Fravel suggests "there may be a 'Leninist advantage'" (Fravel, conclusion, ebook page 431). One answer: yes. Officers started eight of nine strategies with no civilian push (ebook page 429), and the PLA has cut its strength ten times since 1949, three cuts totaling a million soldiers since 1997 (ebook page 432). The other answer: the advantage switches off exactly when it's needed. The Gulf War lessons sat while up to fifty percent of training time went to political study (ebook page 313), and Fravel's own line is "When unity is absent, professionalism atrophies" (ebook page 433). The second answer is stronger for forecasting, because the advantage depends on the one variable outsiders see least.
  4. Does Fravel underrate the threat? His evidence is the calendar: after the 1969 border clash, "for more than a decade, the PLA did not adjust its strategy" (Fravel, introduction, ebook page 28). One answer: he rates it correctly. Threats were "a necessary but not sufficient condition," and foreign wars showed how to prepare for them (ebook page 429). The other answer: he underrates it. The 1980 strategy was built entirely around a Soviet invasion (ebook page 28), and Fravel concedes that a worse American threat could produce a guideline "focused on a single adversary" (ebook page 434), so the threat chooses the problem even when a war abroad chooses the answer. The second answer is stronger in seminar, since it uses Fravel's own forecast against his thesis. (S)
The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Fravel · extrapolation

Iran Will Grade the Drone Orbit, Too

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran, and his working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. What follows is our extrapolation from Fravel, and not Shaun's answer.

Fravel supports the answer through the eyes of the adversary. When the PLA studied Kosovo, it credited NATO's victory to information dominance and listed persistent and continuous reconnaissance, satellites, reconnaissance aircraft, and early warning aircraft among the capabilities that mattered. The side being bombed rated persistent sensing as decisive. Fravel's conclusion adds a second use: a state's changing intentions show up in its operational doctrine, force structure, and training. Those are things a sensor network can watch for months, and a bombing campaign tells you little about them. (A drone orbit is a very patient reader of exercise schedules.)

Fravel cuts against the answer in one hard place: the target studies you back. The PLA's response to Kosovo was countermeasures, including counter-reconnaissance, jamming, and mobility, and it studied the Serbian defense as carefully as the American attack. Fravel notes that Kosovo suggested camouflage could blunt precision-guided munitions. A sustainable presence over the strait is the most studyable thing in the theater, and Iran will have years to study it. His model adds a political warning: a regime changes its strategy when its leadership is united enough to agree, and that is the one thing a sensor can't see.

Opinion, extrapolated: Fravel would back the sensors as a way to read Iranian intentions, and he would tell the paper to budget for the counter-reconnaissance they provoke. Plan for the second year, when the drones are watching an adversary that has been watching the drones. (S)

The Readings So Far · Fravel, Kitfield, Zhang, and Olsen

Two Armies Toured the Same Battlefield

The U.S. Army and the PLA both went to school on the 1973 Arab-Israeli War, and the race between them is the best illustration of Fravel's thesis on the shelf. In Kitfield, Don Starry stands on the Golan Heights, sees the Fulda Gap in the Syrian tank wrecks, and goes home to Fort Knox; he and DePuy decide the Army must win the first battle of the next war. In Fravel, Ma Suzheng's seven-person delegation spends two weeks in Egypt and Syria and reports that the two sides' tactics "basically reflected the operational thought of the Soviet Union and United States." Same war, same lesson, two students. The Americans published their answer in 1976, in a Field Manual 100-5 doctrine called, of all things, "active defense," which critics ridiculed as too defensive. The PLA's answer, with the same two words on it, waited until 1980, after Deng had beaten Hua Guofeng. The gap was four years and one Politburo. (Nobody in either army, we assume, checked the other's trademark.)

The other books slot in along the same line. Zhang's Korea fed the 1956 strategy, and his Zhou Enlai would lose half a million to a million soldiers before he would "sacrifice our newly built air force." Olsen graded Desert Storm against an unstated aim and found it short; Fravel shows the same campaign scoring heavily with an audience in Beijing that nobody in Riyadh was aiming at.

The news has been pinning itself here all week. Yesterday's item on the Air Force's Force Design 2026, classified with no unclassified version, meets Fravel's first chapter: China never announces its guidelines either, and the 1956 strategy never appeared in the Liberation Army Daily or the People's Daily. Opinion: both superpowers now keep their strategies in a drawer, and both pay scholars to guess what is in the other one's. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 3, Day 5 · citations and Chicago style

Look Away Before You Paraphrase

A citation doesn't make a too-close paraphrase honest. Wayne Booth and his coauthors, in The Craft of Research, say readers will suspect plagiarism if they can match your words and phrasing to the source, even when you cite it. Their test is simple: read the passage, look away, think, write it in your own words while still looking away, then run your finger along your sentence and the source. If you find synonyms for the same ideas in the same order, try again. They add that a striking phrase needs quotation marks even when it's only a few words long, and that it's better to seem naive than dishonest.

Fravel supplies the test case. His sentence on the PLA is "When unity is present, professionalism can grow. When unity is absent, professionalism atrophies." Before: "When unity exists, professionalism grows, and when unity is missing, professionalism withers (Fravel)." Every idea sits in Fravel's order with a thesaurus applied, so it fails the finger test. After: "Fravel ties the PLA's professionalism to the party's cohesion: officers get room to act like professionals only while the Politburo isn't fighting itself." Or quote the striking word and own the rest: the PLA's professionalism, Fravel argues, "atrophies" whenever the party splits. In a Chicago author-title paper, the footnote reads: M. Taylor Fravel, Active Defense: China's Military Strategy since 1949 (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2019), 433.

Booth's team also notes that experienced readers skim your list of sources before they read the paper, to see whose work you read and whose you didn't. Opinion: on a SAASS paper, the bibliography is the first page the grader reads, whatever the page numbers say.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: before you submit, search the draft for every quotation mark and check that each closing mark has a note number within a sentence of it. Then search for your three favorite phrases and make sure they're actually yours. (S)

Leadership × Fravel · via War on the Rocks, November 12

Purge First, Strategy Later?

Xi Jinping has purged at least 100 senior military officials since 2022, according to a February CSIS report cited by War on the Rocks on Thursday, and the pace has picked up over the past two years. The piece asked five experts what the purges mean for readiness and a Taiwan contingency; the rest is behind a members' wall, so we read only the first commentary, by Yun Sun of the Stimson Center. She names Generals Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli and Zhong Shaojun, the former head of the Central Military Commission's General Office. She argues that loyalty to the leader now outranks professional competence, that trust and morale have dropped, and that rebuilding command will take longer than rebuilding the officer corps. Her summary: "The purges might have strengthened the civilian leader's control of the military, but the military was nevertheless crippled during the process."

Fravel's closing pages saw this risk coming, if not its size. He warned that the anticorruption drive could open "cleavages within the party elite," and that disunity would likely block a new strategy even when the security environment demands one. He also notes that new guidelines are drafted by the CMC's general office or the General Staff. The office that writes the strategy has lost its chief, which is a hard way to run a staff.

Fravel's own history cuts the other way, though. In early 1980, Deng replaced the commanders in all but two of eleven military regions, and the new strategy followed that September. In 1992 an almost entirely new CMC was installed and about three hundred officers close to Yang Baibing were replaced, transferred, or demoted; the 1993 strategy was finished by December. Both times, the purge came right before the strategy, because it ended a fight instead of starting one.

Opinion: the leadership lesson is that a purge is a bet on unity, and Fravel's test is whether the party agrees on its basic policies and on how authority is structured. Yun Sun's "crippled" and Fravel's 1992 precedent read the same headlines and point opposite directions. A planner who picks the comfortable reading should know there is another one. (S)

Space × Sci-Fi

Space × Fravel × Ender's Game · via Breaking Defense and Defense News, November 12

The Ansible Never Needed a Translator Satellite

The Space Development Agency is paying SpaceX $9.9 million, with options up to nearly $12.7 million, to study whether SpaceX's laser terminals can talk to SDA's missile tracking satellites, Breaking Defense reports. The problem is that the Tracking Layer, the medium-orbit missile warning satellites that Space Systems Command is building, and the Space Data Network backbone use optical terminals with different waveform standards, so they can't connect. The fixes on the table are translator satellites, buying SpaceX terminals for future Tracking Layer satellites, or writing new standards. SDA calls the study "a first step." The same day, Reuters (via Defense News) carried President Zelenskyy's complaint that new restrictions on Starshield, the military version of Starlink, kept Ukrainian interceptors from reaching the speed needed to down Russian jet-powered drones. SpaceX declined to comment.

Fravel's PLA saw this coming from the other side. In December 2002, Jiang Zemin told the CMC that "confrontations among systems" would become the basic feature of battle, and that space had become "the new strategic high ground." In December 2013, Xi Jinping told the same body that "the command system for joint operations is unsound." Joint operations became the PLA's goal in 1993, and the command system was still being rebuilt for them in 2015. Both militaries' hardest problem is getting their own pieces to talk to each other.

Science fiction skipped that step. In Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game, the ansible, a device for instant communication that Card borrowed from Ursula K. Le Guin, lets commanders direct a fleet light-years away, and Ender fights what he believes are simulations that turn out to be real battles. The ansible works with every ship, every time. No one in the novel commissions a risk reduction study.

Opinion: the Starshield story is the Fravel lesson in miniature. A link is a dependency, and whoever controls it has a vote in your war. Jiang called modern war a confrontation among systems; the first confrontation is usually with your own. (S)

Fravel: seminar recall

  • What two conditions does Fravel say produce a major change in China's military strategy?

    A shift in the conduct of warfare, revealed in a war elsewhere, and a united party that lets senior officers act. F 24–25

  • Which of the nine strategies was started by the top party leader, and why?

    1964, Mao's return to luring the enemy in deep, part of his response to revisionism in the party. F 180–181, 429

  • What did Chinese analysts predict about the Gulf War in late 1990?

    A protracted war in which the coalition bogged down and air power played only a marginal role. F 301

  • What did the PLA conclude from Kosovo?

    Air strikes were the most likely way China would be attacked, more destructive than imagined, and China needed counter-air raid research. F 355, 358

  • Why has China's nuclear strategy barely changed?

    Party leaders never delegated it to senior officers; there, civilians resisted change. F 76, 431

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

Writing drills

  • Fix the too-close paraphrase: "The PLA studied the Serbian response with the same care and attention it gave US operations (Fravel)."

    Either quote it ("studied the Serbian response with equal care and attention") or rethink it: "The PLA cared as much about how Serbia survived NATO as about how NATO attacked" (Fravel, ebook 359). Add the page; "(Fravel)" alone sends the reader into the whole book (Booth et al., 207–209).

  • Which words need quotation marks? Fravel calls it a possible Leninist advantage in changing strategy.

    "Leninist advantage" is his coinage, so it gets marks and a note (ebook 431); "changing strategy" is common wording and doesn't (Booth et al., 207–208).

  • Review (Thursday, thesis): give this an opponent: "China learned from the Gulf War."

    "Some read China's 1993 strategy as copying America's Gulf War; Fravel shows the PLA's problem was how to fight a technologically superior adversary with the equipment it already had" (ebook 302, 304). Position and opponent arrive together (Graff and Birkenstein, 21).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
