Seminar 8 · Nguyen, today at 1200

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered: Four Questions, One First Secretary

  1. What effect, if any, did bombing have on the "moral" of the North's political leadership? It changed Hanoi's timetable and its price, and Nguyen finds little sign it changed the goal. Rolling Thunder fed a spy fever that Le Duan's security men used to arrest critics before Tet (chapter 3). Linebacker forced the Politburo to switch from attacking the South to defending the North, and the Christmas bombing raised the cost of delay for leaders who already wanted a deal; Nguyen says it rallied world condemnation instead of breaking Hanoi's will (chapter 8).
  2. How did airpower's use in South Vietnam shape the strategic environment the communists faced? It kept defeating the big offensives and pushed Hanoi toward the negotiating table. After the 1968 and 1969 offensives drained it, Hanoi adopted an "economy of forces" and raised diplomacy to equal rank with fighting (chapter 5). In 1972, American bombing and Saigon's counteroffensives, backed by American air power, rolled back the early gains, and Hanoi's leaders privately admitted their big-unit offensive had failed (chapters 7 and 8).
  3. What role did information operations play during the war? In Nguyen's account, the leading one. Her conclusion calls Hanoi's world relations campaign, aimed at antiwar movements abroad, the key to its success. In July 1971 Le Duan told his negotiators that the diplomatic struggle's first objective was inciting the antiwar movement in Congress and on the streets (chapter 6). At home, information was a police matter: cadres studying Resolution 9 were told its most important part could not be written down (chapter 2).
  4. How accurate were the assumptions of North Vietnam's leadership? Sharp about its allies, poor about the South and about Nixon. Le Duan bet three times, in 1964, 1968, and 1972, on a general uprising that never came (conclusion). Le Duc Tho bet that Nixon, busy with summits, could not hit back in 1972, and got Linebacker (chapter 7). Yet Le Duan read his patrons well, telling Zhou Enlai that Beijing had rescued a "drowning" Nixon.

Pages are in Sources. The table will bring the counterarguments; that's what the table is for. (S)

The Reading · Nguyen, Hanoi's War

Hanoi Had a Boss, and He Kept His Name Off the Door

Nguyen's argument fits in one name. Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 to 1986, ran Hanoi's war, sidelined Ho Chi Minh and Vo Nguyen Giap at nearly every key juncture, and owed his rise to the war he sold. Her conclusion widens it: leaders in Hanoi and Saigon set the nature and pace of American intervention, so a history written only from Washington has the wrong protagonist.

That rearranges the shelf. Clodfelter asked whether bombing could coerce Hanoi; Nguyen asks who in Hanoi was being coerced, and the answer is a man whose credential was the war, which made a cheap settlement politically expensive for him. Zhang showed Beijing as the junior partner scrambling for Soviet help in Korea. Nguyen shows Hanoi working two patrons at once and playing them against each other. Opinion: the most dangerous adversary is the one whose leader can't afford to lose the argument at home. (S)

Weekend Reading · Haun, for seminar Monday, November 9

Weekend Reading: Haun Says Air Power Wins When the Enemy Is Too Scared to Move

Phil Haun, a SAASS graduate who flew A-10s over Kosovo, wrote Tactical Air Power and the Vietnam War to supply a theory he couldn't find while studying at SAASS: one for attacking armies directly. His scorecard covers twenty-three modern American air campaigns since 1965. Direct attack on fielded forces was tried in eleven and met its military aims in nine; strategic bombing succeeded twice in six, and interdiction failed five times out of six.

His thesis is that air power alone kept failing in Vietnam, while air forces in combined arms operations usually succeeded. Its greatest effect, he argues, comes from armed reconnaissance that deters enemy armies from concentrating and maneuvering. He argues against the strategic bombers and the interdiction enthusiasts, and, to his A-10 squadron mates' dismay, he doesn't advocate for close air support either. For this course, he answers the question the last three books circled, which is what American air power was actually good at in Vietnam.

Read for:

  1. Chapter 1, pages 1 to 12: the twenty-three campaigns and the scorecard on page 7.
  2. Chapter 2, pages 13 to 34: the theory, and the dilemma it poses for an army under a lethal air threat, which is to mass and get hammered from the air or disperse and give up the initiative.
  3. Chapter 7, pages 163 to 201: the Easter Offensive; set it beside Randolph and ask why Quang Tri fell while An Loc held.
  4. Chapter 8, pages 202 to 228: the factor tables and why the Air Force keeps dropping armed reconnaissance from doctrine.
  5. Chapter 9, pages 229 to 250: the theory applied to Kyiv and to Taiwan.

Short on time? Chapters 1, 2, and 8. Haun scores campaigns on whether the enemy dared to move, so a reader who skims chapter 3 is technically practicing dispersal. (S)

The 628 Paper · through today's author

If Tehran Tests: Read the Regime's Org Chart Before the Target List

The prompt asks for the single most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1 at 1630. Today we run the syllabus's first example, an IRGC nuclear test, through Nguyen. This is our extrapolation from a book about Hanoi, not Shaun's answer.

Nguyen's Hanoi offers three warnings. Le Duan's rise and the war were one project, so settling cheaply would have cost him his position (chapter 1). His security services turned every American escalation into a reason to arrest the moderates (chapters 2 and 3). And he aimed his diplomacy at the American Congress and the American street, which he judged the softest targets in the war (chapter 6).

Opinion, extrapolated: Airmen preparing for a test should first ask who inside the regime gains from it, because that person decides what the air campaign is up against. They should expect strikes to strengthen the hardliners at home, as Rolling Thunder fed Hanoi's spy fever, and plan for an information fight aimed at Congress and allies as seriously as the one over the target area. Le Duan never needed to win an air battle. He needed Washington arguing with itself. (S)

Eight Books, Two Archives, One Argument About Who Was in Charge

Yesterday's author and today's disagree about Hanoi's org chart. Randolph concluded that no single leader in totalitarian North Vietnam held anything like Nixon's authority over strategy. Nguyen says Le Duan monopolized decision making and sidelined Ho and Giap at nearly every turn. Randolph leaned on the Nixon tapes and Vietnamese official histories; Nguyen got into Hanoi's Foreign Ministry archives. Opinion: when two good historians disagree, check their reading rooms before their reasoning.

The patrons look familiar too. In Zhang, Moscow told its pilots in 1950 to retrain the Chinese first and not to fly beyond the Yalu. In Nguyen, Moscow and Beijing answered Linebacker with defensive arms and no offensive weapons for the South, and both kept their summits with Nixon. Junior allies in both wars got air defense and lectures. And Clodfelter's Linebacker, which cut overland imports from 160,000 tons a month to 30,000, shows up in Nguyen as the campaign that forced the Politburo to defend the North.

The news web keeps growing. Thursday's House majority eyeing the Iran war budget met Randolph's 35-to-8 Senate caucus vote; today Senate Democrats demanding Iran war commitments meet Le Duan's 1971 telegram naming Congress as a target. Only one of them admits it in the archives. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 2, Day 5 · citations and Chicago style

Hit-and-Run Quotations Leave Dents in Your Grade

Friday is citation day, and the guide is Graff and Birkenstein's They Say / I Say, which treats a bad quotation the way insurance adjusters treat a fender bender. Quotations don't speak for themselves, they write; "quotations are orphans" that need a new home in your text. A quote dropped in with no frame is a "dangling" or "hit-and-run" quotation. The fix is the quotation sandwich: a top slice that says who is speaking and sets up the words, and a bottom slice that says what you take them to mean and why they matter. Choose a verb that matches the speaker's spirit ("complains," "concedes") over a neutral "states," and when in doubt, explain more.

Before: "Linebacker II worked. 'Nixon's bombs had done their damage.' The agreement followed." After: "Nguyen, the first scholar admitted to Hanoi's Foreign Ministry archives, concedes that by December 26 'Nixon's bombs had done their damage.' Her claim is narrower than airmen hope, since on the same page she argues the bombing rallied world condemnation instead of breaking Hanoi's will. The damage bought Nixon a date in January."

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: press Ctrl+F, search your draft for the quotation mark, and make sure a sentence of yours follows every hit. Then give the first Chicago note in full (author, title, Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 2012, page) and shorten later ones to "Nguyen, Hanoi's War." If your copy is an ebook without fixed pages, cite the chapter and say which edition you used. A quote without a sandwich is just someone else's sentence loitering in your paper. (S)

Schumer Takes a Victory Lap, and the Iran War Gets a Vote in the Leadership Races

Chuck Schumer is enjoying himself. Punchbowl News reports that Senate Democrats flipped a stunning number of seats and could hold as many as 56 in the next Congress, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled and Democrats ahead in both. Electoral-vote.com's count from Thursday had it at 54 to 44 with two outstanding, and Mississippi within about 1,100 votes at 98 percent reporting. Schumer credited relentless recruiting and called winning "only step one." Not everyone is celebrating him: Senator Chris Van Hollen wants commitments on how leadership will handle the Iran war before he backs anyone for leader. Across the Capitol, every House Republican leadership post is contested, with Steve Scalise and Jim Jordan locked in a tight race.

So the war has become a question in leadership elections, which is a sentence that would have interested Le Duan. In July 1971 he cabled his negotiators that the first objective of Hanoi's diplomacy was inciting the antiwar movement in Congress and on the streets. A year earlier, after Cambodia, Hanoi read Kissinger's offer of a twelve-month withdrawal schedule as weakness forced by antiwar sentiment, and stiffened in Paris. Nguyen's point is that adversaries read American legislatures the way we read their politburos, closely and with a theory.

Opinion, nonpartisan and old: a war that becomes a test in caucus votes acquires a second audience abroad, whatever anyone intends. Nobody in Washington controls how the other side reads the whip count. (Leadership elections after a drubbing are their own genre, too. Hanoi's old guard held one and picked the man it thought had no base.) The Senate now has its majority; the scorekeepers abroad have their newsletters. (S)

Leadership × Nguyen · via Defense News (Reuters), November 5

Personnel Is Policy, in Berlin Today and in Hanoi in 1967

Germany has picked its next top officer, and the résumé is the message. The Defense Ministry named Lieutenant General Christian Freuding, army chief since October 2025, as chief of defense starting in February 2027; Carsten Breuer leaves to chair NATO's Military Committee. Freuding spent years overseeing Berlin's arms supplies to Kyiv, pushed the army to adopt technology tested in Ukraine, and started a drone regiment.

Nguyen would read the appointment as a strategy document. In 1967, with General Nguyen Chi Thanh dead and Giap opposed, Le Duan needed what she calls "a more pliant general" for his risky plan, and he found Van Tien Dung; one historian she cites describes Tet's origins as a Faustian bargain between them. Giap was sidelined, Dung's plan went forward, and in 1975 Dung's troops rode Soviet tanks into Saigon while Giap later lost his Politburo seat. The choice of general told everyone which war the first secretary meant to fight.

Opinion: the leadership lesson cuts both ways. Picking the officer who learned the most from someone else's war is the healthy version of the signal; picking the one who agrees with you is the risky version, and both send a message to the ranks about what gets rewarded. Hanoi promoted the general who said yes. Berlin promoted the one who'd been paying attention to Ukraine, and we know which method we'd rather brief. (S)

Ukraine Rents Its Sky, and the Landlord Hasn't Explained October

Ukraine's Starshield contract with SpaceX expires in mid-2027, and talks on a replacement are only beginning, Kyiv's chargé d'affaires, Denys Sienik, told reporters this week. "We still don't have [an] answer from [the] Pentagon and SpaceX," Sienik said. Today's seminar: Nguyen, Hanoi's War, chapter by chapter

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, introduction

The Man Who Ran Hanoi's War Wanted You to Look at Uncle Ho

Before you analyze an enemy's strategy, find out who writes it, and treat the famous face on the poster as a decoy. Nguyen names the author: Le Duan, who held the top Party post from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand. Historians, she argues, treat Washington's choices as human and Hanoi's victory as fate, when individuals in Hanoi shaped the outcome as much as structures did.

Her method is her evidence. The Party, military, and Foreign Ministry archives remain closed even to Vietnamese scholars, and in 2003 she became the first researcher admitted to the Foreign Ministry's, and is still the only one (the line for the reading room is short). Her prize is a classified chronology quoting the 1968 to 1973 telegrams between the Politburo and the delegation in Paris, Le Duan talking to Le Duc Tho. She calls it the "holy grail."

The weak seminar comment is "Ho and Giap outlasted the Americans." The strong one is "Ho was the face and Giap the famous general, but Le Duan ran the Politburo, so before we ask whether bombing broke Hanoi's will, we should ask whose will." Opinion: with the key archives still shut, some of her conclusions are inference in a confident suit, and saying so will win the room. Le Duan wanted off the historical stage, and American scholarship politely held the curtain. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 1

Hanoi Hired a Caretaker and Got a War Party

Whoever holds the drafting pen and the personnel office holds the strategy, and a leader who rises by selling a war will find the war has become his credential. Nguyen argues that Le Duan fanned the southern flames to push reluctant comrades in the North into war. The chapter's epigraph is his: "Revolution is offensive."

Watch the plumbing. Land reform went so badly that soldiers fired on their own people in 1956; Truong Chinh stepped down as first secretary, and Le Duc Tho ran the cleanup and became the Party's organizational chief. Le Duan arrived in Hanoi in 1957, and the old guard backed him because he seemed to lack a base (a hiring decision that aged badly). Ho had given Giap Resolution 15 to draft, and in early 1958, most likely under pressure from Le Duan, Ho told Giap to hand it over. Le Duan and Pham Hung ran it through twenty-two more drafts, a plenum approved it in January 1959.

The weak seminar comment is "The southern insurgency forced Hanoi's hand." The strong one is "Le Duan took the pen from Giap and used the threat of losing control of the South as his argument." Opinion: ask of any war plan who inside the government profits if the war happens. The Politburo wanted a caretaker without a base. It got a man whose base was the war. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 2

Le Duan Won the Debate by Arresting the Other Side

Internal security is an instrument of war policy, so when a regime escalates, check whom it arrests before you count what it builds. From 1960 to 1963, Nguyen argues, Le Duan and Le Duc Tho fused the Party Secretariat and the Ministry of Public Security into one machine and aimed it at the moderates who wanted to build socialism in the North before fighting for the South.

The Ninth Plenum of December 1963 is the proof. Truong Chinh asked the Soviet-trained theoretician Hoang Minh Chinh to draft the Party line, got a plea for neutrality between Moscow and Beijing, and buried it (he had read the room, and the room had a police force). The hardliners used threats and blackmail and offered Ho Chi Minh the option of "following the Politburo line or standing aside." In January 1964, four hundred cadres gathered to study Resolution 9 heard that its most important aspect could not be written down. Bui Cong Trung soon lost his seat and his post, and Duong Bach Mai died under "mysterious circumstances."

The weak seminar comment is "Hanoi was a disciplined monolith." The strong one is "Hanoi looked unified because Le Duan made dissent a security matter, and Resolution 9 passed over letters from about fifty mid-ranking cadres." Opinion: manufactured unity works until the plan fails, and then nobody is left to say so. It's hard to dissent from a paragraph that doesn't exist. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 3

Le Duan Tested His Tet Assumptions by Jailing the Doubters

Before approving a plan, write down what it assumes, then ask who is still allowed to call an assumption wrong. Official Vietnamese histories treat Tet as a shrewd answer to American failure. Nguyen adds the feud and the police: Le Duan revived the General Offensive and General Uprising that had flopped in 1964 and cleared its path with the largest purge in Party history.

The assumptions were bold. In mid-1967 Le Duan said, "It is impossible for the United States to maintain its current troop level." In July 1967 Ho questioned whether the plan was realistic. Giap then left for Eastern Europe, Ho for Beijing, and the security service arrested critics in three waves, catching the General Staff officers Nguyen Van Vinh and Le Trong Nghia in October just after they briefed the Politburo. The stated reason was protecting surprise. The result: southern commanders got little more than two weeks' notice, and calendar discrepancies sent some provinces in a day early while others got their orders too late.

Use it as a check. For each assumption, name the person who doubted it and say where that person is now; if the answer is Hoa Lo prison, the assumption is untested. Opinion: American bombs fed the spy fever that justified the arrests, a favor Le Duan never thanked Johnson for. He guarded the secret so well that some of his own units missed it. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 4

Hanoi Got Serious in Paris When It Ran Out of Offensives

When a negotiation runs alongside a war, lay the diplomatic calendar over the battlefield calendar, because a side that is still attacking is only probing at the table. Nguyen argues that Le Duan accepted talks after Johnson's March 31, 1968 speech because he had no choice, then kept betting on the uprising. Le Duc Tho stated the house view: "Diplomacy could never replace military strength."

Hanoi accepted preliminary talks on April 3. On May 4 it hit 119 bases, towns, and cities in a second wave, and the first Paris session met on May 13. Moscow, which had helped find the venue, was dismayed (allies love surprises). In September Tho lectured Averell Harriman for nearly an hour on American intervention, while his private cable of September 28, two days before the third wave ended, sketched a real negotiating plan. Only with its forces spent, Nguyen concludes, did the Politburo take diplomacy seriously.

The weak seminar comment is "Tet shocked both sides into peace talks." The strong one is "Hanoi said yes in April, attacked 119 targets in May, and drafted its first serious plan two days before the offensive ended." Opinion: run the check on your own side too. On October 10 the Politburo hardened its demands again, and Tho flew home to talk his bosses down. Harriman got the history lecture; the Politburo got the reality briefing. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 5

The Cambodian Incursion Moved Hanoi's Map and Washington's Clock

Score an operation on the periphery by the options it leaves the enemy afterward, since the ground it takes is the smaller half of the ledger. By 1970, Nguyen argues, a Hanoi drained by its 1968 and 1969 offensives adopted an "economy of forces," shelved the urban uprising, raised diplomacy to equal rank with fighting, and stepped up support for Cambodia and Laos. As the war shrank in South Vietnam, it spread next door.

After Sihanouk was voted out on March 18, the exiled prince formally approved communist use of Cambodian territory. On April 30 Nixon sent American and South Vietnamese troops after the communist headquarters, COSVN. After two days of heavy combat the communists withdrew deeper, toward a headquarters already relocated, and the military balance barely moved. The balance at home did, with Kent State, the Cooper-Church amendment, and the Senate's repeal of the Tonkin Gulf Resolution. On September 7 Kissinger offered a twelve-month withdrawal schedule with no matching demand, and Hanoi read it as weakness forced by antiwar sentiment.

Use it as a check on any raid on a sanctuary: ask where the enemy goes when struck, who invited him there, and what your own capital will do the week after. Opinion: the incursion answered all three in Hanoi's favor. The operation found something. It just wasn't COSVN. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 6

Hanoi Wrote Its Public Peace Plans for Congress

Ask who a peace offer was written for. Nguyen's sixth chapter shows Hanoi running two negotiations in 1971, a secret one with Henry Kissinger and a public one aimed at the American home front, and she concludes that peace never had a chance that year.

A five-day stretch makes the case. On June 26, Le Duc Tho and Xuan Thuy handed Kissinger a secret nine-point plan across a green tablecloth spread to look flexible (diplomacy by upholstery). On July 1, at the 119th public session, Madame Nguyen Thi Binh unveiled seven points that pressed harder on Thieu. Le Duan's telegram to his negotiators named the first target plainly: the antiwar movement in Congress and on the streets.

The weak seminar comment is "Hanoi was intransigent in Paris." The strong one is "Hanoi was flexible in public and rigid in private on purpose, because the public offer was aimed at American voters, and Le Duan said so in writing in July 1971." Opinion: Hanoi learned the trick from Nixon. That January, Thuy cabled home that Nixon's Five-Point Proposal was hollow, built for world opinion, and working anyway. When Kissinger told Hanoi to choose between propaganda and negotiation, he was grading a copy of his own homework. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 7

Brezhnev Shouted for the Record and Toasted for Real

Judge an ally by what it ships and what it withholds; speeches are free. Nguyen argues that Le Duan and Le Duc Tho launched the 1972 offensive partly to outrun their own patrons, since a smashing victory would inoculate Hanoi against Chinese and Soviet pressure to settle. Beijing and Moscow protested Nixon's escalation for the record and leaned on Hanoi to settle more or less on Washington's terms.

Hanoi timed the attack between Nixon's Beijing summit in February and his Moscow summit in late May. Tanks crossed the DMZ on March 30, and Nixon answered on May 8 with Linebacker and the mining. At his dacha on May 24, Brezhnev told Nixon, "I don't want to hurl more epithets on you," hurled a few more, and toasted him. Beijing kept Soviet ships out of Chinese harbors until Tho pleaded on June 18, and when Soviet cargo arrived under torn blankets, Beijing decided Moscow wanted American satellites to see it.

The weak seminar comment is "China and the Soviet Union stood by Hanoi." The strong one is "They sent defensive arms against Linebacker, no offensive weapons for the South, and both summits went ahead." Opinion: Hanoi read its allies better than its enemy, since Tho had bet that Nixon was too busy with summits to hit back. Moscow drank to détente while Hanoi's leaders sat in bomb shelters. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 8

The Christmas Bombing Hit Hanoi's Calendar Harder Than Its Will

Before crediting bombs with breaking a leadership, learn what it already wanted, then ask whether the bombs changed its goal or only its price. By summer 1972 Le Duan had conceded the offensive's failure. Linebacker pushed the Politburo from attacking the South to defending the North, and Saigon retook Quang Tri.

With talks deadlocked and defenses going up, the Politburo told its negotiators, "Signing now or later, timing is no longer an issue." Nixon ordered Linebacker II for December 18, the day Le Duc Tho got home. Over twelve days, 3,420 sorties hit the Hanoi and Hai Phong area, killing more than 2,000 civilians and costing 27 American aircraft, 15 of them B-52s. On December 26, the heaviest day, Hanoi offered to resume talks. Nguyen writes that "Nixon's bombs had done their damage," and on the same page that they mobilized world condemnation instead of breaking Hanoi's will (pick one per argument).

The weak seminar comment is "Linebacker II bombed Hanoi back to the table." The strong one is "Linebacker II raised the price of delay for a Politburo that already wanted out, and Nguyen scores it as the failure of Hanoi's hope to settle without more damage." Opinion: the morale case is thinner than airmen hope. The Party feared unrest enough to put its security minister on the Politburo, and the worried men held the handcuffs. The final round opened January 8, a date booked before the bombing began, which is an expensive way to keep an appointment. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, epilogue and conclusion

Le Duan Waited Fifteen Years for an Uprising and Got Tanks Instead

In a war fought through allies, the small powers often set the pace, so study their capitals as hard as your own. Nguyen's conclusion says leaders in Hanoi and Saigon dictated the nature and pace of American intervention. Le Duan monopolized decisions, sidelined Ho and Giap at nearly every key juncture, and bet three times, in 1964, 1968, and 1972, on a general uprising that never came. Thieu, the supposed puppet, dragged out the American exit and exposed the limits of the Nixon Doctrine.

The epilogue supplies the punch line. On April 30, 1975, troops under General Van Tien Dung rode Soviet tanks into Saigon; the Party never needed the masses to rise, and South Vietnam's leaders had more or less fled a week earlier. Le Duan died in 1986, and 100,000 Hanoians lined the route of his funeral. China sent nobody, and Ronald Reagan did not register the passing. Le Duan won the war and lost the obituary.

The weak seminar comment is "We lost Vietnam because of bad decisions in Washington." The strong one is "Washington made plenty, but Hanoi and Saigon set the terms: Le Duan pushed escalation over Ho's objections, Hanoi's antiwar diplomacy blunted superpower diplomacy, and Thieu slowed the withdrawal." Opinion: quote her caveats too. She concedes nothing is definitive until the archives open, and she warns that crediting small powers too much lets the superpowers off the hook. Her last line is bleak: leaders ensured that "peace could only be won through the barrel of a gun." The diplomats won the argument, and the tanks delivered the verdict. (S)

Nguyen: chapter recall

  • Who does Nguyen say actually ran Hanoi's war, and for how long did he hold the top Party post?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right-hand man. N intro, e19

  • How did Le Duan take control of Resolution 15?

    Ho had assigned Giap to draft it, but in early 1958, most likely under pressure from Le Duan, Ho had Giap hand it over; Le Duan and Pham Hung then ran it through twenty-two more drafts. N ch. 1, e67, e75

  • What choice did the hardliners give Ho Chi Minh at the Ninth Plenum in December 1963?

    Follow the Politburo line or stand aside. They shamed him with his "two mistakes" of 1945 and 1954. N ch. 2, e104

  • What did Le Duan assume about American staying power in mid-1967?

    That the United States could not maintain its troop level, expand the war, or drag it out, so a sharp escalation would find its breaking point. N ch. 3, e144

  • What did Hanoi do the day after it proposed Paris as a venue in May 1968?

    On 4 May it launched the second wave of the offensive against 119 southern bases, towns, and cities, to Moscow's dismay; the public talks were for probing only. N ch. 4, e166, e170

  • What did Resolution 18 of the Eighteenth Plenum change in Hanoi's strategy for 1970?

    It adopted an "economy of forces" strategy, raised the diplomatic struggle to equal rank with the military and political struggles, stepped up support for Cambodia and Laos, and put Le Duan's urban uprising on hold. N ch. 5, e222-223

  • What happened in Paris between 26 June and 1 July 1971?

    Tho and Thuy gave Kissinger a secret nine-point plan across a new green tablecloth; five days later Madame Binh publicly unveiled the PRG's seven points, tougher on Thieu. N ch. 6, e293, e295

  • How did Hanoi time the 1972 offensive against Nixon's summits?

    It launched on 30 March, between the Beijing summit (21-28 February) and the Moscow summit (22-30 May), hoping to neutralize big-power pressure. N ch. 7, e342

  • What did Hanoi's Politburo say about timing just before Linebacker II?

    It told its Paris negotiators that signing now or later was no longer an issue, while it prepared defenses and evacuation of the cities. N ch. 8, e406, e408

  • Who, in Nguyen's conclusion, dictated the nature and pace of U.S. intervention?

    Leaders in Hanoi and Saigon; Vietnamese elites created the context in which American leaders operated. N concl., e433

  • What does Nguyen call the key to Hanoi's ultimate success?

    Its world relations campaign to win antiwar movements abroad, which with small-power diplomacy blunted Washington's superpower diplomacy, more than general offensives or hearts and minds. N concl., e432

    It had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, at a cost of 243 aircraft (115–116).

  • Zhang: what did Moscow order Belov's 151st Air Division in 1950?

    To retrain Chinese pilots first and not to fly beyond the Yalu, so Chinese troops did not get the air cover they counted on (62).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how much did Linebacker I cut North Vietnam's overland imports?

    From 160,000 to 30,000 tons a month, with 155,548 tons of bombs dropped (ch. V, e184).

  • Randolph: why did the first B-52 raid on North Vietnam, at Vinh on April 10, 1972, miss?

    The American charts of North Vietnam were off by about a kilometer (121).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

Writing drills

  • Fix the hit-and-run: "Le Duan was confident. 'It is impossible for the United States to maintain its current troop level.'"

    "In mid-1967, as he pushed the Tet plan past Giap's doubts, Le Duan insisted that 'it is impossible for the United States to maintain its current troop level' (Nguyen, ch. 3, e144). He was betting that one sharp escalation would find America's breaking point, and he jailed the planners who doubted it" (Graff and Birkenstein, 46).

  • Pick a better verb: "Nguyen states that peace could only be won through the barrel of a gun."

    "Nguyen concludes, bleakly, that leaders ensured 'peace could only be won through the barrel of a gun'" (conclusion, e433). "Concludes, bleakly" carries her tone; "states" flattens it (Graff and Birkenstein, 47).

  • Review (Thursday, although and because): turn "Linebacker II forced Hanoi to sign" into a thesis.

    "Although Hanoi offered to resume talks on December 26, the heaviest day of the Christmas bombing, Linebacker II changed the price of delay more than Hanoi's goals, because the Politburo had already shifted to seeking American withdrawal that summer" (Nguyen, ch. 8, e360–361, e412; Booth et al., 125–126).

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
