Book of the Day · Crane, American Airpower Strategy in Korea

The Air Force Didn't Advertise Its Limits. Korea Did.

Recall first: answer each question from memory, then open it to check. Trying before looking is what makes it stick.

  • 1. Who limited airpower in Korea, and how?

    Washington, step by step. Truman first allowed air action only south of the 38th Parallel. On 29 June 1950 he approved strikes in the North, and the JCS's one restriction was to stay clear of the Soviet and Manchurian borders.1 After FEAF bombed Rashin, about 20 miles from the Soviet border, State Department fears led the JCS to stop further attacks there in September 1950.2 In November the JCS limited the Yalu bridge attacks to the Korean side of the spans.3

  • 2. What was Truman afraid of?

    A wider, possibly global war. After a 30 November 1950 press conference on the atomic bomb, an alarmed Clement Attlee rushed to Washington.4 In April 1951, with Chinese forces building up and Soviet forces massing in the Far East, Truman ordered nuclear weapons and more SAC bombers toward Okinawa. The bombers themselves stayed on Guam.5

  • 3. Did airpower achieve its objectives?

    Partly, and less than promised. Interdiction disappointed. FEAF came to regret the name STRANGLE, and Communist repair crews of up to 500,000 kept the railways running.6 An April 1952 FEAF study found the rail campaign had not worked despite more than 15,000 cuts. That study launched "air pressure."7 The June 1952 hydroelectric strikes destroyed about 90 percent of North Korea's power.8 Even air superiority was shaky: in September 1951, FEAF warned it was "in jeopardy."9

  • 4. Why no atomic bomb?

    Partly practical. The 1951 HUDSON HARBOR test missions showed long delays and weak target evaluation, and the Air Staff concluded Korea offered no suitable atomic targets.10 Later, Weyland told Clark he opposed atomic use. He wasn't sure the Manchurian airfields could be neutralized, and he expected the Soviets to learn from the strikes and use them for propaganda.11

  • 5. What did airpower start fights about at home?

    Weyland said he fought the Navy as much as the Communists, and Smart spent 40 percent of his time answering Army and Navy proposals.12 Close air support was the sorest point. Marines delivered bombs in about 10 minutes, against about 45 for the Air Force.13

The review. Crane says his purpose is to examine "the image and reality of American airpower."14 The gap between the two is the book. Leaders and the public expected too much, partly because the Air Force wasn't candid about its limits. FEAF's Gen. Smart admitted that "we certainly don't advertise our limitations."15 The plan was to win the air quickly and then bomb the enemy's war-making capacity. Instead, the MiG-15 contested the air, the real sources of supply sat across a border airmen couldn't touch, and FEAF fell back on interdiction and then air pressure.16

The sting is in the ending. The war's close was "ambiguous enough to allow the U.S. Air Force" to believe it had succeeded, so the service drew few lessons and went back to planning for general war under the New Look. Crane says that cost it in Vietnam.17 Opinion: that's yesterday's Gaddis argument seen from the cockpit. When Eisenhower's New Look turned asymmetrical and nuclear, the Air Force had every budgetary reason to file Korea under "exception."18

Bring to seminar: Crane notes that whether the nuclear threat helped end the war "remains open to dispute."19 A 1953 FEAF study blamed policy makers for never stating limited-war objectives "compatible with air force capabilities."20 Whose job was it to close that gap: the politicians who set the limits, or the airmen who didn't advertise theirs? Next Up · Thursday 29 Oct

Zhang: The Same Air War, from North of the Yalu

Xiaoming Zhang's Red Wings over the Yalu tells the Korean air war from the Communist side, mostly the Chinese side, using newly opened Chinese and Russian archives and PLAAF oral histories.1 It follows three threads: China's ideas about airpower and its rush to build an air force, the odd shape of Soviet help, and how Chinese air operations actually went.2 Zhang's ambition is plain: "This book thus should significantly modify conventional wisdom about the Korean War."3

The bridge from Crane. Crane shows Washington limiting American airpower for political reasons. Zhang argues that the US and Soviet operations were limited mainly by politics, while the PLAAF was held back by inexperience.4 Same war, different kinds of limits. That's the syllabus's second theme in one comparison.

Read for these:

  • Whose numbers to trust. Zhang warns that his kill ratios are official Chinese and Russian figures that may misrepresent reality.5 He calls American claims "probably highly inflated."6 Crane's figures are on the other side of that ledger.
  • Stalin's "cryptic promise" of air support, and whether he reneged. Stalin's October telegram asking for Chinese troops never mentioned it.7
  • Fighters as defensive weapons. PLAAF planners saw fighters as defensive and bombers as aggressive, and built a fighter force.8
  • The lesson China kept: the war "limited the Chinese air force to an air defense role."9

If you only have three hours: read the introduction (3–11) and Ch. 9, the conclusion (201–214), first. Then read Ch. 3 (55–77) on Stalin, Mao, and air support. Skim Ch. 6 (122–142) on the covert Soviet air role and Ch. 7 (143–171) on PLAAF combat.10 (S)

Writing Lab · Week 1, Day 2

Evaluation: The Step Everyone Skips

The rule. Hollon's PEER paragraph runs Point, Evidence, Evaluation, Review. Evaluation means judging the evidence (is it reliable, biased, complete?) and explaining how it supports your point. Hollon calls the missing Evaluation "the source of A LOT of problems for all writers."1 The usual failure is the evidence dump: three facts in a row, then "Thus."

Before and after

Before: evidence dumpKennan identified five centers of industrial and military power. He favored strongpoint defense over perimeter defense. The Truman administration believed its resources were limited. Thus, Kennan's strategy was adopted.
After: full PEER[P] Kennan's strongpoint strategy won early acceptance less for its logic than for its fit with the Truman administration's budget. [E] Kennan argued that only five centers of industrial and military power mattered and that the United States should defend those strongpoints rather than a perimeter.2 [Ev] Gaddis is careful on this point. He presents Kennan's ideas partly as rationalizations of moves the administration was already making, which weakens any claim that Kennan drove policy alone.3 [E/Ev] The appeal lay in fit, since officials were convinced the country had only limited resources.4 [R] If the strategy rested on a perception of scarcity, it should have unraveled once that perception changed, and the next section tests that prediction against NSC-68.

What changed: the point makes a claim instead of stating a fact. One sentence judges the source. The last sentence tells the reader where the argument goes next. The "after" follows Hollon's other rules too: no contractions, third person, no bare "this."

Pro tip

The scaffold trick. After each piece of evidence, write a sentence that starts "This matters because…" Then delete those three words and sharpen what's left. The scaffold forces you to evaluate, and deleting it keeps you clear of Hollon's bare-"this" rule.

Model of the week

Lamb and Grego's War on the Rocks piece "Golden Dome Is Technologically and Financially Infeasible" puts the thesis in its title. It gives one subheading per reason and evaluates the other side's best evidence: it takes on the Congressional Budget Office's lower cost estimate by naming the assumptions behind it.5 That's the skeleton for the 628 paper: one crisis, one claim, a handful of reasons.6

Two-minute drill

  • Fix it: "It was decided that interdiction would continue."

    Passive voice with no actor (run the "by an idiot" test). Name the decider your source identifies: "[The commander] continued interdiction." If you don't know who decided, that's a research question, not a style choice.

  • Fix it: "This shows that airpower had limits."

    Bare "this," plus a claim too vague to argue. Name the noun and sharpen the claim: "The interdiction campaign's results show that…" Then say which limits, and who imposed them.

Nebraska Is Running the Strongpoint Play

Electoral-vote.com's Senate table today has an odd row for Nebraska. A Concord POP poll (Oct. 15–18) has Republican incumbent Pete Ricketts at 41% and independent Dan Osborn at 48%, and the Democratic column reads "No Democrat."1 EV's whole reaction was one word: "Wow."

Opinion from here. Whatever the reason for the empty column, the effect is Kennan's strongpoint defense in electoral form.2 A party that can't win a seat under its own label stops contesting it head-on and lets someone else hold the line. Nobody announces that kind of concession. It shows up as an empty column in a poll table.

Compare Michigan on the same page. YouGov (Oct. 2–19) has Democrat Abdul El-Sayed at 48% and Republican Mike Rogers at 46%, a race EV says has "a very small margin for error."3 That's the symmetrical fight: both parties all in, matching dollar for dollar. Kennan's rule was to define interests first and judge threats against them, never the reverse.4 For a party, the interest is the Senate majority. Which seats are worth defending follows from that, not from which ones it fought for last time.

One empty row in a poll table can say more about party strategy than a week of press releases. (S)

Acquisition × Gaddis

850 Jets, Millions of Drones, and the Perception of Means

Last Thursday, the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin announced a 10-year framework for at least 850 F-35s. That's about 85 a year, which matches the fiscal 2027 request. Congress funded 47 in fiscal 2026.1 The deal depends on congressional approval and funding, and the stopgap law running through Dec. 11 generally bars new multiyear buys.1 A week earlier, Defense Secretary Hegseth said the Pentagon wants $50 billion to grow its stockpile of autonomous weapons from thousands of units to millions.2

Opinion: put those side by side and the department is betting on both the exquisite fighter and the cheap swarm. That's coherent only if you believe the money will grow to cover both. Gaddis found that Truman-era strategy rested on the opposite belief: that resources were limited no matter how dangerous the threat.3 The gap between 85 requested and 47 funded is the whole debate in two numbers.

One more figure. GAO found that the F-35's mission-capable rate fell from 67% in fiscal 2021 to 44% in fiscal 2025. Lockheed argues that steady multiyear orders will help fix that.1 Opinion: buying predictability is a reasonable answer to a readiness problem. It's a weaker answer to a strategy question that hasn't yet chosen between jets and drones.

If the money grows, nobody will remember this item. If it doesn't, someone will have to choose, and Congress has already voted once: 47. (S)

Leadership × Gaddis

Change for What? Petraeus, Kennan, and the Order of Operations

Retired Gen. David Petraeus told a Washington audience on Oct. 16 that the US and NATO need "urgent revolutionary institutional change" to prepare for modern war. He wants that change to reach force structure, training, procurement, personnel policy, and bases. He would swap much of the heavily manned legacy force for cheaper, more numerous unmanned systems, and he wants production that can push software changes weekly. He called the new Autonomous Warfare Command a commendable step.1

Opinion: he may well be right, and Ukraine is a strong body of evidence. The leadership lesson is in what the report leaves out. By Military Times' account, Petraeus named no specific adversary or interest the change should serve.1 Kennan's first rule ran the other way: threats only mean something when measured against your interests.2

For anyone leading change at any echelon: say what the change protects before you say what it replaces. People will put up with a revolution in "how" much longer if they can repeat the "why" from memory. The order of operations is interest, then threat, then reorganization. Skip the first step and the reorganization becomes the goal.

Kennan would add one more step: say what the change does not require. He learned that one the hard way (see yesterday's "X" item). (S)

The Writing Desk × War on the Rocks

Steal This Skeleton

Whatever you think of its conclusion, Frederick Lamb and Laura Grego's War on the Rocks piece "Golden Dome Is Technologically and Financially Infeasible" is a clean model of an argument paper.1 The title is the thesis. The full thesis arrives in the fourth paragraph, after a short historical hook. Four subheaded sections follow, each a separate reason: current defenses are unproven, boost-phase timelines are brutal, space-based intercept needs enormous numbers of interceptors, and countermeasures can defeat it.1

It also handles the hardest step in Hollon's PEER paragraph, Evaluation.2 The authors grant that boost-phase defense has real appeal, then explain why it still fails. They take on the Congressional Budget Office's lower cost estimate by naming the assumptions behind it, and they reject the idea that adversaries won't adapt, calling it "the fallacy of the last move." The piece ends by restating the thesis and recommending something specific.1

That's the shape of the 628 paper: one crisis, one claim, a handful of reasons, and a direct answer to the best objection.3 Outline yours the same way, with the thesis in the title and one subheading per reason.

Space tie: Friday's McDougall reading covers the moment Americans realized an enemy could reach the heartland quickly from above.4 Golden Dome is the latest answer to that 1957 fear. Opinion: it won't be the last. (S)

Book of the Day

Kennan Wrote the Memo. Everyone Else Wrote the Budget.

John Lewis Gaddis's Strategies of Containment tours forty years of American grand strategy, from Kennan and NSC-68 through the New Look, Flexible Response, détente, and Reagan.1 It looks like a history of ideas, but it turns out to be about money. Gaddis defines strategy as "the process by which ends are related to means," and he keeps coming back to the means.2

His organizing tool is the difference between symmetrical and asymmetrical containment.3 The symmetrical version meets the adversary wherever it pushes. The asymmetrical version picks its own ground. Kennan's own instinct was asymmetrical. He named five centers of industrial and military power that mattered (the United States, Britain, Germany and central Europe, the Soviet Union, and Japan) and argued for "strongpoint" rather than "perimeter" defense.4 Gaddis says much of that approach's appeal in the Truman years came from "the prevailing perception of means available," the conviction that the country had only limited resources.5 The later chapters follow what happened when that perception changed. As Washington came to believe it could afford more, the list of places worth defending grew.6

The next part is my extrapolation for airmen, not a claim Gaddis makes. Seen this way, Korea and Vietnam look less like separate wars and more like two outputs of the same symmetrical logic. That's worth carrying into this week's airpower readings.

Opinion: it's long and slow in the middle, and essential. Read the introduction and the epilogue first, then decide how many administrations you need in between. Required reading for anyone who has ever briefed a "resource-constrained environment" with a straight face. (S)

Political Strategy

Eight Days Out, Both Parties Face Gaddis's Question

Electoral-vote.com reported that early voting is running well ahead of 2022.1 That's a turnout story. It's also an allocation story, and allocation is where every campaign committee is living this week.

Opinion from here on. The symmetrical move is to match the other side's money in every competitive race. The asymmetrical move is Kennan's strongpoint defense:2 a short list of races where an extra dollar changes the outcome, and polite silence everywhere else. The symmetrical risk is money spread thin across seats that were never in play. The asymmetrical risk is spending election night explaining why you "abandoned" a candidate who lost by two points.

Nobody has ever won a news cycle by announcing a strongpoint defense. Plenty have won elections that way. (S)

Leadership

The "X" Problem: Your Intent Will Be Read by Strangers

In the summer of 1947, George Kennan published "The Sources of Soviet Conduct" in Foreign Affairs under the name "Mr. X." Arthur Krock soon revealed who wrote it, and Walter Lippmann took it apart in a series of columns longer than the original.1 Careless drafting left Kennan agreeing in places more with Lippmann's critique than with his own article, and he couldn't say so publicly until his memoirs twenty years later.2 The article called for "unalterable counter-force at every point," which reads like perimeter defense.3

Opinion: every commander's intent has the same problem. Say what you are not asking for. It's only obvious to the author. (S)

The Writing Desk

Mistakes Were Made (By an Idiot)

The best passive-voice test in circulation: put "by an idiot" right after the verb.1 "The bridge was struck (by an idiot)" reads fine, so it's passive. "The wing struck the bridge (by an idiot)" is nonsense, so it's active. The same handout goes after the bare "this": ask "this what?" and name the noun.2 (S)

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
