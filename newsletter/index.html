Counterpoint · Haun against Clodfelter · review before Sekirin

Counterpoint: Haun Derails Linebacker's Credit

Phil Haun is right and Mark Clodfelter gives interdiction too much credit: the Easter Offensive was broken by aircraft hitting North Vietnamese divisions in the South, before Linebacker I could reach them. Haun writes that the outcomes at Quang Tri, An Loc, and Kontum were settled before the interdiction campaign could have an effect, and that at An Loc and Kontum air power was "decisive in stopping the offensive." Clodfelter credits Linebacker, along with mining, close air support, and stiffer South Vietnamese resistance, with wrecking Hanoi's capacity for offensive war.

Kontum makes Haun's case. Over the first two months, more than 100 B-52 sorties hit the Central Highlands, and a prisoner from the 320th Division said his regiment of 2,500 had taken 1,200 casualties before it ever attacked. On May 12 alone, seventy-eight tactical strikes and twenty-five B-52s hit the approaching force. When the assault came on May 14, two experimental Army Hueys firing TOW missiles knocked out seven of ten attacking tanks on the first day. By May 24 the North Vietnamese had no more than twenty-five working tanks. (Two helicopters, seven tanks. Rolling Thunder spent $9.60 to do a dollar of damage.)

Interdiction's record in Haun is thin. Linebacker I raised Hanoi's time and cost of resupply, but a MACV study found the army had stockpiled in the South before it invaded and was never forced to cancel or change a major operation. By October, a PACAF report estimated that Hanoi could truck in 10,000 tons a day from China, double what it had moved by ship and rail before the campaign. The Air Force's own evaluation that fall said: "we were unsuccessful in our objective of isolating NVN." When the after-action report agrees with the critic, the argument is mostly over.

Clodfelter's best case deserves its full weight. He reports that interdiction cut overland imports from 160,000 to 30,000 tons a month, that mining cut seaborne imports from more than 250,000 tons a month to near zero, and that General Vogt said Linebacker did more damage to the lines of communication than all earlier efforts. He argues that a conventional offensive of tanks and heavy artillery was "tailor-made" for Air Force bombing doctrine, because it needed constant resupply, and that the Politburo found its people in danger of starving. Note that the two books disagree on tonnage: Clodfelter counts what actually arrived, and Haun quotes a PACAF estimate of what Hanoi could move. Ask which number the other side is using before you argue.

It still loses, and Clodfelter's own timeline is the reason. He writes that the North did not feel the full effects of bombing until it had used up its stockpiles, and that by June Giap's offensive was spent. An offensive that was finished in June was not stopped by a squeeze that bit later. We concede the economic point: the blockade hurt Hanoi and gave Kissinger leverage in the fall. First order, direct attack broke the divisions at An Loc and Kontum. Second order, Haun says the recapture of Quang Tri, also won with direct attack, pushed Hanoi toward a ceasefire. Third order, the Air Force kept the wrong lesson; Clodfelter's air leaders held up Linebacker II as proof that bombing works, and Haun notes the service twice dropped armed reconnaissance from its doctrine.

What would change our mind? North Vietnamese records showing that ammunition or fuel shortages, rather than losses at the front, forced Hanoi to call off attacks in May and June. The line for seminar: "The Easter Offensive was broken at An Loc and Kontum before Linebacker could bite; even Clodfelter says the North felt the bombing only after its stockpiles ran out." Monday's book is Haun's theory with propellers: Sekirin's drones attack fielded forces directly, at mass. In plain terms, the airpower that wins battles hits the army, and the airpower that wins arguments at headquarters hits the railroad. (S)

Tehran Has Read the Black Sea Chapter Too

Sekirin would tell Shaun that the biggest threat to a Hormuz hold is the cheapest navy Tehran can build. The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the Iran war without a settlement, and his thesis is that Airmen should prepare now to hold the strait the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. This is our extrapolation from Sekirin, not Shaun's answer, and it tests the clock and the small footprint.

Sekirin's naval evidence cuts first against the hold. Marine drones, paired with ground-launched cruise missiles, let an almost non-existent Ukrainian navy deny the Russian Black Sea Fleet control of much of the western Black Sea. His conclusion repeats it: nimble marine drones now deny large Russian warships access to those waters. Iran can buy that capability cheaply, and in a narrow strait the side with the large, expensive hulls is in the Black Sea Fleet's position. (Being the big ship in a small sea is a role with a short run.) Opinion: Sekirin supports preparation (a), a small American footprint, but he says it has to be unmanned and massed. He would also want the partners' surface forces in preparation (b) to be drone formations, not frigates.

The clock is the harder problem. Sekirin says single drones are cheap, but a commanded, jam-resistant force of them "will not come cheaply," and paying for it means scrapping or shrinking tanks, artillery, and even fighter jets. He also notes that Russia makes around one million drones a year. In a drone war, time works for whoever out-produces the other side. If Tehran can build attack drones faster than the coalition can build interceptors, the clock runs for Tehran, which is the opposite of Shaun's thesis. Yesterday's item on the Reaper fleet, about a quarter lost and fewer than ten replacements available, shows the United States losing that race in the air today.

Our take: Sekirin strengthens Shaun's preparation (a) and puts a production number under his first point. Two questions for the draft:

  1. Which Gulf partner builds the drones and interceptors, and how many a month does the hold need to stay ahead of Iran's output?
  2. What does the hold do on the day an Iranian swarm of surface drones hits a partner's tanker, and who decides? (S)
The Readings So Far · Haun, Clodfelter, Fuller, and Sekirin

Same Kill Chain, New Price Tag

The idea of seeing the enemy and striking him at once is more than fifty years old. What has changed is the price, and who owns the network. Haun describes the Infiltration Surveillance Center at Nakhon Phanom, a complex with two IBM 360-65 mainframes that let 400 analysts read signals from sensors scattered along the Ho Chi Minh Trail. The same war produced Haun's favorite paradox: DIA said Commando Hunt I cut supplies by 82 percent, the CIA said the enemy still got the fewer than fifteen truckloads a day it needed, and Haun says both were right. Clodfelter adds the paperwork; in 1965 and 1966, strike photos piled up in squadron corners because the photo interpreters were swamped.

Sekirin's version fits in a backpack. In Ukrainian brigades with good drone organization, surveillance drones are linked to strike drones and artillery, often through live video over Starlink terminals, and the enemy is attacked as soon as he is detected. Fuller found the drone's most overlooked trait was endurance, which kept al-Qaeda's leaders hiding. Igloo White needed a building and two mainframes. Sekirin needs a terminal and a charged battery. (Progress, of a sort.)

This week's news keeps hitting the same chain. Wednesday's Colombia item said to find the network and the supplier before the drone flies. Yesterday's Reaper item showed what happens when the shooter can't be replaced. Today's Counterpoint adds the part technology hasn't fixed: Kontum worked because a single air boss in an Army helicopter and close teamwork between Air Force controllers and Army advisers identified the targets. Haun's verdict, near the end of his book, is that target identification is the biggest challenge in attacking fielded forces.

Our take: read Sekirin this weekend for one thing. Did Ukraine solve identification, or only speed? If it is only speed, then the oldest problem in this course is now arriving faster. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 5, Day 5 · citations and Chicago style

Run Your Finger Along Sekirin

Cite every paraphrase, and paraphrase far enough from the source that a reader can't line your words up with it. Booth and his coauthors, in The Craft of Research, say you must cite a source every time you use its words, even in paraphrase or summary. The usual failure, they write, is losing track of which words are yours and which are borrowed. Their test is simple. Read the passage, look away, write it in your own words, then run your finger along your sentence. If you find the source's ideas as synonyms in the same order, start over.

Sekirin's discardability passage is an easy trap. He writes that the trait is "derived from the dramatically lower purchase prices" of mass-produced drones, compared with conventional weapons. Before: "Discardability comes from the much lower purchase prices of mass-produced drones compared with conventional weapons." (That is his sentence with a few words swapped, and a citation doesn't fix it.) After: "Sekirin argues that drones are cheap enough to lose, so commanders can send them on missions they would never give a pilot." Then the Chicago note: Illya Sekirin, Rise of the Machines (Warwick: Helion, 2026), 35. Booth's quick tip on notes gives the form: first name first, commas between the parts, and publication data in parentheses. The bibliography entry reverses the name and uses periods.

Intent doesn't count either. Booth's line is "we read words, not minds," and his test is whether the original author would recognize the words or ideas as hers. An old editor's trick: search your draft for every quotation mark and every "argues," and check that a note follows each one before the next sentence starts. A paraphrase you forget to cite is just plagiarism you haven't been caught at yet. (S)

Leadership × Sekirin · via Military Times, November 25

It's Only a Game Until Somebody Signs for It

A new study finds that commercial AI models will pick military targets from drone and satellite images, and that telling them it's a game gets past their refusals. Daniel Terrill reports in Military Times that Vals AI, a San Francisco firm, tested fourteen models from companies including Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI. Every model identified targets at least some of the time, and seven gave targeting information in more than half the tests. Framed as a game, all but one did so in most tests. The models sometimes flagged civilians, ambulances, and surrendering or incapacitated soldiers. Shown images of the elementary school in Minab, Iran, which was bombed on the first day of the Iran war, nine models identified it as a target, and twelve did when told it was a game. The researchers' minimum fix is a classifier that screens military-targeting requests.

Sekirin is betting heavily on exactly this capability. He writes that "machine vision" will make identifying enemy targets more efficient, and that in AI-directed strikes the delay between seeing and striking is "virtually non-existent." His own memo of January 2024, printed in his appendix, asks for AI software that lets drones attack soldiers and equipment in designated areas on their own if the link to the operator is lost. (He was planning this in a staff memo, not a novel.) He is right about the speed, and the speed is the problem. Haun says target identification is the biggest challenge in attacking fielded forces. The delay Sekirin wants to remove is where a human used to do that job.

Opinion: this is a leadership story. When the delay drops to zero, the judgment has to be made before launch. The commander's work moves upstream, into defining the designated area, writing down what the machine may hit, and training operators to doubt the machine's call the way they would doubt a new analyst's first report. The study's worst finding is the game framing, because it shows that the standard changed when the label changed. Units do the same thing when an exercise is "just training."

Our take: Sekirin's drones will get AI targeting, and they should, because jamming makes it necessary. A leader who fields them owes the force a written rule for what the machine may strike and a person who answers for it. A system that picks targets when you call it a game has the judgment of a very fast intern, and you supervise it the same way. (S)

Space × Sci-Fi × Sekirin · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, November 23

The Ansible Has a Sole-Source Contract

The Space Force put sixty-nine satellites for its new data network into orbit in two weeks. Greg Hadley reports in Air & Space Forces Magazine that three Falcon 9 launches from Vandenberg, on November 6, 11, and 20, carried the first satellites of the Space Data Network "backbone," twenty-three per launch by the public catalog's count. SpaceX builds them under a $2.29 billion contract awarded in May. The Space Force says the constellation will be government-owned and government-controlled, and "on track to deliver regional capability this year and global support by next year." A draft Senate spending bill warns that the service seems to "abandon meaningful competition in favor of sole-source awards" and tells it to keep several vendors. The service added five other vendors through small contracts in August.

Sekirin shows why the network is the weapon. In his account, the best Ukrainian brigades link surveillance drones to strike drones and artillery through live video over Starlink terminals, and the enemy is hit as soon as he is seen. He also shows the network missing. On one reconnaissance flight he found as many as six Russian vehicles, had no Starlink to send the footage, and drove the memory card to the commander himself. The battalion's drone teams then lost two thermal drones to Russian jamming while confirming his report. (That is a kill chain whose speed is set by a car.)

Orson Scott Card saw this in 1985. In Ender's Game, Ender Wiggin believes his final battles at Command School are simulations. They are real, and his orders travel instantly to fleets light-years away by the ansible, the novel's faster-than-light radio. Our reading of the twist: whoever controls the link controls what the commander thinks he is doing. That connects to today's AI study, where calling the task a game changed what the machine would do.

Opinion: the Senate is right. A network that every drone, sensor, and shooter depends on should not have one supplier, however good it is, because Sekirin's memory card is what fighting looks like when the network goes away. Government ownership is the right call, and competition is the second half of it. In plain terms, the most important weapon in Sekirin's war is a data link, and the United States should not buy its version of that from only one company. (S)

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

Write it yourself. The "Your turn" box matters most. Producing the paragraph teaches more than reading mine.

Built for comps. Class is book and notebook only. This page is the testing ground: every book read stays here as recall questions, cumulative through the year.

Weekends: reader mail. News sources: Electoral-vote.com, Punchbowl News, Military Times, War on the Rocks, Defense News, Breaking Defense, Air & Space Forces Magazine, SpaceNews, CSIS, and RAND.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
