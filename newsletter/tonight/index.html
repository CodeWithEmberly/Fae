Tonight's Reading · for Monday's seminar

Lambeth, The Transformation of American Air Power: The Booster's Brief, With Receipts

Lambeth argues that between the mid-1970s and 2000, American air power made a nonlinear jump in what it can do in a major theater war. Three things converged: stealth, accurate attack on fixed targets from safe standoff ranges, and the battlespace awareness that better command, control, and surveillance systems provide. The result, he says, is an air arm that can set the conditions for victory from the opening hours of a joint campaign, by taking away an enemy's capacity for organized military action. He wrote it as a RAND analyst with roughly 280 training sorties in more than 35 aircraft types, so expect an advocate who has actually pulled Gs.

The book argues against two camps at once. Against the Army partisans who called the Gulf air war a sideshow, he says air power did most of the work against Iraq's fielded army. Against his own side, he says the old fight over strategic bombing of cities and industry is moot. "Strategic" should name the effect sought, and by that test the Baghdad target list in 1991 did little. He even scolds Robert Pape for filing all this under "tactical" air power.

For this course, Lambeth is the high-water mark of the airman's claim, written before September 11. The syllabus pairs him with the theme of technological promise and asks whether his argument still persuades. Read him for the assumptions: a major theater war, an armored enemy, good intelligence, and a public that wants few casualties. Each of those assumptions held in 1991, and the rest of the course tests each one. (S)

  2. Chapter 1, pp. 9–10. Air power can kill only what it can see, identify, and engage. Lambeth's own example is the failure to eliminate Iraq's nuclear program in 1991, which he files as a failure of air power and intelligence together.

People and Terms

The Cast, With Name Tags

  1. Merrill McPeak. Air Force chief of staff after the Gulf War who called it the first time a field army was defeated by air power, and set off the credit fight (p. 4).
  2. Colin Gray. Strategist Lambeth borrows for the line that there is no such beast as strategic air power (p. 7).
  3. C4ISR. Command, control, communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance; the source of "information dominance," the third leg of his argument (pp. 6–7).
  4. Red Flag. Realistic combat training at Nellis, approved in 1975, built on the finding that a pilot's first ten combat sorties decide whether he survives (p. 62).
  5. Lee Butler. The general who judged SAC a nuclear outfit and proposed abolishing it; SAC and TAC went away in June 1992 (pp. 165–166).
  6. Fire support coordination line. The boundary between ground and air control of fires; once about 20 miles out, later pushed hundreds of miles forward (p. 292).
  7. Halt phase. The Air Force concept of stopping an armored invasion mainly from the air; a defense panel declined to endorse it (pp. 290, 299).
  8. Task Force Hawk. The Army's Apache deployment to Albania in 1999: 5,350 people, 24 helicopters, no combat missions (pp. 208–209).
Bridge From Today · Fravel to Lambeth

Same War, Two Readers, Opposite Moods

Fravel and Lambeth studied the Kosovo air war at the same time and reached different moods about the same facts. In March 2000, the PLA's National Defense University concluded that NATO's surveillance and command systems gave it total information dominance, that air strikes were deadlier than China had imagined, and that air strikes were now the most likely way China would be attacked. The PLA's answer was countermeasures, with more research on defending against air raids. Lambeth, writing the same year, calls Kosovo a win for air power and also a step backward in efficiency from Desert Storm, slowed by gradualism. (The people being bombed were more impressed than the people doing the bombing.)

Keep one question in mind while reading: of Lambeth's three advantages, stealth, standoff precision, and information, which one is easiest for an enemy to take away? Lambeth gives you a hint in chapter 7, where he notes that GPS signals are weak and that JDAM will depend on them entirely. (S)

(Appropriators have had a long year.)

Lambeth wrote about this exact pressure, from the military side. In his conclusion he says the belief that Americans will not accept casualties is oversimplified, and that public support depends on how people judge the stakes of a commitment and the competence of the leaders running it. He cites a pollster's rule from 1999: the public applauds force when the country wins and calls it a mistake when it loses or leaves. Before Desert Storm, not one poll in seven found a majority for immediate action; after a good start, 68 to 84 percent approved. Opinion: Lambeth's model says the December fight is about judged stakes and judged competence more than about the number of casualties. That makes the funding bill a referendum both parties think they can win, which is how shutdowns get started. Lambeth's other lesson applies to the planners: air power can never be more effective than the strategy it serves, and Congress votes on the strategy. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Lambeth, The Transformation of American Air Power

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 1

Call It Strategic After It Works

An attack is strategic because of what it achieves, whatever the label on the target. Lambeth uses that tool in his opening chapter to declare the old fight between airmen and land warriors over strategic bombing largely moot. For most of the Cold War, strategic air power meant nuclear bombers whose reason for being was never to be used. His central claim is that since the mid-1970s, stealth, accurate standoff attack and better battlespace awareness have given American air power a nonlinear jump in what it can contribute to a major theater war. Colin Gray supplies the slogan: "There is no such beast as 'strategic' air power."

Desert Storm is the test case. Models predicted allied casualties in the thousands, and the order President George Bush endorsed allowed that losses could reach 10 percent of fielded ground troops. On opening night some 800 sorties hit Iraq's command and control and air defenses, and the coalition lost one aircraft, a Navy F/A-18. Within three days it owned the air and could strike Iraq's fielded forces with near impunity. Lambeth then makes the concession that gives his definition teeth: attacks on so-called strategic targets in and around Baghdad, power plants among them, had little strategic effect on the outcome. (An airman admitting the downtown target list underperformed is rarer than an F-117 on Iraqi radar.)

The weak seminar comment is "Desert Storm proved air power can win a war by itself." It overgeneralizes from one case and ignores the author's own counterevidence: Lambeth concedes the Baghdad strikes did little, and even Merrill McPeak, the Air Force chief who called Desert Storm the first defeat of a field army by air power, granted that ground forces moved in and dictated terms. The strong version: "Lambeth's claim is narrower. Air power achieved strategic effects against Iraq's army, so the word should name the result sought, and by that test the Baghdad list was the least strategic part of the campaign." Opinion: this is the most reusable idea in the chapter, and it lets airmen keep their favorite word. Lambeth, who has flown some 280 training missions in more than 35 aircraft types, is an open advocate, and his case so far rests on one short war.

The narrow claim survives because each effect led to the next. First order, the opening strikes on Iraq's defenses and command network succeeded for the price of one jet. Second order, the coalition owned the sky within three days, and a 37-day air war was followed by a ground offensive of four. Third order, that success set off a budget war. McPeak spoke of an air "campaign" but only a ground "operation," the Army called the air war a sideshow, and the Pentagon's report to Congress ran nearly three months late amid hundreds of disputes, giving 135 pages to the air war, 100 to the ground war and 85 to naval operations. Measuring strategic effect by what Iraq's army could still do pre-empts the reply that air power merely softened up the enemy. Unable to agree on who won, the services counted pages. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 2

The Hands Were Tied, and the Grip Was Weak

When a campaign fails, sort the causes into limits placed on the force and limits of the force. Richard Hallion blamed Vietnam's results on limits placed on air power, and Lambeth calls that fair as far as it goes, which is about halfway. Strategy failed first, he grants, but the air weapon itself had real deficiencies in equipment, use and organization. The 1972 air war worked better because the war had turned conventional and laser-guided bombs had arrived.

The first column is long. Targets were picked at Tuesday lunches, MiGs sat in sanctuary at five bases inside the 25-mile Hanoi circle, and SAM sites could be hit only while firing. John McNaughton advised McNamara, "We won't bomb the sites, and that will be a signal to North Vietnam not to use them." (Hanoi read the signal differently.) The second column is just as long. The F-105 was designed as a nuclear bomber whose side-by-side hydraulic lines let one round kill both flight controls. A 1961 PACAF manual ordered that "Nuclear training will in every instance take precedence" and held some units to one conventional event per aircrew per year. The F-4 had no internal gun, and through March 1968 Air Force crews fired 224 AIM-7s for 20 kills.

The weak seminar comment is "Air power failed in Vietnam because the politicians tied the airmen's hands." It is half the ledger and ignores the author's own counterevidence. Even Linebacker, with most restrictions lifted, destroyed an estimated 489 vehicles near the DMZ against only 38 farther north, and breaking the siege at Khe Sanh took 2.5 sorties per enemy soldier killed. The strong version: "Lambeth splits the failure in two. Washington's restrictions were real, and the Air Force also went to war with a nuclear bomber, a fighter without a gun and a manual that put conventional training last. Once laser-guided bombs arrived, the Thanh Hoa bridge went down in a day after hundreds of inconclusive sorties." Opinion: Lambeth's rule that civilians should stay out of operational details once objectives are set is easier to write than to follow at a Tuesday lunch.

The lesson outlived the war because each effect fed the next. First order, gradualism gave Hanoi time to grow from about 1,400 antiaircraft guns in August 1964 to 7,000 to 10,000 guns and more than 200 SA-2 sites by early 1967. Second order, the SAMs pushed American fighters below 3,000 feet, where more than 80 percent of U.S. losses occurred, and jamming pods and new tactics helped cut the Air Force loss rate from 3.2 per 1,000 sorties in 1965 to 1.6 in 1967. Third order, aircrews learned to fight inside a dense air defense, a legacy Lambeth says put all four services on a path to Desert Storm. That chain pre-empts the tied-hands excuse: the restrictions bred a threat the force had to beat with its own gear and tactics. So ask why the Navy kill ratio rose from 3.7 to 1 to, for a time, 13 to 1 after Topgun while the Air Force all but dropped dissimilar air combat training. The Navy's best weapon of 1972 was a 480-page report. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 3

The Jet Is the Easy Part

A new airplane buys a chance to win, and the crew that flies it and the chain of command above it decide whether that chance gets cashed. Lambeth's third chapter covers the two decades between Vietnam and the Gulf, when a Soviet buildup in Europe left NATO unable to match the Warsaw Pact weapon for weapon and hunting for force multipliers. Of his three areas of gain, he puts aircrew proficiency first.

The training numbers carry the argument. General Robert Dixon approved Colonel Moody Suter's Red Flag in 1975 on the finding that a pilot's first 10 combat sorties largely decide whether he survives the war, so Nellis would hand him the first 8 or 10 in peacetime. One account called it "the heart-pumpinest, palm-sweatinest war we have." Annual flying time for Air Force fighter pilots rose from about 150 hours in the mid-1970s to more than 230 by the mid-1980s, over twice the Soviet average. Class A mishaps across the services fell from 309 in 1975 to 76 in 1995, so harder training also made safer pilots, a dividend nobody had planned for. Lebanon is the control case. In December 1983, after one SA-7 was fired at an F-14 over Lebanon, President Reagan ordered a reprisal, and at 0330 the European Command moved the launch up to 0630. Twenty-eight A-6s and A-7s went, none with the proper munitions and no crews properly briefed. One A-6 pilot died and his bombardier-navigator was taken prisoner. Lambeth's verdict: merely possessing capable weaponry "meant nothing."

The weak seminar comment is "The Air Force won in the Gulf because it bought F-15s, F-117s, and smart bombs in the 1980s." It ignores the author's own counterevidence: the chapter includes a raid where capable aircraft failed, and a 1982 air battle Lambeth says Israel would likely have won with Syria's equipment. The strong version: "Lambeth's control case is Lebanon in 1983, where the hardware was fine and the planning was pure Vietnam; the payoff came from flying hours and Red Flag." Opinion: the Bekaa equipment swap is a counterfactual nobody can test, and his Libya account says every target was hit effectively while his own footnote cites a count of only 4 of 18 F-111 laser-guided bombs on target. Read his footnotes, because they sometimes argue with his text.

The Lebanon fiasco paid off later, in Nevada and over Iraq. First order, a botched strike cost a pilot and handed Syria's proxies a prisoner for propaganda. Second order, two years later Navy Secretary John Lehman got the Navy its own Red Flag at Fallon, Nevada, where each air wing would spend three weeks before deploying, and the Navy also set up a threat cell called SPEAR. Third order, SPEAR correctly appraised Iraq's air defenses on the eve of Desert Storm, in what the Gulf War Air Power Survey called perhaps the best assessment of them. That chain pre-empts the claim that the 1980s were just a procurement boom, because the payoff that mattered grew out of a humiliation and a schoolhouse. The Navy learned the most from the raid it would most like to forget. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 4

Who Gets the Sorties Decides Who Gets the Blame

When a ground commander says the air commander ignored him, first ask who set the priorities, because friction at a seam usually traces to the command structure. Lambeth's fourth chapter argues that air power's decisive act in Desert Storm came after air control was won: engaging an entire army with precision, at night, from medium altitude. The ugly fight over who owned the sorties, he says, grew mostly out of General Schwarzkopf serving as his own land component commander.

The Army's complaint has numbers behind it. In the first two weeks the Army nominated 1,185 targets, 202 made the air tasking order, and 137 were hit. Over the whole war it nominated 3,067, and little more than a third were attacked. The other ledger is bigger. Of 23,430 weapon releases against discrete aim points, 67 percent went at fielded Iraqi ground forces. Schwarzkopf had approved the air commander's targets, and he told his division commanders back in November that Horner alone was in charge of the air. The corps commanders, in Gordon and Trainor's phrase, saw the battlefield as a "giant bowling alley," caring about the Iraqis in front of them, while Schwarzkopf wanted the Republican Guard behind them. The Gulf War Air Power Survey found that some ground commanders blamed Horner or Glosson without realizing the apportionment was Schwarzkopf's.

The weak seminar comment is "Desert Storm proved airmen fight their own war and ignore the ground commander." It asserts a grievance and ignores the counterevidence, since two of every three bombs and missiles went at the Iraqi army. The strong version: "The corps targets lost to Schwarzkopf's theater priorities. With no separate land component commander, nobody between the corps and the CINC could explain that, so the corps blamed the airman." Opinion: Lambeth is an advocate here. His rebuttal of William Odom's claim that Army tanks and helicopters killed 70 to 80 percent of Iraqi tanks leans on the Air Force's own survey, plus CIA imagery analysts. Expect the Army to notice whose survey it is.

The friction traces back to one command decision. First order, Schwarzkopf kept the land component for himself and, for most of the war, short-circuited his own targeting board by telling Horner and Glosson directly what to hit. Second order, corps commanders who could not see that logic used the fire support coordination line as a fence. The survey dates the XVIII Airborne Corps push north of the Euphrates to February 27. Separately, McPeak told the Commission on Roles and Missions that on the third day of the ground operation that corps commander kept the air commander off the main resupply line between Baghdad and Kuwait for 17 hours, and that a VII Corps extension gave Republican Guard units a sanctuary to escape to Basra. McPeak blamed what happens when "boundaries are set by people who do not have full authority" on both sides. Third order, the grudge outlived the war: in 1994 a senior airman still reported Army distrust at every level. The chain needs no villain, which pre-empts the charge of Air Force selfishness. Lambeth likens the credit fight to asking which scissor blade cut the paper. Schwarzkopf later admitted he knew little of the line debate, so the scissors were missing a hand. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 5

SAC Had to Die So the Bombers Could Go to War

The word "strategic" belongs to effects, and a service that pins it to a weapon will misuse the weapon. Lambeth's fifth chapter covers the 1990s, when the Air Force shrank and got better at once. Fighter wing equivalents fell from 39 to 20, yet stealth, satellite-guided bombs, and bombers cut loose from the nuclear mission made the smaller force more capable. The best passage is about an org chart: the Air Force had to abolish its most famous command before its bombers counted in a conventional war.

General John Chain, SAC's commander in chief, named the problem in 1988. Three pillars of conventional wisdom kept bombers out of theater war, among them the belief that "strategic" meant "nuclear." Lambeth calls this an abiding flaw: Americans attached the word to weapons and targets when it should describe actions and effects. Desert Storm proved the point. B-52s dropped Mk 82s by the thousands, yet General Lee Butler judged their proficiency close to the level of the end of World War II. Show up at the next war with the same act, he warned, and "we would not be invited to participate." Butler decided SAC was "a nuclear outfit, had always been, was, and would always be," and proposed abolishing it. SAC and TAC went away in June 1992. The stealth arithmetic made his case: in Desert Storm a nonstealth package needed 38 aircraft so that 8 could bomb three aim points, while 20 F-117s hit 37.

The weak seminar comment is "Stealth and precision transformed the Air Force after the Cold War." It is true but unarguable, and it credits the hardware with work the institution had to do. The strong version: Chain had the conventional bomber idea in 1988 and got 31 bombers committed before, by Butler's account, the system refused to go further. It took a mediocre Gulf War showing and a dead command to finish the job. Opinion: Chain's three pillars are the most portable tool in the chapter, and any service can run its own vocabulary through them.

Abolishing SAC paid off over Kosovo. First order, abolishing SAC put the bombers under Air Combat Command. Second order, the Weapons School added B-52 and B-1 divisions, fighter and bomber crews began training together, and the B-1B went conventional only. Third order, over Kosovo 21 heavy bombers dropped 11,000 of the 23,000 bombs and missiles expended, and the B-2 dropped a third of the precision munitions on fewer than 1 percent of the sorties. That chain heads off the reply that bombers were always conventional assets in waiting. They flew conventional missions over Vietnam for eight years, and in Butler's telling the experience was lost once they came home.

Lambeth admits the soft spots. He calls it an open question whether reform would have happened had the nuclear competition continued, and concedes fighter and bomber crews still do not speak a common language. Seminar question: if the Berlin Wall had stood, would SAC still exist? Some organizations reform, and others have to be dissolved so their successors can. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 6

My Jeweler Outranks Yours: Kosovo and the Myth of Air Alone

Air power can be no better than the strategy it serves, and "air alone won" is the weakest way to defend it. Lambeth's sixth chapter covers Operation Allied Force, NATO's 78-day air war against Yugoslavia in 1999. He counts it as the third regional win of the decade decided by air power, and as a step backward in efficiency from Desert Storm. NATO ruled out ground forces before the first bomb, expected Milosevic to fold within days, and got a war nobody had planned for.

The numbers show a campaign rationed by committee. The first week averaged about 50 strike sorties a night; Desert Storm's first week ran as high as 1,000 combat sorties a day. With no ground threat to fear, Serb units dispersed and hid, and three weeks in NATO could not confirm a single destroyed tank. NATO later claimed 150 tanks disabled, and Clark cut that to 110 after finding decoys, some made of milk-carton material. The Americans fought each other, too. General Short wanted Belgrade's power plants and ministries, General Clark wanted the fielded forces, and Clark settled one exchange with "My jeweler outranks yours." The Army sent 5,350 people to support 24 Apaches, at a cost of $234 million, and the Apaches flew no combat missions.

The weak seminar comment is "Kosovo proved air power alone can win a war." It overgeneralizes from one case and ignores the author's own counterevidence. Lambeth, who is about as pro-air as RAND analysts come, says there is good reason to be wary of that claim. The strong version names the other pressures: Russia backing the Western position on June 3, Milosevic's indictment as a war criminal, and cronies whose business interests kept getting bombed. Then it adds the signs of a coming ground war: engineers strengthening the road from Durres to Kukes for Abrams tanks three days before he quit, and a plan for a 170,000-strong invasion force. Opinion: the bombs did the punishing, and the prospect of ground troops made the punishing count.

The decision to rule out ground forces echoed through the whole war. First order, Serb units could disperse and hide, so attacks on fielded forces mostly failed while ethnic cleansing got worse. Second order, Clark asked for 300 more aircraft, drawing scarce AWACS and Prowlers from the Persian Gulf and Korea, and moving jets from Incirlik halted patrols over northern Iraq. Third order, Lambeth warns that gradualism may be here to stay in coalition wars, and that it risked squandering the reputation air power earned in Desert Storm. The argument is hard to rebut because his witnesses are airmen. Short, Ryan, Fogleman, and McPeak all faulted the strategy, which heads off the charge that only jealous soldiers doubt air power.

Lambeth's verdict is one line: air power "can never be more effective than the strategy it is intended to support." Seminar question: would a Desert Shield-style force on the Albanian and Macedonian borders have shortened the 78 days? Somewhere a 5,350-person task force is still waiting for its Apaches to fly. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 7

Space Joined the Fight When the Fighter Pilots Took the Keys

Culture follows command: put the customer in charge of the supplier, and the supplier starts building what the customer needs. Until 1991, Air Force space officers came from secret reconnaissance and nuclear deterrence, trained as engineers, and lived apart from flyers who called them space geeks. Desert Storm showed both camps what satellites could do, and a run of fighter pilots at the head of U.S. Space Command then pulled space into the warfighting mainstream. Lambeth wants air and space to grow together and, as of 2000, calls a separate space service counterproductive.

The Gulf war supplied the evidence. GPS went to war five satellites short of full coverage, and handheld receivers went from a few hundred to thousands. A communications satellite was moved from the Pacific to the Indian Ocean, a first for combat, and satellites carried 85 percent of theater communications. Missile-warning satellites caught Scud plumes within 30 seconds of launch, after the Iraqis obligingly ran three practice launches. After the war, Horner, the Desert Storm air commander, took over U.S. Space Command. The Space Warfare Center opened on November 1, 1993, and in a 1994 test an F-15E attacked a simulated mobile Scud launcher at night using satellite coordinates. Ashy, Estes, Myers, and Eberhart followed Horner: five fighter pilots in a row.

The weak seminar comment is "Desert Storm was the first space war." It treats McPeak's slogan as a finding, and Lambeth himself grants that purists might object, since space only did support work. The strong comment names the mechanism: the hardware was in orbit by 1991, and what changed afterward was who ran it. The ICBM crews joined Air Force Space Command and lent the space technicians a shooter's credentials, and the old space push became operator pull. Opinion: the sharpest evidence comes from a critic. Senator Bob Smith said in 1998 that the Space Warfare Center mostly helped aircraft drop bombs: "this is not space warfare; it is using space to support air warfare." Lambeth calls that correct. So the integration he celebrates is the Air Force using space for the Air Force.

Putting pilots in charge changed how every service used space. First order, pilots running Space Command made space an operator's tool, with space training at the Weapons School and Red Flag. Second order, the other services came to lean on it, and the head of Navy Space Command admitted the Navy is "functionally dead in the water without space." Dependence also made space a target: GPS signals are weak, and JDAM would rely wholly on them. Third order, reliance outran skill: after Kosovo in 1999, the director of the National Reconnaissance Office complained that allied commanders made parts of the campaign harder on themselves through underdeveloped space literacy. We infer that this pre-empts the better-satellites objection: the satellites were up before 1991, and habits changed only when the command did.

Smith faulted the Air Force for promoting too few space officers to the top, while Lambeth credits its progress to fighter pilots running space. Can both be right? Space became a warfighting medium the day the pilots arrived, which tells you who was keeping score. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 8

The Air Power Debate Is Two Fights, and Only One Still Matters

Before you argue about air power, ask which argument you are in. Lambeth says the American debate is two fights that people lump together. One is classic theory: does strategic bombing work, and can air power win alone? The other is roles and money: who fights the deep battle, on whose budget. Lambeth calls the first largely moot and the second the real fight. His tool for ending the first is a definition: strategic describes the effect on an enemy's ability to fight, whatever the target.

The Gulf numbers carry the case. Infrastructure attacks took no more than 10 percent of allied strike sorties, and destroying nearly all of Iraq's petroleum capacity produced no significant military results, because the war ended before fuel shortages could bite. The heavy work fell on Iraq's army; the Republican Guard was a strategic target from the start. So Lambeth says Robert Pape, who studied 33 strategic air campaigns and concluded that "strategic bombing cannot be decisive," split the war into strategic and theater halves it never had. The money fight is messier. McPeak carved a theater into rear, close, deep, and high battles, giving airmen the last two; the Army's Jay Garner summed up the Air Staff line as "Air Force uber alles." The fire support coordination line, once assumed to sit about 20 miles out, had been extended hundreds of miles forward, and the Army used that to claim control of all fires over land.

The weak seminar comment is "Desert Storm proved air power can win alone." It walks into a question Lambeth calls a nonissue and ignores his own counterevidence: the 23,000 sorties against Iraqi ground forces never reached the 30 percent attrition Schwarzkopf wanted before the ground push. The strong comment says air power seized the air, then broke Iraq's fielded army and made the ground endgame relatively painless, a strategic effect that required nothing in downtown Baghdad. Opinion: Lambeth wins the definition fight and is shakier on the scorecard. When critics say more Iraqi equipment was captured or destroyed in four days of ground war than in six weeks of air strikes, he asks whether it had already been abandoned. Good question. A question is still not a count.

The second fight matters more because its effects kept compounding. First order, air power gutted Iraq's deployed forces and made the four-day ground war cheap. Second order, the Army dug in to protect its doctrine and programs, then moved into deep attack itself with ATACMS, the stealthy Comanche helicopter, and the Army After Next plan. Third order, a standoff the civilians would not referee: a $4.5 million National Defense Panel declined to endorse the Air Force halt phase. The budget explains the heat; in 1998 the Army drew 25 percent of obligational authority against about 30 percent each for the Air Force and Navy. That chain pre-empts the rice-bowl dismissal; Lambeth calls it honest disagreement, and his numbers show why each side felt cornered.

If the Army's new vision quietly borrowed air power's speed, range, and precision fires, who won the theory argument? The Army insisted that boots win wars, then went shopping for a stealth helicopter to win them from farther away. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Lambeth, chapter 9

Lambeth's Last Word to Airmen: Stop Saying "Dominant"

An argument for a new weapon wins when it is pitched to the commander who must use it, with the limits conceded first and the benefits stated in the other service's terms. That is Benjamin Lambeth's closing advice, aimed at his own side. Over two decades, he argues, American air power became truly strategic, able to shape a joint campaign from the opening blow, producing "the effects of massing without having to mass." Land power's role against large-scale aggression may increasingly be "to secure a win rather than to achieve it."

The concessions come first, and they are real. Bombers from the continental United States are too few to run the halt-phase strategy alone. Korea would not repeat the Gulf: more than 500,000 troops along the demilitarized zone, a regime fighting for survival, and no hope of keeping American combat deaths under 200, the Desert Storm figure. He tells airmen to repudiate Douhet's claim that command of the air is victory (false in 1921, false now) and to stop boasting about dominant air and space power, a habit that put the other services on the defensive in the budget wars. The audience he wants is the theater commanders in chief, who worry about their war plans and their reputations and are the most credible witnesses before the Joint Requirements Oversight Council. The Air Force chief of staff, however eloquent, gets heard there as a man shopping for his own service's hardware. (Lambeth never says the perception is wrong.)

The weak seminar comment is "Lambeth shows that air power can now win wars." It ignores the author's own counterevidence: the chapter says three times that air power cannot win wars single-handedly. The strong comment is narrower: against an armored invasion, air does the heavy killing and ground forces finish and hold. His best number is that the Gulf coalition lost fewer lives to hostile fire in five weeks of fighting than to accidents in five months of workups. Opinion: his evidence runs thin in one place. The claim that air power's effects are now a question of when, not whether, leans on Kosovo, a campaign he calls agonizingly slow and burdened by gradualism. One slow win is one data point. (Still one more than Douhet had.)

His advice is about salesmanship because the fight moved from the battlefield to the budget. First order, precision and stealth let a few aircraft do what Vietnam's huge strike packages could not. Second order, air power became a rival for deep attack, and the land warfare community claimed the new paradigm for itself just as a balanced budget forced triage among duplicative deep-attack programs. Third order, the fight moved to Pentagon models like TACWAR, which makes air power kill every surface-to-air missile along the front before a strike can pass, the attrition bias General Ronald Fogleman protested in a 1996 memorandum. We infer that an airman who shouts about dominance in that room loses twice, once to the model and once to the Army. The chain pre-empts the charge of Douhet-style zealotry, because Lambeth buries Douhet before the Army can.

Seminar question: Lambeth notes the Air Force still preferred the F-22, joint strike fighter, and C-17 over more B-2s. Is that the parochialism he scolds, or a service quietly hedging against his optimism? His final advice to airmen comes down to talking about the other fellow's problems, which also works at dinner parties. (S)

Chapter recall

  • What makes air power "strategic," in Lambeth's view?

    The objective pursued and the effect achieved, not the type of target; he borrows Colin Gray's line that there is no such beast as strategic air power. L 7

  • What does Lambeth concede about the Desert Storm strikes on Baghdad?

    Attacks on power plants, leadership and transportation nodes had little strategic effect on the outcome, while the air campaign did achieve strategic effects against Iraq's forces. L 8

  • How did the Title V report divide its pages among the services' wars?

    135 pages on the air war, 100 on the ground war and 85 on naval operations, after a delay of nearly three months over hundreds of disputes. L 5

  • How does Lambeth answer Hallion's claim that Vietnam showed limits on air power rather than limits of it?

    He calls it fair but partial: strategy failed first, but the air weapon also had real deficiencies in equipment, use and organization. L 13

  • What did the 1961 PACAF F-100 training manual say about conventional training?

    Nuclear training took precedence in every instance, and units outside the mobile strike force were limited to one conventional event per aircrew per year. L 36

  • What happened to the Navy's kill ratio after Topgun?

    It rose from 3.7 to 1 to as high as 13 to 1, while the Air Force, which had all but dropped dissimilar air combat training, saw its ratio decline. L 48

  • What finding justified Red Flag?

    A pilot's first 10 combat sorties largely determine whether he survives, so Red Flag gave crews the equivalent of their first 8 or 10 in peacetime. L 62

  • What went wrong with the Navy's December 1983 strike in Lebanon?

    EUCOM moved the launch up to 0630 at 0330, so 28 aircraft went with the wrong munitions and unbriefed crews; an A-6 pilot was killed and his bombardier-navigator captured. L 97

  • What did the Navy build after the Lebanon raid, and what did it pay off in?

    Strike U at Fallon and the SPEAR threat cell, which correctly appraised Iraq's air defense system before Desert Storm. L 99

  • What share of Desert Storm weapon releases went against fielded Iraqi ground forces?

    About 67 percent, or 23,430 releases against discrete aim points. L 137

  • Why did corps commanders blame Horner for their unserviced targets?

    They did not realize the apportionment was Schwarzkopf's, who as his own land component commander favored the Republican Guard over the targets in front of each corps. L 134

  • What did the XVIII Airborne Corps do with the fire support coordination line on February 27?

    Pushed it north of the Euphrates. Separately, McPeak said that on the third day of the ground operation the corps commander blocked air strikes on the main Baghdad-Kuwait resupply line for 17 hours. L 135

  • What were General Chain's three "pillars" blocking conventional use of SAC's bombers?

    That the bombers were built for nuclear use only, that "strategic" meant "nuclear," and that theater war belonged to tactical fighters. Lambeth adds that "strategic" should describe actions and effects. L 164

  • Why did General Butler propose abolishing Strategic Air Command?

    After Desert Storm he concluded SAC was a nuclear outfit at its core and could never be a competent conventional organization; SAC and TAC were disestablished in June 1992. L 166

  • What was the Desert Storm stealth comparison Lambeth cites?

    A nonstealth package needed 38 aircraft so that 8 could bomb three aim points; 20 F-117s attacked 37 aim points. L 156

  • What did NATO claim about Serbian tanks, and what did Clark later revise it to?

    NATO first claimed 150 of an estimated 400 tanks disabled; Clark scaled that to 110 after finding many were decoys. L 197

  • Besides bombing, what pressures does Lambeth list behind Milosevic's capitulation?

    Loss of Russian support, his war-crimes indictment, elite and crony pressure, and above all the prospect of a NATO ground intervention. L 192

  • What did Task Force Hawk cost, and what did it accomplish in combat?

    About $234 million by May 31; the Apaches flew no combat missions, though its counterbattery radars helped target Serb artillery late in the war. L 209, 212

  • What does Lambeth say turned "space push" into "operator pull" after Desert Storm?

    Five fighter-pilot commanders in a row at U.S. Space Command, starting with Horner, the Desert Storm air commander, plus the Space Warfare Center founded in 1993. L 238-241

  • What did Senator Bob Smith say was wrong with the Air Force's space effort in 1998?

    He said the Space Warfare Center mostly used space to help aircraft drop bombs, which is using space to support air warfare, and he threatened to push for a separate space service. L 251-252

  • How many handheld GPS receivers did the program office buy in its wartime emergency purchase?

    It bought 13,000, of which some 4,500 reached the theater. L 236

  • What are the two arguments Lambeth says make up the air power debate, and which one matters?

    A theory argument over whether strategic bombing works and whether air power can win alone, and a roles and resources argument over service doctrine and budget shares; the second is the one that matters. L 262-263

  • What share of allied strike sorties went to infrastructure targets in Desert Storm?

    No more than 10 percent; the decisive work was against Iraq's fielded forces. L 267

  • What was the 1998 Joint Publication 3-09 fight about?

    Whether the land component commander could control all fires in his area, with the fire support coordination line pushed hundreds of miles forward; the Joint Chiefs met on May 12, 1998, but disagreement persisted. L 292

  • What new role does Lambeth propose for U.S. land power against large-scale aggression?

    To secure a win rather than to achieve it, with air power carrying the bulk of the work of beating down enemy forces first. L 321

  • Who does Lambeth say is the best audience for the "air power story," and why?

    The theater CINCs, because they care about whether their war plans work and are the most credible witnesses before the Joint Requirements Oversight Council; the Air Force chief is seen as an advocate for his own service's hardware. L 316

  • What is wrong with the TACWAR campaign model, according to the critique Lambeth cites?

    It is attrition-based: it makes air power kill every SAM along the front instead of punching a hole, and it gives no credit for destroying ground-controlled intercept sites. L 311
