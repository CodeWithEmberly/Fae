Tonight's Reading · for Friday's seminar

Fravel, Active Defense: Nine Strategies, One Rule About When They Change

Fravel argues that China makes a major change in its military strategy only when two things line up: a war somewhere else shows that the way wars are fought has shifted, and the Communist Party at home is united enough to let its generals respond. China has issued nine national military strategies, called strategic guidelines, since 1949. Three of them, in 1956, 1980, and 1993, were major changes. Senior officers started eight of the nine, and the party chairman only approved them. (Try saying that at a Pentagon budget drill.)

The book argues against the two obvious explanations. The first is threat: China changes strategy when an enemy gets scarier. Fravel's best evidence against it is the calendar. The Soviet Union was China's main threat from the 1969 border clash, and the PLA did not change its strategy for more than a decade. In 1993, China's leaders called their neighborhood the best it had been since 1949 and adopted their most ambitious strategy anyway. The second is emulation: late modernizers copy the leading military. China in the 1950s had Soviet advisors, Soviet factories, and Soviet rifles, and it rejected the Soviet strategy of striking first.

For this course, Fravel is Desert Storm seen from the other side. The syllabus calls the Gulf War's lesson to China a second-order effect of the American victory: Iraq lost, and an adversary learned that some of its basic assumptions were wrong. Tomorrow's question is how a one-party state turns a lesson like that into a strategy, and how long it takes. The answer is about two years, most of them spent waiting for the Politburo. (S)

Bridge From Today · Olsen to Fravel

The Campaign Olsen Graded Had a Second Audience

Olsen graded Desert Storm's strategic air campaign against its unstated political aim and found it short. Fravel shows the same campaign scoring heavily with an audience nobody in Riyadh was aiming at. Fravel's evidence points to the first, and it shows the lesson waiting two years for the party to agree on it. (S)

House Democrats are moving toward term limits for committee chairs, according to Punchbowl News this morning. Rep. Punchbowl counts at least 183 members of next year's caucus elected in 2018 or later. House Republicans have had term limits since 1995; Democrats had them in 2007, until Speaker Pelosi ended them. Opposition is real. Rep. Zoe Lofgren warned that "every African-American chairman would be removed," and Rep. Bennie Thompson said, "The system has worked all these years."

The stakes grew this week. Electoral-vote.com reports that Democrat Scott Colom won Mississippi's Senate race, leaving Democrats and independents with at least 55 seats, and lists the chairs coming in January, including Thompson at Homeland Security and Jamie Raskin at Judiciary. (A gavel is worth more when your party holds it, which is why nobody wants to hand it back.)

Fravel's book is about the same question in a very different system: who has the authority to change course, and when. His answer for China is that change waits for a leadership that agrees on its basic policies and on the structure of authority. Deng replaced the commanders in nine of eleven military regions in early 1980 before the PLA adopted the 1980 strategy. Chapter by Chapter: Fravel, Active Defense

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, introduction

The Threat Arrived in 1969. The Strategy Arrived in 1980.

A threat tells you what a military fears and says very little about when it will change. Since 1949 China has adopted nine national military strategies, called strategic guidelines, issued by the party's Central Military Commission. Three of them, in 1956, 1980, and 1993, set out to make the PLA fight in a new way. Fravel's answer: China made major changes in response to shifts in the conduct of warfare, as revealed in the last war involving a great power or its clients, but only when the Chinese Communist Party was united and stable.

The book opens in September 1980, with China's senior officers in a month-long meeting on beating a Soviet invasion. The Soviets had almost fifty divisions on the northern border. On the last day Deng Xiaoping said "I approve these four characters," meaning active defense, and retired a strategy of protracted war deep inside China. The awkward part for any threat theory: the Soviet Union had been China's main threat since the 1969 clash at Zhenbao Island, and for more than a decade the PLA did not adjust. In 1956 China faced no pressing threat. In 1993 its leaders called the regional environment the best since 1949. What 1980 and 1993 share is somebody else's war: the 1973 Arab-Israeli War, and the 1990-91 Gulf War with its precision-guided munitions and space-based surveillance (the Desert Storm Olsen showed us yesterday, now seen from the other end of the bombsight).

The weak seminar comment is "China changed strategy because the Soviets threatened it." Weak because it ignores the author's own counterevidence. The threat was constant from 1969, so it cannot explain why the change came in 1980. The strong version: "The Soviet threat was constant from 1969. The strategy changed in 1980, after the party reunified and after the 1973 war showed what Soviet operations would look like." Also ask of any source whether the label is one the Chinese used. Many scholars, Fravel included by his own admission, called early-1980s strategy "people's war under modern conditions." The CMC never used the phrase, and generals said it from the late 1950s into the 1990s. Opinion: an author who confesses his own mislabel in the introduction has bought some credit for the chapters ahead.

The 1980 case is strongest as a chain. First order, Zhenbao in 1969 made a Soviet invasion China's main threat. Second order, strategy stood still, because party leaders were consumed by the Cultural Revolution's factional fights and the PLA was restoring order while its officer corps swelled with noncombat posts. Third order, unity returned in steps, with the Gang of Four arrested in October 1976 and Deng consolidating power at the Third Plenum in December 1978; officers pushed again in 1979 and won, and the new guideline sparked an effort to modernize the armed forces. A threat that held steady for a decade cannot explain a date, which disposes of the obvious rival theory before it reaches the podium.

A question for seminar: the Gulf War lessons waited until party unity returned in late 1992, so how long would they have waited otherwise? Fifty divisions on the border will get a general's attention, but only a quiet Politburo gets his strategy signed. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 1

To Explain a Change, Study the Years Nothing Changed

To explain why something changed, test your theory against the times it did not. Fravel defines major change as a new strategy that forces a military to build capabilities it lacks for tasks it cannot yet perform, and he measures it in operational doctrine, force structure, and training. A shift in the conduct of warfare is the necessary condition; party unity is the sufficient condition that lets the military respond. That gives four boxes: a shift plus unity yields major change, a shift plus disunity yields no change, unity without a shift yields minor change, and neither yields nothing, not even tinkering.

The officers lead because the party lets them. In a party-army, senior officers are party members, each what William Odom calls an "agent of the party," and communist countries have seen few if any military coups. So a united party can hand military affairs to its soldiers with minimal oversight, an arrangement Fravel dubs "subjective objective control" (Huntington, meet Lenin). The Central Military Commission is a party committee, chaired by the top leader and staffed mostly by PLA chiefs; the general office or the general staff drafts a guideline, party leaders approve only the broad outline, and an enlarged CMC meeting adopts it. Guidelines are never announced, so they cannot be signals, and they keep no calendar, unlike the American Quadrennial Defense Review.

The weak seminar comment is "China copies the strongest military, as Waltz predicts." Weak because it overgeneralizes. It assumes every war teaches every observer the same lesson and never checks what China actually took from the wars it watched. The strong version: "Waltz gave emulation two paragraphs, and other people's wars teach different lessons to different students." The 1999 Kosovo War showed off stealth and precision strike, and also that camouflage could blunt precision-guided munitions. A state that expects to be bombed might study the camouflage. Opinion: airmen should find that sobering, since the lesson the target draws from a precision campaign may be how to hide from it. Run this check on any plan: which box are we in, and have we looked at the empty ones? Fravel compares his three major changes with the minor ones and the quiet years to avoid the bias of studying only the hits.

Disunity's effects stack up in order. First order, a divided party pulls the military into governance and keeping law and order. Second order, factions court the army as the ultimate guarantor of party rule, the struggle spreads into the ranks, and mass campaigns eat training time. Third order, strategy stalls even when the outside world is shouting for change, because the party cannot agree or will not delegate. Fravel then closes the obvious escape hatch, that a threat would simply reunify the party: rising tension with Moscow after 1969 did little to heal a leadership split by the Cultural Revolution. That pre-empts the claim that unity is just threat in disguise.

A question for seminar: Fravel says future major change could come from new overseas missions, so long as the party stays united; which war would China be watching then? Waltz spent two paragraphs on emulation, and Fravel built a whole research design around the years nothing happened. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 2

Guerrillas Got the Legend, Regulars Won the War

Before you accept the label an army fights under, check how it actually fought. Fravel's second chapter applies that test to the Chinese civil war and finds the popular picture mostly wrong. Of the CCP's three forms of warfare (mobile, positional, and guerrilla), the most common was mobile warfare by regular main force units, and those units carried out the decisive operations. The chapter also supplies the vocabulary the PLA still uses: the strategic guideline, active defense, luring the enemy in deep, and people's war.

The numbers make the case. In the fifth encirclement campaign of 1933 and 1934, Chiang Kai-shek sent about one million soldiers against 120,000 Communists and closed a ring of blockhouses around them, and positional defense from weakness failed. Of roughly 100,000 who set out on the Long March, about 10,000 reached northern Shaanxi. The Hundred Regiments offensive against Japan in August 1940 cost 17,000 casualties and provoked General Okamura's three alls campaign, which cut the population under CCP control almost in half, to 25 million. The end came by field battle: in six months of 1948, the Nationalists lost 1.5 million soldiers. And the phrase people's war? Mao first used it in April 1945, and it caught on only in 1958.

The weak seminar comment is "Mao won with guerrillas, so Chinese strategy is people's war." Weak because it repeats the legend without evidence. Fravel counts the campaigns and the casualties, and the comment never engages them. The strong version: "Fravel shows guerrilla warfare dominated only in the stalemate phase, regulars in mobile warfare decided the war, and the phrase people's war barely existed until 1958." Mao's operating rule, "fight when you can win, leave when you cannot fight and win," is maneuver doctrine wearing a peasant hat. Opinion: also check the alibi of whoever wrote the history. Stripped of military responsibility at Ningdu in October 1932, Mao could pin the fifth-campaign disaster on others at Zunyi in 1935, though Fravel judges that Mao's own ideas would also have failed catastrophically. Being fired early turned out to be a fine career move.

The chain of effects runs long. First order, the blockhouse campaign smashed the Central Soviet and forced the Long March. Second order, Zunyi let an untainted Mao blame his rivals and raise his standing, and his 1936 lecture on strategy was canonized in the party's 1945 resolution on history. Third order, after 1949 his verdict became a rhetorical club against rival strategies, the civil-war arguments returned in the mid-1960s and late 1970s, and civil-war habits shaped strategy until 1993. We infer the argument is hard to rebut because Fravel builds it largely from the party's own official histories, so a defender of the guerrilla myth ends up arguing with Beijing's records.

Active defense was a doctrine for the weak: wait for the blow, then counterattack. Mao put it plainly in 1939: "we will not attack unless we are attacked." Fravel notes that China's growing power raises serious questions about what that means once China is no longer the inferior side. So ask: what does an underdog's strategy become when the underdog bulks up? A creed written for a hunted army fits oddly on a superpower. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 3

Moscow Sold the Guns; Beijing Wrote Its Own Plan

To beat a rival explanation, test it in the case built for it to win. Fravel does that with the 1956 guideline, China's first national military strategy, adopted by an enlarged Central Military Commission in March 1956. If any country should have copied its patron's strategy, it was China in the 1950s, a late modernizer allied to one of the two strongest militaries on earth. China did not copy. Senior officers led by Marshal Peng Dehuai wrote a forward defense against a US invasion because they saw warfare changing after World War II, Korea, and the bomb, and a remarkably united party left them alone to do it.

The Soviet footprint was enormous: equipment for 60 infantry divisions, 44 of 156 factories devoted to defense industry, almost 600 military advisors and at least 7,000 technical experts. Yet on May 22, 1955, Defense Minister Georgy Zhukov told Peng that a nuclear attack would be decisive within minutes. Peng replied that Germany and Japan had struck first and lost anyway. His 1956 plan assumed China would absorb the first blow: no more than a quarter of the ground forces would hold fortified coastal areas while the rest counterattacked, denying the United States a quick victory for roughly six months. Soviet advisors insisted the offensive was the only route to victory, and one called active defense metaphysics. Peng's summary of the differences: China's method suited its backward situation, "not because we are particularly smart."

The weak seminar comment is "China copied the Soviet model; look at the uniforms." Weak because it treats what China bought as proof of what China decided. Uniforms and rifles say nothing about whose war plan the PLA wrote. The strong one: "Peng rejected first strike to Zhukov's face, and the Soviet-style general staff of 1954 was scrapped in 1957 for a three-department structure that lasted four decades." Opinion: ask what an ally rejected, since what it bought tells you little. Ownership was never in doubt: Mao skipped every enlarged CMC meeting until June 1958 and approved Peng's report in April, after the fact. As Mao put it, "I have not interfered in military affairs for four years." Few chairmen have delegated so cheerfully, and fewer stayed that way.

The second- and third-order effects show how deep the break ran. First order, the Zhukov exchange clarified Peng's thinking and hardened his wish for a formal guideline and a Chinese military science. Second order, a 1958 enlarged CMC meeting on dogmatism demoted Liu Bocheng and Xiao Ke and ordered regulations drafted with China's own experience as the basis; eighteen combat regulations on combined arms followed between 1961 and 1965. Third order, Peng's purge at Lushan in 1959 got his report negated, yet Lin Biao's 1960 replacement moved about four divisions out of roughly one hundred. The 1956 ideas resurfaced in the late 1970s, and General Zhang Zhen used the 1956 strategy as a reference when drafting the 1993 one. That chain pre-empts the obvious counterargument: if emulation fails where conditions favored it most, it can hardly carry the weight anywhere else.

So ask: would officers have owned this strategy had the party been divided? Peng could out-argue a Soviet marshal; a private letter to Mao about the Great Leap Forward proved a tougher room. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 4

When the Chairman Rewrites the War Plan, Check Who Was in the Room

A strategy change that tracks the leader's domestic enemies better than the foreign ones is politics in uniform. In June 1964, Mao Zedong threw out the forward defense of the northeast and revived "luring the enemy in deep," the civil war habit of yielding ground and grinding the invader down in a long war. Fravel calls it the anomaly of the book: the only one of China's nine guidelines started by the top party leader instead of senior officers, and a retrograde change that swapped the current strategy for an old one. His explanation: Mao was hunting revisionists inside the party, and a war scare was the handiest tool for pulling power from Beijing's planners.

Mao unveiled the change on June 16, 1964, at the Ming Tombs, before the Politburo Standing Committee and regional party secretaries. No generals were consulted; Lin Biao, who ran the army, was briefed weeks later. The decision came three months before the Gulf of Tonkin incident, months before China's first atomic test, and well before Moscow massed troops on the northern border. Even after Tonkin, Mao told Le Duan that America had not sent ground forces. When the American threat faded in June 1965, Mao pushed harder, offering an invader Shanghai, Nanjing, and Wuhan. He even said the quiet part aloud: "if the enemy really will not come, that's actually a bad thing," because only an invasion would expose the bad elements at home.

The weak seminar comment is "Mao fell back on people's war because Vietnam scared him." Weak because it asserts a motive without checking the calendar, and the calendar is the author's best counterevidence: the decision came first and the threat came later. The strong version runs on dates: Mao dropped forward defense before Tonkin, in front of party bosses rather than generals, and liked the strategy best after the threat receded. For any plan, ask who was in the room, what threat was named, and whether the plan grows more urgent or less as the threat shrinks. Opinion: a strategy that needs the enemy to show up is a fishing trip, and Mao said as much: "you can't catch fish without bait."

The chain of effects is what makes the case stick. First order, the guideline pushed war preparation down to provinces and counties. Second order, that decentralization weakened Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, the first-line leaders Mao attacked when he launched the Cultural Revolution. Third order, the party split froze the strategy in place: when the Soviet threat arrived after Zhenbao Island in March 1969, China doubled down on luring in deep, the PLA swelled to 6.31 million, more than 2.8 million soldiers spent 1967 to 1972 on domestic chores, and the guideline survived until 1980. That chain pre-empts the external-threat reading, because a strategy driven by fear of America should have softened when the fear did, and this one hardened.

One question for seminar: if a strategy exists only as remarks scattered over a year, which the president of the Academy of Military Science later found in pieces, was it ever a strategy? Mao planned to lure the Americans in deep. He lured his own colleagues instead. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 5

The Answer Was Ready by 1978. The Party Was Not.

Before crediting an event with causing a change in strategy, date the idea: if the reformers' case predates the event, the event is a footnote, and the holdup is usually politics. The 1980 guideline, "active defense," was the second of China's three major changes: a forward defense of the northern border built on positional warfare and combined arms. The Soviet threat cannot explain the timing, since it had grown for over a decade, to fifty divisions by 1979. Two things did. Senior officers read the 1973 Arab-Israeli War as proof that warfare had changed, and they could act only after Deng Xiaoping beat Hua Guofeng and restored party unity.

The paper trail is long. Su Yu sent Mao, Zhou Enlai, and Ye Jianying a report attacking the dispersed, retreat-first strategy in February 1973. In January 1974 a seven-person PLA delegation studied the war in Egypt and Syria and named anti-tank and antiair combat its defining features. Yet in December 1977 the CMC formally affirmed "active defense, luring the enemy in deep." Su's January 1979 lecture reached seventy percent of General Staff cadres by March. Meanwhile Deng won the Third Plenum in December 1978, replaced commanders in nine of eleven military regions in early 1980, and pushed Hua's allies off the Standing Committee that February. Only then did the "801" seminar open, on September 17, 1980, with more than one hundred senior officers. The Academy of Military Science combed Mao's statements to show he rarely meant luring in deep as strategy, a feat Fravel calls gymnastics. Deng closed the deal: "I approve these four characters."

The weak seminar comment is "China changed strategy because the PLA got bloodied in Vietnam in 1979." Weak because it confuses a vivid event with the cause. It never asks whether the reform case existed before the war, and it did. The war was ugly: 7,915 killed and sixteen days to take Lang Son, fifteen to twenty kilometers from the border. The strong version: "Su Yu lectured a month before the invasion on five years of work, the 1980 planning papers barely mention the war, and a limited offensive teaches little about stopping a Soviet invasion." Opinion: the Vietnam story tempts because it has a body count. The calendar has better evidence.

The effects confirm the cause. First order, forward defense replaced strategic retreat. Second order, the organization followed: new combat regulations, combined arms group armies, and the "802" exercise at Zhangjiakou in September 1981, 110,000 troops and 1,300 tanks and armored vehicles, the largest since 1949 (after two dress rehearsals, so call it theater with live tanks). Third order, cuts in 1980, 1982, and 1985 halved the PLA to about 3.2 million, and defense fell from 17.4 to 8.8 percent of the budget; the 1988 local-wars guideline built on 1980 and left the how for the Gulf War to answer. We infer the clincher: new doctrine and new formations answer Soviet tank columns, and those are what got built.

For seminar: if the officers had the answer by 1978, what were they waiting for? Fravel's reply is a united party under one boss. The Soviet tanks were fast; the strategy moved at the speed of the Politburo. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 6

The Lesson Arrived in 1991. The Party Signed for It in 1993.

A new way of war changes a military's strategy only when the party at home is united enough to let the generals act, so ask of any reform two dates: when the lesson arrived, and when the leadership could sign for it. Fravel's chapter 6 runs that test on the 1993 guideline, the third major change in Chinese strategy since 1949. The Gulf War told PLA officers that warfare had shifted. The party, split after Tiananmen, could not act on the news until the Fourteenth Party Congress in October 1992.

The lag is the evidence. In the fall and winter of 1990, Chinese military analysts predicted the coalition would bog down against Iraq's battle-hardened army and that air power would play a marginal role. (Readers of Olsen may wince.) Desert Storm then flew more than 116,000 sorties, and the ground war lasted one hundred hours. In early March 1991 the CMC launched an army-wide study. Admiral Liu Huaqing put it plainly: "Our past considerations were correct, but a new situation has occurred." Jiang Zemin warned that "backwards technology means being in a passive position and taking a beating." The General Staff sent the CMC reports in 1991 and 1992. The CMC sat on both. Meanwhile up to fifty percent of training time went to political study. Only after Deng's southern tour, and after the Liberation Army Daily printed the PLA's pledge to protect and escort his reforms 308 times, did the party congress install an almost entirely new CMC. That CMC finished a new strategy by the end of December, and Jiang presented it on January 13, 1993.

The weak seminar comment is that China watched Desert Storm and copied the Americans. Weak because "copied" claims more than the evidence shows. China studied the war closely and then had to plan a high-technology war with the equipment it already owned. The strong version uses Fravel's own test of alternatives. Emulation fails because the 1993 problem was how to fight a high-technology war with the equipment China already had. The threat explanation fails too: Jiang's speech introducing the guideline called China's relations with its neighbors the best since the founding of the state. Opinion: a military that rewrites its strategy while calling its neighborhood the friendliest ever is responding to how wars are fought. Its enemy list had gotten shorter.

The second- and third-order effects are what make the case stick. First order, the January 1993 guideline put joint operations in place of the positional, mobile, and guerrilla trinity the PLA had used since 1927. Second order, the 1997 cut of 500,000 shrank the army by 18.6 percent, the 1998 General Armaments Department became the first new general department since 1958, and in 1999 the CMC issued seven campaign outlines, including the PLA's first written doctrine on joint operations. Third order, the 1993 guideline is still the basis of Chinese strategy, and the ground forces department Jiang turned down in 1998 finally arrived in the 2015 reforms of chapter 7. Because each link points at a style of fighting and none names a new enemy, the chain pre-empts the reader who wants 1993 to be about threats.

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 7

Counting Characters: How Fravel Dates a Strategy Nobody Announced

When the archives are shut, read the vocabulary: the words a bureaucracy uses for its own changes tell you how big it thinks they are, and a word count can date a decision nobody announced. Fravel's chapter 7 covers the 2004 and 2014 adjustments to the 1993 guideline and codes both as minor. He calls the analysis preliminary, since the officers involved have not written memoirs. The 2004 change fits his model of shifting warfare; the 2014 change mostly does not, and he says so.

The evidence for 2004 is two wars. On March 24, 1999, NATO began a seventy-eight-day bombing campaign over Kosovo; on May 7, five US JDAMs hit the Chinese embassy in Belgrade, killing three Chinese journalists. A March 2000 PLA report concluded that information superiority was the basic superiority. The 2003 Iraq War produced three PLA studies within nine months, all noting that precision-guided munitions rose from eight percent of munitions in the Gulf War to sixty-eight percent. In June 2004 the CMC swapped informatized conditions for high-technology conditions; an authoritative source called it enriching and improving, which Fravel reads as code for a limited adjustment. The evidence for 2014 is a word count, because nobody announced it. The 2015 white paper dropped four characters, to winning informatized local wars, which one AMS scholar said meant "a qualitative change has occurred." The phrase has appeared in the Liberation Army Daily 94 times, 81 of them since mid-August 2014. A companion phrase about the new situation first ran on August 2, 2014, so Fravel places the decision in July.

The question to ask of any new strategy is whether it describes a new war or a new org chart. The weak seminar comment is that the 2014 guideline answered a rising American threat. Weak because it asserts a motive and offers no evidence for the timing, which is the thing that needs explaining. The strong version follows Fravel: no significant shift in warfare occurred between 2004 and 2014, and the strategy change and the PLA reorganization were announced together in November 2013, where past reorganizations trailed a new strategy by years. Xi Jinping told the CMC in December 2013 that "the command system for joint operations is unsound." Maritime threats he rates a secondary factor. Opinion: the best page in the chapter is the one where the author flags the case his theory explains least.

The second- and third-order effects run from Belgrade to the 2015 reforms. First order, Kosovo persuaded the PLA that air strikes were deadlier than imagined and China quite vulnerable, so it studied counter-air raid operations and the Serbian defense. Second order, the 2004 guideline made integrated joint operations the main form of operations, and the September 2004 CMC seated the service commanders for the first time. Third order, the 2015 reforms turned seven military regions into five theater commands, broke four general departments into fifteen organizations, created a ground forces command first proposed in the early 1980s, and cut 300,000 troops. Because the PLA's studies hunted for countermeasures, the chain pre-empts the charge that China was simply copying the United States.

Joint operations became the goal in 1993, and the command system was still being rebuilt for them in 2015. Doctrine writes quickly; org charts fight back. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, chapter 8

Few Bombs, No Generals: Why China's Nuclear Strategy Never Moved

A strategy changes only where someone below the top is allowed to change it. China's conventional strategy changed nine times after 1949; its nuclear strategy, assured retaliation with a small force that can survive a first strike, has barely moved since the first test in October 1964. Fravel's explanation is ownership. Top party leaders never delegated nuclear strategy to senior officers, so the views of Mao, Zhou Enlai, and Deng Xiaoping still set the terms. Mao summed up the doctrine for Edgar Snow months after the test: "To have a few is just fine."

The best evidence is a document that died. In the mid-1980s a Second Artillery group under Li Lijing spent almost three years drafting a Nuclear Strategy Outline and circulated it as a classified document at the end of 1988. Yang Shangkun, the senior CMC member after Deng, said it should be killed, recalled every copy, and banned discussion. The content, built on Deng's idea of limited retaliation, was harmless. The author was the problem: "the Second Artillery does not have the authority to do this." Earlier, at the January 1955 meeting that decided to build the bomb, Peng Dehuai was the only senior officer among roughly ten participants. Zhou, not even a CMC member, summoned Zhang Aiping at midnight on May 30, 1965 to create the missile force. In September 1980, a speech by He Jinheng was the first time other senior PLA officers heard how the weapons would be used. (Sixteen years after the first test, the generals finally made the need-to-know list.)

The weak seminar comment is that China kept a small arsenal because it could not build a bigger one. Weak because it is true for the early years and then overgeneralizes to fifty, ignoring the author's evidence that the strategy held while the technology changed. That is the technological determinism of Lewis and Xue's 1988 China Builds the Bomb. The strong version uses Fravel's test: the strategy held steady for fifty years while the available technology changed dramatically. Leaders chose early: Zhou put missile warheads ahead of gravity bombs in December 1963. Opinion: if technology drove the strategy, the strategy should move when the technology does, and a theory that predicts change and gets fifty years of stillness has some explaining to do.

The chain starts with the 1964 government statement that China would never use nuclear weapons first. First order, the pledge fixed a retaliatory posture and cut nuclear planning off from conventional war. Second order, a force that must absorb a first strike prizes survivability: dispersed bases, tunnels nicknamed the underground great wall, then the mobile DF-21 in 1991 and DF-31 in 2006. Third order, by the 1993 guideline the Second Artillery had added conventional missiles and a long-range strike mission, yet nuclear strategy did not change with any guideline; only implementation did, now roughly sixty ICBMs and about eighty warheads able to reach the United States. One variable, delegation, explains nine conventional changes and zero nuclear ones, leaving rival explanations to account for the stillness. Fravel calls it the exception that proves the rule.

Seminar question: as the arsenal grows to answer US missile defense, at what number does a few stop being few? Whatever the number, nobody in uniform will be asked to pick it. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Fravel, conclusion

Watch the Wars Abroad and the Purges at Home

To forecast when China will change its military strategy, watch two gauges: a foreign war that exposes a gap between PLA capabilities and future war, and the unity of the party at home. Fravel's conclusion is that the three major changes, in 1956, 1980, and 1993, each came when a shift in the conduct of warfare met a united party, which let senior officers lead. A major change means a new vision of warfare that then drives reforms in doctrine, force structure, and training. The definition has teeth: the one-million-man downsizing of 1985, long read as a change in strategy, fails it.

The pattern repeats. In 1956, amid unprecedented party unity, Su Yu and Peng Dehuai drew on World War II, Korea, and the nuclear revolution. In 1980, after Deng Xiaoping restored unity following the Cultural Revolution, Su Yu, Song Shilun, Yang Dezhi, and Zhang Zhen read the Soviet threat through the tank and air battles of the 1973 Arab-Israeli War. In 1993, after Deng mended the split over Tiananmen, Liu Huaqing, Chi Haotian, Zhang Zhen, and Zhang Wannian responded to the 1990-91 Gulf War (yes, Olsen's war). The exception is 1964, the only time the top leader changed strategy himself. Mao acted as part of his attack on party revisionists, which ended in the Cultural Revolution; security was not the motive.

The weak seminar comment is that the party controls the PLA, so strategy is whatever the chairman wants. Weak because it is true but unarguable, and it ignores the author's own count: the chairman started only one of nine strategies. The strong version: of nine strategies since 1949, senior officers initiated and led eight, subject only to the chairman's approval. A party-army needed no civilian shove, which Fravel calls a possible "Leninist advantage" in changing strategy. He turns Huntington around too: a politicized army can still be professional, as long as the party holds together. "When unity is absent, professionalism atrophies." Opinion: the Western theory that generals resist change until civilians push them gets China backward. In nuclear strategy the civilians did the resisting.

The causal chain starts with unity. First order, a united party delegates military affairs to senior officers without fearing a coup. Second order, those officers study foreign wars, because a late modernizer must; invasion threats were necessary but not sufficient, and the wars showed how to prepare. Third order, the logic now points at the United States, whose operations the PLA will keep studying, and Fravel warns that Xi Jinping's anticorruption drive, which caught Zhou Yongkang, Xu Caihou, and Guo Boxiong, could open a split that blocks change even when the security environment demands it. One mechanism, delegation by a united party, explains the major changes, the 1964 exception, and the frozen nuclear strategy, so it cannot be knocked down piece by piece.

Seminar question: is the 2014 reform, where Fravel concedes Xi's intervention was likely essential, the case that breaks the model? Fravel insists Beijing's intentions are not so inscrutable after all. They just took one scholar a whole book to decode. (S)

Chapter recall

  • What are Fravel's two conditions for a major change in China's military strategy?

    A significant shift in the conduct of warfare, as revealed in the last war involving a great power or its clients, and a united, stable Communist Party. F e24

  • Why is the timing of the 1980 change a puzzle for threat-based explanations?

    The Soviet Union had been China's main threat since the 1969 Zhenbao clash, yet the PLA did not adjust its strategy for more than a decade. F e28

  • What mislabel of early-1980s Chinese strategy does Fravel correct, including in his own work?

    "People's war under modern conditions." The CMC never used the label, and generals used the phrase from the late 1950s into the 1990s, across different strategies. F e23

  • In Fravel's framework, which condition is necessary and which is sufficient for major change?

    A shift in the conduct of warfare is the necessary condition; party unity is the sufficient condition that lets the military respond. F e57

  • What does Fravel predict when the conduct of warfare shifts but the party is divided?

    No change in strategy, major or minor, because disunity prevents the military from responding. F e57

  • Why are China's strategic guidelines unlikely to be signals to foreign or domestic audiences?

    They are issued internally and not announced publicly when adopted. F e65

  • Which form of warfare did the CCP use most before 1949?

    Mobile warfare by regular main force units; guerrilla warfare was prominent mainly in the strategic stalemate phase. F e80

  • When did Mao first use the term "people's war," and when did it catch on?

    April 1945, in a report to the Seventh Congress; it came into wide use only in 1958. F e116

  • Why could Mao blame others for the fifth encirclement defeat at Zunyi?

    He had been stripped of military responsibility at Ningdu in October 1932, so the defeat was not his. F e92

  • What did Zhukov tell Peng in May 1955, and how did Peng answer?

    Zhukov said a nuclear first strike would be decisive within minutes; Peng replied that Germany and Japan struck first and still lost. F e167

  • What happened to China's Soviet-style general staff structure?

    Adopted in 1954 and 1955 as an exact replica, it was dismantled in 1957 into three departments that lasted four decades. F e170

  • How much did Lin Biao's 1960 guideline change Peng's 1956 strategy?

    Very little: a new label and redeployment of about four divisions out of roughly one hundred, a minor change. F e175

  • What makes the 1964 guideline an anomaly among China's strategic guidelines?

    It was the only one started by the top party leader instead of senior officers, and it was a retrograde change that revived an old civil war concept. F e180

  • Where did Mao first reject the forward defense, and who heard it?

    At the Ming Tombs in June 1964, before the Politburo Standing Committee and regional party secretaries; Lin Biao was only briefed weeks later. F e202

  • Why did luring the enemy in deep survive the 1969 Soviet threat?

    The party and military splits of the Cultural Revolution blocked any change; China doubled down instead, and the strategy lasted until 1980. F e224

  • What two factors, beyond the Soviet threat, explain the timing of the 1980 change?

    Senior officers' judgment, drawn from the 1973 Arab-Israeli War, that warfare had changed, and the restoration of party unity once Deng beat Hua Guofeng. F e229

  • Why does Fravel reject the 1979 Vietnam war as the main cause?

    Su Yu's case was formed before the invasion, the 1980 planning sources barely mention the war, and a limited offensive said little about stopping a Soviet invasion. F e265

  • What was the "802" meeting?

    A September 1981 exercise at Zhangjiakou with more than 110,000 troops, the PLA's largest since 1949, meant to make the active defense guideline concrete. F e277

  • Why did China wait until January 1993 to act on Gulf War lessons it drew in 1991?

    Party disunity after Tiananmen and a politicized PLA kept the CMC from acting on General Staff reports until unity returned at the Fourteenth Party Congress in October 1992. F e306

  • What did Chinese analysts predict about the Gulf War in late 1990?

    A protracted war in which the coalition bogged down against Iraq's army and air power played only a marginal role. F e301

  • Why does Fravel reject emulation of the United States as the reason for the 1993 guideline?

    The problem in 1993 was how to fight under high-technology conditions with China's existing equipment; copying American methods was beside the point. F e325

  • How does Fravel date the unannounced 2014 strategic guideline?

    By counting phrases in the Liberation Army Daily: winning informatized local wars ran 94 times, 81 after mid-August 2014, and the new-situation phrase first appeared August 2, 2014, pointing to July. F e369

  • What does Fravel say drove the 2014 adjustment, since warfare had not shifted?

    The need for a framework to justify PLA reforms for joint operations, announced together with the strategy change at the November 2013 Third Plenum. F e371

  • What did PLA studies say about precision-guided munitions in the 2003 Iraq War?

    Their share of munitions rose from eight percent in the Gulf War to sixty-eight percent. F e361

  • Why has China's nuclear strategy stayed constant while its conventional strategy changed nine times?

    Top party leaders never delegated nuclear strategy to senior officers, so it stayed fixed on assured retaliation as Mao, Zhou, and Deng defined it. F e373

  • What happened to the Second Artillery's Nuclear Strategy Outline?

    Drafted over almost three years and circulated at the end of 1988, it was killed by Yang Shangkun, who recalled every copy because the Second Artillery lacked the authority to write it. F e416

  • What is Fravel's strongest evidence against technological determinism?

    The strategy stayed consistent for fifty years while the technology available to China changed dramatically. F e375

  • What two conditions does Fravel say produce a major change in China's military strategy?

    A significant shift in the conduct of warfare internationally and a united party leadership, which lets senior officers lead the change. F e426

  • Which change does Fravel's argument not explain, and why?

    1964, the only time the top leader, Mao, changed strategy himself, as part of his attack on revisionists in the party. F e427

  • What does Fravel say could block China's next change in strategy?

    Party disunity, possibly opened by Xi's anticorruption campaigns, even if the security environment gives a compelling reason to change. F e436
