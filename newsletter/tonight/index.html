Tonight's Reading · for tomorrow's seminar

Kitfield, Prodigal Soldiers: Twenty Years to Fix One War

Kitfield argues that the junior officers who lived through defeat in Vietnam stayed in uniform, rebuilt their services from the inside over two decades, and got their redemption in Desert Storm. He tells it through a handful of careers, chiefly Barry McCaffrey, Chuck Horner, Colin Powell, Jack Galvin, and Walt Boomer, followed from 1965 to 1993. The prologue sets the claim in one scene: McCaffrey watches Schwarzkopf unveil the left hook in November 1990 and thinks the Army will not fight a war of attrition or a limited war this time. Kitfield calls these men "prodigal sons all." (We note that the prodigal son in the parable did not have to pass a congressional budget review.)

The book argues against the public picture of a military frozen in its worst years, the one reinforced by the Iran rescue and Beirut. It also argues against McCaffrey's own nightmare plan: probe the border, escalate slowly, spare targets inside Iraq, and watch the protests grow. Desert Storm, in Kitfield's telling, is the war that went the other way because the officers had learned.

The course reads it for a different reason. The syllabus calls the "iron majors" arc alluring and incomplete, because it skips the collapsing Soviet Union and a coalition built for one limited objective. Kitfield half-concedes the point himself: moving VII Corps out of Germany was unthinkable until the Warsaw Pact fell, and the force rode a wave of Cold War spending. Read the book for the reforms, then keep asking how much of the result was reform and how much was timing. His epilogue already worries that Desert Storm was an "unsustainable peak." (S)

Read For

Six Things to Catch With a Pencil

  1. Prologue, pp. 19–24. McCaffrey's feared plan (slow escalation, nothing inside Iraq) against the plan Schwarzkopf actually briefs. The gap between them is the book's whole thesis.
  2. Chapter 16, pp. 166–167. Red Flag's arithmetic. Vietnam showed a pilot needed about ten combat missions to have a good chance of surviving, so the Air Force built the ten missions in the Nevada desert. More than thirty pilots died there in the first few years.
  3. Chapter 17, pp. 175–177. Creech's diagnosis of Tactical Air Command: half the planes not combat ready on a given day, 220 grounded for weeks, sorties falling 8 percent a year. Then his four pass/fail rules for commanders.
  4. Chapter 20, p. 199, and chapter 23, p. 228. Meyer tells Carter the Army is hollow; after Desert One, Meyer lists the failures it exposed: services that couldn't work together, broken equipment, bad communications, unclear command. Hold that list until Goldwater-Nichols (p. 297), which reads like the fix.
  5. Chapter 29, pp. 269–270. Weinberger's six tests for using force, and Powell's doubts about any doctrine that claims to fit every crisis.
  6. Chapter 38, p. 362, and chapter 42, pp. 385–386. Horner's first reaction to a Pentagon-built air plan was that Washington picking targets was how Vietnam went wrong. Then the F-117s: about 2 percent of combat sorties and more than 40 percent of the strategic, heavily defended targets.

Kitfield calls the Air Staff planner "Col. Jack Warden." The syllabus and Olsen, who comes up Thursday, both call him John Warden, so use John in seminar. (S)

People and Terms

The Cast, Without a Program

  1. Barry McCaffrey. Commander of the 24th Infantry Division in 1990 and the book's main thread, from Vietnam adviser to Powell's assistant in 1993 (pp. 14, 24, 427).
  2. Chuck Horner. Fighter pilot in Thailand in 1965, Red Flag flight lead in 1978, and Ninth Air Force commander who ran the Desert Storm air war (pp. 44, 166, 332).
  3. Bill Creech. Tactical Air Command commander who found TAC "overcentralized and thoroughly dispirited" and ran it on four rules: no lying, no integrity violations, no abuse of power, no public temper (pp. 175, 177).
  4. Shy Meyer. Army chief of staff who told Carter in 1979 that the Army was hollow and who pushed for a joint special operations force after Desert One (pp. 199, 228).
  5. Red Flag. Large air combat exercises at Nellis from 1975, flown against Aggressor pilots using Soviet tactics (pp. 166–167).
  6. Weinberger's six tests. Vital interests, enough force to win, clear objectives, forces sized to them, public support, and force only as a last resort (p. 269).
  7. Goldwater-Nichols (1986). Strengthened the chairman and the unified commanders, and made joint duty a requirement for general or flag rank (pp. 20, 297).
  8. Instant Thunder. The Pentagon-built air plan that Horner's planners reworked into Central Command's four-phase campaign against Iraqi centers of gravity (p. 362).
Bridge From Today · Haun to Kitfield

Haun Counted the Wins; Kitfield Throws the Parade

Haun's numbers come with you tonight. Of twenty-three modern American air campaigns since 1965, direct attack on enemy forces met its military objectives nine times in eleven, strategic bombing two in six, and interdiction failed in all but one of six. Haun also says the Air Force brought back battlefield air interdiction to support AirLand Battle and then listed no such missions on its Desert Storm tasking orders. Keep one question in mind while reading: when Kitfield credits airpower in Desert Storm, is he crediting the strategic campaign against Baghdad or the attacks on the Iraqi army in the field? Haun would want to know, and so will Olsen on Thursday. (S)

Congress returns today with a war-funding fight on its calendar. Punchbowl reports that Rep. Thomas Massie is expected to file a privileged resolution today to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, forcing a House vote by Wednesday, and that the House looks poised to pass it, which would send the case to a Senate trial. Government funding runs out December 11. Punchbowl says the odds of a shutdown have risen sharply since last Tuesday, partly because of public opposition to the Iran war, and Sen. Michael Bennet wants Senate Democrats to block any funding package that includes more money for it.

The electoral math explains the new mood. Electoral-vote.com counts 254 House seats called for Democrats, 162 for Republicans, and 19 still open, with Democrats likely to reach about 260. The national House vote came in near D+13, while final polling had it at D+8 to D+11. (The pollsters got the direction right and the size wrong.) Democrats hold at least 54 Senate seats, with Alaska and Mississippi still counting.

Kitfield's book has the framework for this. Weinberger's fifth test, in his November 1984 speech, asked for reasonable assurance that the American people would support a war before it started, because he feared another round of Vietnam's domestic turmoil. Abrams went further. He moved as much as 70 percent of the Army's combat service support into the reserves, so that no president could go to war without calling up the reserves, and with them the public. Kitfield adds that this did not guarantee support. It guaranteed that the argument about support would start early and loudly.

Opinion: the lesson for an officer is about timing. The first-order effect of skipping or misreading the support test is a war that starts on schedule. The second-order effect is that the argument comes back later through the funding calendar and the oversight committees. The third order is that the military carries a war whose funding is contested every few months. That pattern holds no matter which party is in power. Weinberger's speechwriters thought six tests were too many. Congress seems to be checking one of them by itself, against a calendar. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Kitfield, Prodigal Soldiers

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, prologue and chapters 1–2

Kitfield Serves Dessert First

Before you grade a reform, fix the baseline, then ask what else changed besides the reformers. Kitfield knows a good ending when he sees one, so he serves it first. The prologue flies Barry McCaffrey by Blackhawk on November 14, 1990, to hear Norman Schwarzkopf unveil the plan to liberate Kuwait. Chapters one and two rewind to 1965 for the "before" photo: brave officers in services built for some other war.

The prologue's plan sent two Army corps on a left hook of more than three hundred miles. McCaffrey, tears in his eyes, thought: "By God, we learned. We learned." Duty, Honor, Country rewinds to October 1966 near Dong Ha, where McCaffrey's South Vietnamese airborne battalion was surrounded. An AC-47 gunship firing eighteen thousand rounds a minute caught an enemy battalion in the open. Kitfield's verdict on that Army: "a confidence untempered by great introspection." Barnstormers puts Chuck Horner in Thailand in 1965, where Saigon changed the bomb loads for the first missile-site raid three times overnight. Six aircraft were lost attacking cardboard decoys. (Expensive cardboard.)

The weak seminar comment is "The Vietnam generation learned its lessons, and Desert Storm proves it." The strong version uses Kitfield against himself. His own prologue admits that pulling VII Corps out of Germany would have been unthinkable before the Warsaw Pact collapsed a year earlier, and that the force was riding a wave of Cold War spending. Opinion: that is a windfall, and windfalls teach nothing. Credit the learning, then name the luck.

The windfall version wins because you can follow the corps. The first effect of the Warsaw Pact's collapse was that Jack Galvin could ship VII Corps out of Germany, a move he called a reverse Reforger. The second was the plan itself, since Schwarzkopf had told Powell he could run the left hook only with an extra corps. The third came on February 27, when the White House wanted a cease-fire while VII Corps was still leading the destruction of the Republican Guard. Anyone who says a better plan alone won the war has to explain where the corps came from, since the plan was drawn around a corps only the end of the Cold War could lend.

Ask the room: if the Soviets had still pinned VII Corps in Germany, would the left hook exist? Redemption arcs are easier to draw when the other superpower stops returning calls. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 3–5

Count the Calendar, Lose the War

Whatever an institution counts, and however it rotates its people, becomes its real strategy. These chapters show all three services in 1966 and 1967 running personnel and measurement machines that served careers and the home front better than the battlefield.

In I Corps, Tom Draude learned how cheap cohesion is when a lance corporal thanked him for one thing: "You knew my name." The twelve-month individual rotation made even that hard, and the Pentagon liked it partly for quieting calls at home to bring the troops back. Mike Myatt, ordered twice straight into a fortified hamlet during Operation Oregon in March 1966, collected eleven dog tags. In Find, Fix, Destroy, after a gory fight in DePuy's Big Red One on August 25, 1966, a senior officer told Major Jack Galvin to add forty-five enemy dead from ground already counted. Galvin handed him the radio: "No, sir. You can call them in." He was fired the next day. Red River Rats has Chuck Horner, at Nellis in 1967, judging the rule that sent pilots home after one hundred missions as misguided as the war itself.

The weak seminar comment is "Body counts were a bad metric." Everybody agrees, so it earns nothing. The strong version names the mechanism: rotation rules, mission ceilings, and tallies bought domestic patience and fast promotions, and pilots paid over Hanoi, where the exchange ratio fell from four MiGs per loss to one loss for every two MiGs by 1967. Opinion: that answers the seminar's question about internal versus imposed change, since nobody had to impose these rules.

The mechanism version wins because the chain runs from Hanoi to Nevada. The first effect of the hundred-mission rule was churn, and Kitfield ties the MiGs' rising success to squadrons constantly diluted by new pilots. The second showed in the losses, as the MiG share of American aircraft lost climbed from 3 percent in 1966 to 22 percent in early 1968. The third came after the war, when the Red Baron reports concluded a pilot needed about ten combat missions for a fair chance of survival, so from 1975 the Air Force spent hundreds of millions of dollars on Red Flag to supply them before the next war. Blame the MiG-21 alone and you cannot explain why the service that took those losses spent its money buying back experience.

The check to run on any plan: for each metric and rotation rule, ask whom it serves, the enemy, the voters, or the résumé. If it serves the résumé, expect a major with a radio and a problem. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 6–8

The Army Ordered a Mirror, Then Classified It

An institution cannot fix what it will not say out loud, so the first reform is publishing the diagnosis. These chapters carry the Vietnam generation from the jungle to Walter Reed to the Pentagon, ending with the Army War College telling the chief of staff what was wrong. The chief listened, then locked the report away.

In Prodigal Soldier, Barry McCaffrey flew back to Vietnam in the fall of 1968 and was soon his brigade's senior company commander, the rest killed, wounded, or relieved, before machine-gun fire nearly took off his arm. In Wounds, most West Point classmates at his 1969 dinner party were leaving the Army. A Pitiless Folly opens with Jack Galvin refusing a dusk frontal assault; his brigade commander wrote that he "puts consideration for his men before that of the mission." In July 1970, Lieutenant Colonels Walt Ulmer and Mike Malone briefed the generals on interviews with about 450 officers: falsified reports, AWOL up 70 percent, and desertion up 144 percent from 1967 to 1970. A three-star half rose from his chair: "That's not the goddamn Army that I know!" Another told him that was the trouble.

The weak seminar comment is "Morale collapsed after Vietnam." The strong version says the reform agenda came from the Army's own War College in 1970: longer command tours, central selection boards, ethics courses, room for honest mistakes. Its first of thirty-one recommendations was to circulate the study itself. Westmoreland ordered it held close. Opinion: that is the cleanest test of any reform, and the Army flunked it. The stunned generals come to us through Ulmer and Malone's memory, so mind the source.

The inside version wins because the buried study kept working. The first effect of the lockup was estrangement among junior officers who heard about it through the grapevine, though Ulmer and Malone could still brief the findings at the military schools. The second showed up in those schools a few years later, when generals at a Leavenworth ethics seminar were stunned by students who reviled careerism, and Creighton Abrams went out and backed them. The third came a decade after the briefing, when Shy Meyer gave Ulmer a corps at Fort Hood and orders to build a center for leadership excellence on his study. So the claim that reform was forced on the Army from outside misses the diagnosis, which traveled through the Army's own classrooms and promotions.

Ask of any reform program: who gets to read the diagnosis? An Army that hides its own X-rays should not be surprised when the patient stops trusting the doctor. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 9–11

The Chart Never Runs to the Other Side of the Jeep

The farther a headquarters sits from the danger, the better its numbers look, so test the numbers where the danger lives. Kitfield's chapters 9 through 11 follow three officers between 1971 and 1973 who find the official picture and the ground no longer on speaking terms. Each answers by going to look.

In "Yankee Station," Cmdr. Stan Arthur, bombing roads and bridges North Vietnam rebuilt during the lull, marks his five hundredth combat mission by telling reporters, "I'm back to digging holes." In "Coming Home," Lt. Gen. Bill McCaffrey hears the Hamlet Evaluation Survey, briefed in Saigon, call hamlets holding more than 90 percent of the population pacified. His own test: have the local American adviser drive him to a village. If the adviser ran to the far side of the jeep, it wasn't pacified. In "Days of Rage," Lt. Col. Colin Powell, running a half-strength battalion in Korea, hands a known agitator named Odom his discharge papers: "Because you're leaving tonight."

The weak seminar comment is "Vietnam's metrics were misleading." The strong one sets the 90 percent pacification score beside a general's jeep test and a pilot bombing the same rebuilt roads. Opinion: Kitfield reports that the jeep test worked every time, which is the general grading his own homework. Note too what was forced on the Army: the Pentagon made it accept as much as 25 percent high school dropouts, while the cure in Korea came from inside, from division commander Hank Emerson down to Powell.

The strong version wins because the numbers aimed real weapons. First order, commanders used the survey to direct air strikes, river patrols, and sweeps. Second order, with control scattered from Hawaii to Omaha, B-52 raids ran out of sync with pacification, and advisers spent their days assessing damage from strikes they never wanted. Third order, in 1991 Col. Hal Hornburg still remembered Vietnam targets hit days late and pilots bombing empty jungle because Washington barred secondary targets. At Highway Six his Strike Eagles got their targets in the air from Joint STARS within ninety minutes, which we read as that lesson finally learned. Nobody can call bad metrics harmless paperwork after that.

Seminar question: if the best metric was watching where the adviser stood, what was the computer for? A chart has never once run to the other side of a jeep. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 12–14

Slogans Get Shot, Force Structure Sticks

A reform survives when it is wired into structure that later leaders cannot route around, and a reform sold by slogan dies with its salesman. Kitfield's chapters 12 through 14 show the Army of 1973 trying both. The slogan loses.

In "All Volunteer," Lt. Gen. George Forsythe's Project Volar puts beer machines in barracks, abolishes five A.M. reveille, and advertises that the Army wants to join you. Field officers greet his briefers with fury; their barracks already held drugs. Westmoreland puts the plan to his generals, gets silence, and Forsythe resigns. Kitfield's verdict: "So they shot the messenger." In "Last Act," Gen. Donn Starry tells Abrams the Army has thirteen divisions on paper, could field twelve, and only four are combat ready. Abrams gets Schlesinger to approve sixteen divisions from roughly 765,000 soldiers by moving as much as 70 percent of combat service support into the reserves. His line: "They're never going to take us to war again without calling up the reserves." In "Epiphany," Starry walks the Golan, where Israel lost the equivalent of three years of American tank production in just over two weeks, and concludes with Gen. William DePuy that the Army must win the first battle of the next war.

The weak seminar comment is "Abrams built Total Force to keep presidents honest." The strong one cites the 70 percent, plus the rule that keeping reserves in a theater beyond 180 days needs Congress. Opinion: the motive arrives secondhand, since Kitfield reports that no one close to Abrams doubted it, which is evidence about his friends. The volunteer Army was forced on the service by the White House; Total Force was Abrams's own unilateral bet.

The strong version wins because the 70 percent kept working after Abrams died. First order, reservists now manned the ports, airports, and rail stations, so any big deployment needed a call-up on practically the first day. Second order, in August 1990 Schwarzkopf told President Bush activation was a foregone conclusion, and the call-up put Congress on a 180 day clock and hastened the debate over Desert Shield. Third order, when the force was nearly doubled for an offensive option, Congress was fully engaged, as the system intended: Abrams reaching seventeen years past the grave. Kitfield adds that it guaranteed debate, never support. That chain answers the secondhand-motive objection: the structure forced the debate whatever Abrams privately meant.

Forsythe sold a feeling; Abrams sold a tripwire. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 15–17

Bloodless Battles Need Honest Referees

Realistic training works only when the scorekeeping is honest, because a fake war with fudged scores mostly teaches people to fudge. Kitfield's chapters 15 through 17 follow the Army and Air Force building simulated battlefields in the 1970s. The simulations found units far worse than their paperwork claimed.

In "Bloodless Battles," Brig. Gen. Paul Gorman tests SCOPES, a crude sight system in which soldiers wear numbers and get declared dead; in early runs most units were simply wiped out. A retired general's verdict: "You're teaching American soldiers to die!" In "Red Flag," more than thirty pilots die at Nellis in the first few years, and at Homestead an instructor was forced to graduate a student who had failed six straight missions. Capt. Ron Keys answers with his Dear Boss letter. In "Sky Blazer," Gen. Bill Creech takes Tactical Air Command in 1978 with half its planes not combat ready on any given day. He makes lying a no-appeal offense and breaks wing maintenance shops into squadron teams. A sergeant explains the gain: "when was the last time you washed a rental car?"

The weak seminar comment is "Red Flag made pilots better." The strong one says Red Flag exposed pilots who could not survive a realistic fight, the Dear Boss letter exposed the falsified records behind them, and Creech forced out a brigadier general who lied to him about a crash. Opinion: this was reform from below, pushed by a self-styled Fighter Pilot Mafia whose visionary, Moody Suter, kept his data on bar napkins. Kitfield's scenes read like those pilots' memories, so ask who is telling each story.

Each exposure had consequences. First order, the letter brought a TAC investigation of Homestead with Keys on the team, and the wing commander's name vanished from the brigadier generals list. Second order, Creech's reforms paid off: by the early 1980s TAC neared 85 percent mission capable, planes flew twenty-one sorties a month instead of eleven, and crashes fell from one per thirteen thousand flying hours to one per fifty thousand, despite tougher training. Third order, as the 1991 air war opened, F-117 wing commander Alton Whitley, a former Red Flag commander, saw his young pilots starting where he had needed harrowing combat missions to arrive. That answers the retired general: the Air Force that trained for real crashed about a quarter as often.

Seminar question: what is a simulator worth if the referee is the one being graded? Chuck Horner ends the chapter picking up a stray Coke can before Creech's visit and deciding they are all Creech clones. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 18–20

Can Do, Can't Deploy

A readiness report is worth exactly as much as the officer willing to say "no" in it. Kitfield's chapters 18 through 20 find the same rot in every service in 1979. Recovery starts when one chief stops saluting and describes the force he actually has.

In Borderline, Lt. Col. Barry McCaffrey patrols his West German barracks at night with a four-cell flashlight, ready to hit trouble between the eyes (a counseling technique we do not recommend). Nearly half the 1979 recruits rated CAT IV, the lowest category the military accepted. In Long Memories, the carrier Coral Sea flies eleven or twelve days in thirty under fuel rationing, with parts shortages grounding up to a third of its aircraft. In A Hollow Force, Gen. Shy Meyer tells Jimmy Carter at Camp David on November 24, 1979, that only four of ten active divisions at home can deploy overseas: "basically what we have is a hollow Army." Ordered by Army Secretary Clifford Alexander to retract the same warning given to Congress, Meyer offers to resign instead: "I took my oath to the Constitution." He keeps his job, which in Washington counts as a rout.

The weak seminar comment is "Carter starved the military." The strong one uses Kitfield's numbers: the budget grew somewhat in Carter's first three years, while inflation in the high teens, a falling dollar and fuel costs ate the buying power. Opinion: Meyer's real target was the can-do reflex, which he blamed for costing the country dearly in Vietnam.

The strong version wins on consequences. The first effect of the candor at Camp David, helped along by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan a month later, was a $16 billion increase and a five-year spending plan that jumped. The second came after Desert One, when Meyer kept warning of a hollow Army and Congress passed Carter's amended 1981 budget intact, raising military pay by roughly a third in two years and capping recruits from the lowest mental category at 25 percent. The third arrived in Reagan's first term, as the services restocked spare parts and scheduled war reserve munitions to grow about 40 percent, refilling the shelves the Coral Sea had been raiding. We infer that this chain answers the partisan version before anyone raises it, since the repair began on Carter's watch once a chief stopped telling the president what he wanted to hear.

The can-do motto is fine for a rifle platoon and dangerous in front of a commander in chief. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 21–23

Be All You Can Be, With the Parts You Can Find

When the machine is broken, a slicker pitch will not sell it and a braver plan will not save it. Kitfield's chapters 21 through 23 set two stories from 1980 side by side. Max Thurman fixes the Army's recruiting machine before he buys the jingle, and the hostage rescue sends a superb team into Iran on a joint machine nobody had fixed.

In All You Can Be, Maj. Gen. Max Thurman stops an ad screening to announce "There are no wimps in the Army!" He had accepted the consultants' warning that no ad would help unless the Army changed the product. So recruiting officers from captain up got thirty days to sign up a recruit personally or leave, and every recruiter carried a Mission Box quota card. Only then the song, commissioned on a Friday and screened on Monday. In Storm Clouds, on April 16, 1980, Eagle Claw needs six helicopters and sends eight, two of them grounded for parts. There is no full dress rehearsal and no weather briefing. In Desert One, a dust storm turns back two Marine crews, another helicopter loses a hydraulic pump, and Charlie Beckwith refuses to go with five: "Delta's going home." Minutes later a helicopter drifts into a fuel-laden C-130.

The weak seminar comment is "Desert One was bad luck with a dust storm." The strong one uses Meyer's own list of what failed: services unable to work as a team, equipment breakdowns, communications, unclear command, poor training. Opinion: Kitfield's verdicts usually arrive through a favored head. Carter took full blame on the radio, and the book simply replies that Meyer knew better. Ask whose recollection each scene rests on.

Washington later went after the items on Meyer's list. The first effect came shortly after, when Jones convened an independent study group of retired officers to examine the Joint Chiefs, and Meyer came away convinced the Pentagon needed a joint special operations force. The second surfaced two years later: that study found fewer than 2 percent of Joint Staff officers had any joint experience, and Jones took the finding to Congress. The third took until 1987, when Congress created Special Operations Command over the services' objections and gave its commander his own budget authority, the unified approach Meyer had pushed since Desert One. We infer the bad-luck reading collapses, since nobody writes a law against dust storms.

The Army could write a hit song in one weekend and could not keep eight helicopters flying in five months. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 24–26

Nobody Volunteers to Be Reformed

The services will share a training range long before they share power, so jointness that costs a service control has to be imposed from outside. In chapters 24 through 26, pilots from different services learn to fight as one team at Nellis, while the new Middle East command and the fight over the Joint Chiefs advance only when outsiders push.

In Central Command, Capt. Stan Arthur arrives at a trailer complex at MacDill as the Navy's man on the Rapid Deployment Joint Task Force. In Killer Accountants, Gen. Bill Creech finds the Red Flag syllabus rehearsing the first minute of World War III. Chuck Horner leads a pickup team of Air Force, Marine, Navy and Guard pilots and expects chaos. The mission commander explains: "Sir, we've all done this before at Red Flag." In A Parting Shot, Gen. David Jones tells the House Armed Services Committee in 1982 that the joint system he chairs is unworkable.

The weak seminar comment is "The military reformed itself after Vietnam." The strong one sorts the evidence. Red Flag grew from inside the Air Force. The services felt the Middle East command was foisted on them by civilians, and it gained momentum only after the Carter Doctrine and Desert One. Jones concluded fundamental change would have to be forced from outside. The House passed a modest reform bill by voice vote in October 1982; it died in John Tower's Senate.

The strong version wins because the outside push kept paying. The first effect was a sitting chairman breaking the chiefs' code of silence for the first time, as a junior House staffer, Archie Barrett, watched in disbelief. The second came in 1986, when Barrett helped hammer out Goldwater-Nichols, passed over furious service opposition, and the law overrode the ban on the joint transportation command Jones had wanted, which opened in 1987. The third came in 1990, when Schwarzkopf used that law's authority to make Horner the single air component commander, and Horner's control of every aircraft in theater let Army Apaches fire the opening shots on a key Iraqi radar. Opinion: Red Flag is weak proof of self-reform, since Nellis taught pilots to fly together and only a statute put one airman in charge of everyone's aircraft.

Retired Adm. Thomas Moorer told House investigators, "I say if it works, don't fix it." Desert One had already answered the first half of that sentence. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 27–29

Five Headquarters Between a Sniper and a Decision

A warning from the field moves only as fast as the slowest headquarters it passes through, so count the links before you blame the man at the bottom. Kitfield's chapters Semper Fi, Urgent Fury, and Politics cover the autumn of 1983 and its aftermath. A chain of command too long to hear its own Marines got them killed in Beirut, and services that could not talk to each other stumbled through Grenada. Washington answered with a checklist for going to war and a great deal of money.

In Semper Fi, a truck bomb flattened the Marine headquarters at the Beirut airport on October 23, 1983, and 241 Marines were dead or dying. When Marines warned that saluting VIP cars marked those cars for snipers, the fix was to salute every vehicle, so fire teams dashed out to salute their own dump trucks. Their objections climbed through five headquarters, from the ships offshore to the Pentagon. Adm. Robert Long's commission found "the chain of command was too long to be sensitive to threat changes." In Urgent Fury, a Marine colonel refused to fly Army Rangers to Grand Anse until Maj. Gen. Norman Schwarzkopf threatened a court-martial. In Politics, Caspar Weinberger announced six tests for using force on November 28, 1984, over speechwriters who wanted three.

The weak seminar comment is "Nobody can stop a suicide truck bomb." The strong one says the Marines saw it coming when the Lebanese stopped waving that summer, and their objections were watered down on the way up while the mission and the rules stayed put.

The strong version wins because the consequences run in a line. First, rules that never changed left the gate guards without loaded magazines on the morning of the attack. Next, the Long report revived a reform effort Kitfield calls moribund, and Rep. Bill Nichols, who had visited Beirut and chaired hearings on the bombing, decided the command structure had let those Marines down. Finally, in August 1990, Goldwater-Nichols made Colin Powell the principal uniformed adviser and gave Schwarzkopf unprecedented latitude in his theater, with no orders detouring through a faraway headquarters in Hawaii. Long did fault the colonel on the scene, but anyone who blames one colonel must explain why Congress then rebuilt the whole chain above him.

Opinion: Weinberger's tests screen the decision to go in. Beirut went in as peacekeeping, became a war, and nobody ran the tests again. The Marines at the whisky posts would have settled for one test, run twice. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 30–32

The Kitchen Staff Takes the Palace

An institution that will not reform itself gets reformed by whoever controls the agenda, and the reform lasts only if it rewrites who gets promoted. Kitfield's chapters Windmills and Reformers, A Palace Coup, and Reveille follow two kinds of change. Congress forced jointness on a Pentagon that fought it to the end. The Army, helped by better pay and a new recruiting pitch, rebuilt its ranks in a few years.

Windmills and Reformers opens past midnight in October 1984, as Sen. John Tower keeps Rep. Bill Nichols's Joint Chiefs reform at the bottom of the stack until time runs out. Rep. Ike Skelton called the leftover scraps the first such reform since 1958: "We've broken the dam." In A Palace Coup, Sen. John Warner kept stepping out and returning with fifteen amendments at a time, so Barry Goldwater took every pressure tactic on staffer Jim Locher's list and froze the committee. The Senate passed the bill 95 to 0 on May 7, 1986, and the law made joint duty a requirement for general or flag rank. In Reveille, recruits with a high school diploma jumped from 62 to 85 percent by 1983, and Barry McCaffrey went from barring reenlistment only for felonies to facing lawsuit threats for setting the bar too high.

The weak seminar comment is "Beirut and Grenada proved the services couldn't fight together, so Congress fixed it." The strong one says the failures supplied the case, Tower buried it anyway, and the bill moved only when Goldwater took the chair and held the committee hostage.

The chain runs all the way to the Gulf. First, the law made joint schooling and a joint tour the price of a star. Next, the services stopped treating joint duty as a home for deadwood, and McCaffrey's NATO posting was partly a product of the law. Finally, the promotion rule gave Chairman Colin Powell the pick of the litter for his Joint Staff, and Kitfield credits the same reforms with Chuck Horner's control of every aircraft in the Gulf theater. Anyone who says the services would have fixed themselves must explain why they fought the law furiously before they complied with it.

Opinion: watch whose chair the narrator sits in. Kitfield tells this story through Locher's eyes, and the opponents get the villain lines. Ask what Navy Secretary John Lehman's side would say. Generals lobbied Goldwater against the bill at receptions, and then a junior aide would find him in private to thank him. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 33–35

Lose in the Mojave, Guess Wrong in Europe

Nobody forecasts the next war, so build a force that rehearses the worst case and grades its own losses honestly. In Kitfield's telling, the Army learned to lose in the California desert, and a general who missed the end of the Cold War ran a rehearsal that turned out to be real.

At the National Training Center, Col. Jerrell Hamby's Soviet-style opposing force mauled roughly nine of every ten visiting units, so the Army made each unit critique itself, with no rankings. Col. Barry McCaffrey's light battalion of about six hundred men was wiped out in twenty minutes, and the lesson he took was that "an army learned far more from its defeats than it ever did from victory." In A Defining Struggle, Gen. Jack Galvin gets word that the Berlin gates swung open at 11:17 at night on November 9, 1989. In Burden of Duty, McCaffrey takes the 24th Infantry Division in June 1990, admits he totally missed the call on Europe, and tells his officers they will be at war within ninety days. In July, Central Command's exercise Internal Look played an Iraqi invasion, and the worst case and the real world were "virtually indistinguishable."

The weak seminar comment is "The Reagan buildup won the Cold War." The strong one checks the evidence. Soviet generals admired NATO's command network, a $25 billion a year habit, but Kitfield sets Afghanistan, Chernobyl, and the cost of matching the Strategic Defense Initiative beside it, calls the mix complex, and still credits the buildup.

The money can be traced. First, it bought a network that in early 1990 picked one stray Warsaw Pact MiG-23 out of the clutter over Central Europe and met it at the border. Next, Soviet generals kept coming back to that capability when explaining their unease. Finally, the VII Corps that had waited for the Warsaw Pact at the Fulda Gap left for the Gulf in what Galvin called a reverse Reforger, and we infer that only a Europe no longer under threat could spare it. The chain claims only what the money visibly bought, so nobody can dismiss it as a guess about Moscow's intentions.

Opinion: deterrence is a claim about what didn't happen, and here the witnesses are the deterred side talking to the winner. McCaffrey blew the forecast on Europe and got lucky on Iraq. The training was the part he never had to guess. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 36–38

The Org Chart Can't Shake Hands

A reform law can hand a commander authority. Whether his subordinates pool their forces depends on whether they trust each other. Kitfield's chapters on August and September 1990 show Goldwater-Nichols giving Colin Powell and Norman Schwarzkopf more power than any predecessor held. Then they show the results turning on who liked whom.

In A Gathering Storm, Powell half jokes that his most influential act as chairman was putting his own title on the letterhead. The joke has teeth: he now directs the 1,600-person Joint Staff without anyone's okay. Chuck Horner sketches Saudi air defense on a yellow pad, an old Copper Flag exercise with a new country penciled in. In A Line in the Sand, the Air Force promises five squadrons in a week and delivers more than double. In Band of Brothers, Schwarzkopf makes Horner the single air manager, and Walt Boomer hands over Marine air on a handshake. When a staff major grumbles, Boomer cuts him off: "General Horner and I have an understanding." Vice Admiral Hank Mauz, on a flagship that cannot receive the tasking order, proposes separate Navy and Air Force sectors. Horner hears Vietnam's route packages, threatens to resign, and turns his back. Air Force chief Mike Dugan talks to reporters about the secret plan, and Cheney fires him.

The weak seminar comment is "Goldwater-Nichols made Desert Storm joint." The strong version: the law made Horner air boss on paper, the Marines came aboard because Boomer trusted him, and the Navy stayed at arm's length partly because its admiral lived on a ship. Opinion: Kitfield narrates from inside Horner's and Boomer's heads, and Mauz gets one short paragraph of defense. Ask what the admiral remembers.

The strong version wins because the Navy's distance kept costing it after the shouting stopped. Horner buried the sector plan, so the Navy kept fighting from a ship that could not take the tasking order. By December the order was still being flown out to the fleet, an irritant Mauz handed to his successor, Stan Arthur. When the air war began, carriers were hand-carrying it aboard on computer discs, a Ranger planner had to ask whether H hour meant Greenwich or local time, and Arthur got his rules of engagement after his jets were airborne. The problem outlived Mauz, so it was more than Horner's temper. Horner figured Bill Creech had cloned them all. The clone who got fired was the one without a seat at the table. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 39–41

Landing Just to Say We Landed

When an option happens to serve the service proposing it, ask whether it serves the mission. Kitfield's chapters on the fall of 1990 show a clear objective doing double duty. Naming the Republican Guard as the target told commanders what to attack, and it gave them a yardstick for throwing out ideas that mostly served their own institutions.

In War Council, Schwarzkopf briefs twenty-two generals and admirals on November 14 at a former officers' club, the Desert Inn: an air blitz, Marines up the middle, and an Army left hook. Powell had already promised him the extra corps: "If we go to war, we will not do it halfway." In The Gulf, Walt Boomer has 32,000 Marines afloat, while at least three Iraqi divisions dig in along the coast and mine the beaches. A Corps that skips its signature mission, with a drawdown coming, will be asked why it exists. Brigadier General Tom Draude says the quiet part: landing "just to say we did a landing, then shame on us." Boomer agrees. In Peace on Earth, Major Marie Rossi's helicopter company turns out to have a male-only command slot. Rossi answers that the Army had better pull its enlisted women too. The matter is dropped.

The weak seminar comment is "The Marines skipped the landing because it was too dangerous." The strong version adds that the room feared for the Corps's future after the war, and the generals still voted against their own service. Opinion: it is a flattering scene, told by the men who look good in it. Now apply the test to the Navy, which floated its carriers into the cramped Gulf partly to stay relevant. Mission or marketing?

The decision kept paying out. Boomer first ruled out a landing for show. In February aboard the Blue Ridge, with the approaches mined and a landing that meant wrecking part of Kuwait City, he told Schwarzkopf he could win without it, so long as the Iraqis believed the Marines were coming ashore. On the ground, the Marines breached both mine belts with one man killed the first day and soon encircled Kuwait City. We infer that every Iraqi division watching the beaches was one not facing that breach; Kitfield never runs the count. By that inference, the landing that never came still did a day's work for the Corps. Draude figured his outburst might sink his career. Apparently honesty was the safe play that winter. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 42–44

The Hangar Queen Goes to War

A weapon's peacetime and wartime report cards are graded on different curves, so before you accept either verdict, ask what conditions produced it. Kitfield makes this point three times in three chapters, which for a journalist counts as a thesis. In all three, gear that took a beating in the press goes to the desert and performs. He treats combat as the only honest test, and he is half right.

In "Air War," F-117s flew thirty first-night sorties over Baghdad without a loss, and overall flew 2 percent of combat sorties against more than 40 percent of the strategic targets. In "Eve of Destruction," the Apache, which GAO told Congress in April 1990 could not perform all its missions half the time, reaches 100 percent readiness in Barry McCaffrey's 24th Division. Its wrench time rose from two and a half to twelve hours a day, and spare parts arrived at triple the normal rate. In "Ground War," the M-1 hits targets on the move from thirty-five hundred meters, outside the T-72's range, and Kitfield concludes that "some critical truths about weapons only reveal themselves on the battlefield."

The weak seminar comment is "Desert Storm proved the critics of the Reagan buildup wrong." The strong version: "The Apache hit full readiness because the Army had five months, little Iraqi air threat to its depots, and the whole fleet's spare parts to spend. A war in Europe would not grant five months, and GAO said so at the time." Opinion: Kitfield reports that caveat and then lets it drift away, a reporter's privilege and a seminar's opening.

The strong version also follows the surge past the photo op. First order, McCaffrey went to war with every Apache flying. Second order, those Apaches scouted inside Iraq for weeks, and at Rumaylah an Apache-supported flanking battalion helped the 24th destroy seven hundred Iraqi vehicles for one M-1 lost, while back home the Army emptied the parts bins and halved flying hours. Third order, by 1993 the Pentagon was fretting over sagging readiness in a force shrinking by at least a third; linking the two is our inference, since Kitfield never does. Because payoff and overdraft flow from one decision, anyone answering that the Apache worked concedes half our point.

A weapon that needs five months and the Army's whole parts pipeline is a fine weapon. It is also a fine budget request. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Kitfield, chapters 45–46 and epilogue

The Pendulum Swings Back After the Parade

Lessons bought in defeat are easiest to lose in victory, so a leader's last job after a win is guarding the standards the loss taught. The last two chapters show the Vietnam generation cashing its lessons in Kuwait; the epilogue shows them slipping by 1993. Barry McCaffrey's final challenge, in Kitfield's words, is to "sustain in victory the lessons that they had purchased at such cost in defeat."

In "Inferno," Mike Myatt hunts for flanks because at Ap Chinh An his Vietnam battalion lost half as many killed and wounded trying to take one hamlet as two Marine divisions lost liberating Kuwait. When Iraqi armor came out of the burning Al Burqan oil fields, Capt. Eddie Ray's nineteen light armored vehicles charged a force three times their size and destroyed fifty armored vehicles without losing a man. In "Partings," Chuck Horner and Hal Hornburg set Highway Six, where Joint STARS cued F-15Es to trap more than a thousand vehicles in a night, against Vietnam targets left unhit for days, and credit one name: Creech. Maj. Marie Rossi, a company commander leading by example, flew her Chinook into an unlit antenna after the cease-fire, the bill for a war called cheap. In the epilogue, a woman McCaffrey takes for a White House staffer tells him, "I don't talk to the military," and Horner fumes over boot prints on an airlift plane's ceiling, Creech's creed shrunk to seat-back size.

The weak seminar comment is "Vietnam taught the officers, and Desert Storm proved they learned." The strong version uses McCaffrey's own pendulum: the Army has righted itself only after debacles, from Kasserine Pass to Task Force Smith to the Ia Drang to Desert One, and Desert Storm was an unsustainable peak. Opinion: the epilogue is the most honest part of the book, because it admits the arc has no ending. Horner, who rates the air campaign decisive, even admits little reliable evidence that it destroyed any mobile Scud launcher.

The strong version wins because the victory's own consequences point downhill. First order, the post-Cold War restructuring would shrink the military by at least a third. Second order, by 1993 recruit quality was falling for the first time in a decade, eating at the all-volunteer turnaround that had been a key to the reforms. Third order, missions grew as the force shrank, with Somalia and perhaps Bosnia and Korea beckoning. We infer that fewer, weaker recruits facing more missions is how the pendulum finds its next Kasserine. Every documented link comes from the victors themselves, which makes "victory proves the lessons stuck" hard to say with a straight face.

If the services reform only after a disaster, what counts as a disaster when you have just won? Every generation of officers swears it will never forget. The boot prints suggest otherwise. (S)

Chapter recall

  • What did McCaffrey conclude when Schwarzkopf unveiled the left hook?

    That they would not fight a war of attrition or a limited war, and that the Army had learned. K 24

  • Besides limiting combat exposure, what advantage did the Pentagon see in the 365-day individual tour?

    It helped discourage pressure at home to bring the troops back. K 63

  • What was the first of the Ulmer and Malone study's thirty-one recommendations, and what did Westmoreland do with the study?

    Disseminate the study throughout the officer corps; Westmoreland ordered it held close while his staff studied the recommendations. K 111-112

  • How did Bill McCaffrey test whether a village was really pacified?

    He asked the local American adviser to drive him there; if the adviser ran to the far side of the jeep, it wasn't. The official survey meanwhile called more than 90 percent of the population pacified. K 120

  • What was the Golden Handshake?

    Abrams's deal with Schlesinger to grow the Army from thirteen to sixteen divisions without more people, by moving support missions into the reserves under Total Force. K 149

  • How did Navy and Air Force air-to-air results differ late in Vietnam?

    After Top Gun, the Navy scored twelve kills per loss from 1970 to 1973, while the Air Force, flying the same F-4, stayed near one loss for every two kills. K 162

  • What did Shy Meyer tell Jimmy Carter at Camp David in November 1979?

    Only four of the ten active divisions in the United States could deploy overseas in an emergency, and the Army was basically "hollow." K 199

  • What was Max Thurman's Mission Box?

    A card every recruiter carried at all times showing a monthly quota, broken out by high school graduates, upper mental categories, women and non-graduates, with no substitutions allowed. K 213

  • What did Horner's pickup Red Force at the RDJTF exercise reveal about Red Flag?

    Pilots from the Air Force, Marines, Navy and Air National Guard organized themselves at once because they had all done it before at Red Flag, giving the services a common way to fight. K 243–244

  • What did the Long Commission say was why the Beirut rules of engagement never changed?

    The chain of command was too long to be sensitive to threat changes. Marine objections ran from Beirut through the fleet, London, and Rogers in Belgium before reaching the Pentagon. K 262–263

  • How did Sen. John Tower kill the House's Joint Chiefs reform in the October 1984 conference?

    As conference chairman he controlled the agenda and kept putting the reform at the bottom of the stack until the final hours, leaving Nichols only token scraps. K 279

  • What rule changes made NTC after-action reviews work?

    A unit's own chain of command critiqued its performance, units were not rated or compared, and observer-controllers acted as mentors asking what commanders were thinking. K 311

  • What did Horner's "yellow pad" air defense plan for Saudi Arabia really amount to?

    A Copper Flag exercise from his old continental air defense command, with Saudi Arabia substituted for the United States. K 335

  • What did Tom Draude say about the proposed Marine amphibious landing?

    That landing just to prove they could, or to improve the Corps's political standing, would shame them. Boomer and Myatt agreed and dropped the idea of a landing for show. K 373

  • What share of Desert Storm combat sorties did the F-117 fly, and what share of strategic targets did it attack?

    About 2 percent of combat sorties, against more than 40 percent of the strategic and most heavily defended targets. K 386

  • What Vietnam battle shaped Mike Myatt's hunt for flanks in Kuwait?

    Ap Chinh An, where his battalion lost half as many killed and wounded trying to take one hamlet as two Marine divisions lost liberating Kuwait. K 411
