Tonight's Reading · last night before Thursday's seminar

Olsen, Strategic Air Power in Desert Storm: Right Target, Wrong Theory

Olsen argues that the strategic air campaign against Saddam Hussein's regime worked as a military operation and fell short as a political one. It degraded Iraqi war-making and put fog and friction into the regime's decisions. The heart of the plan, attacks on Saddam's power base, did not reach its hoped-for but unstated aim of changing the regime. His explanation is short: Washington did not understand the regime it was bombing. American intelligence counted divisions and tanks, and nobody mapped how Saddam actually held power.

The book argues against two camps at once. Against the triumphalists, it says paralysis did not topple anyone, and the Republican Guard withdrew in good order. Against Robert Pape and the critics, it says Saddam's survival does not prove the concept failed, because regime change was never a declared Coalition objective and so never got the intelligence or resources it needed. Olsen, a Norwegian airman, gives Warden full credit as an innovator and then grades the promise as overreach.

For this course, Olsen opens the "Unipolar Moment" section. Kitfield told you Desert Storm was redemption. Olsen says the campaign that most impressed the world also started a habit: in the decade after 1991, air power became the instrument of choice against stubborn regimes, partly because it seems to offer, in a phrase he borrows, "the pleasures of gratification without the burdens of commitment." Read it as the first test of whether bombing for political effect can deliver a political result. (S)

Bridge From This Morning · Clodfelter and Randolph to Olsen

A Small Demand and a Big One, Bombed at the Same Time

This morning's counterpoint concluded that Linebacker II changed Hanoi's schedule and left the terms about where October had them: Clodfelter himself calls the January 1973 changes from October "largely cosmetic," and Randolph shows Hanoi still met its minimum objectives. The lesson was that bombing works when the demand is small. Olsen hands you a war with two demands running at once. The declared one, leave Kuwait, was modest by comparison, and on February 11, 1991, Saddam told Primakov he would withdraw without economic or territorial compensation. The undeclared one, a new regime, was the largest demand there is, and all 43 of Iraq's top leaders were alive after March 1. (Same bombers, two report cards.)

Keep one question in mind while reading chapter 5: did the bombing succeed at the small demand and fail at the large one, and did Washington's rising terms after February 11 turn the small demand into a bigger one? (S)

John Thune, re-elected Republican leader on Tuesday with no challenger, told Punchbowl he expects Republican defections on high-profile votes over tariffs, Iran, and corruption. To colleagues blaming him for the midterms, he put a question: "Okay, tell me, what would you have done differently?" By his account, nobody said much. If Democrats reach 56 seats, four Republicans could end a filibuster.

The House is less tidy. Electoral-vote.com reports that a procedural vote on Rep. Thomas Massie's impeachment articles against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth failed 211 to 204 on Tuesday, partly because many Democrats were at an event elsewhere in the Capitol. Massie has moved on to a War Powers resolution to end the Iran war. House Republicans, meanwhile, chose Jim Jordan as their next leader, 106 to 73. (Showing up remains the most underrated whip operation in Washington.)

Olsen shows Congress arguing about air power in September 1990, before a bomb fell. As Cheney and Powell testified, Senator William Cohen warned that wiping out the Iraqis "within a matter of a couple of days with air power" was "sadly mistaken," and Senator John McCain said air assets could be used primarily to gain the objectives before a ground war. Days later the Air Force chief, General Michael Dugan, told reporters the cutting edge "would be in downtown Baghdad." The Washington Post headline read "Dugan Says Air Force Can Do It Alone," and the next day Cheney asked for his resignation. Olsen says the hearings, the Dugan affair, and journal articles produced a high degree of public debate about strategic air power, while the real plan stayed secret in the Black Hole.

Opinion: Congress's leverage over a war is largest before it starts and returns late, through money and oversight. First order, the 1990 hearings set public expectations for air power. Second order, a chief who said too much in public was gone in a day, and the Air Staff kept a lower profile for some time. Third order, Washington went to war with a plan the public had debated only in outline. Thune's question, what would you have done differently, is the one Olsen spends three hundred pages answering for 1991. His answer starts long before the first sortie. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Olsen, Strategic Air Power in Desert Storm

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, introduction

Grading a Campaign on the Aim Nobody Wrote Down

Judge a strategic air campaign against the aim it was really meant to achieve, even when nobody put that aim on paper. That is Olsen's opening move, and any planner can reuse it. His subject is the first of Desert Storm's four phases, the strategic air campaign against the Iraqi regime, and he gives it real credit: it degraded Iraqi war-making capability and induced fog and friction at the strategic level. The heart of the strategy, the attacks on Saddam Hussein's political power base, did not meet the "hoped for, but unstated, objective of changing the regime." His explanation is blunt. Washington did not understand the regime it was bombing.

The declared aims were tidy. On 8 August 1990, six days after Iraq invaded Kuwait, President George Bush named four national objectives, from Iraqi withdrawal to Gulf security. The UN Security Council passed 12 resolutions, ending with authority to use all means necessary if Iraq stayed past 15 January 1991. Regime change appears in none of it. Meanwhile, American intelligence cared more about order of battle than about how regimes are built, so planners got little insight into how Saddam held power. Olsen's fourth chapter fills that gap with five overlapping pillars, from the Ba'ath Party to the kinship complex, and names the center of gravity as the relationship between the sole leader and that power base. (Counting tanks is easier. It also answers a different question.)

The weak seminar comment is "Strategic bombing failed because Saddam stayed in power." The strong version: Olsen credits the campaign with degrading Iraqi war-making and imposing strategic fog and friction. It fell short against a regime-change aim that was never declared, and his fifth chapter names three drags on the concept: a thin grasp of the regime, inadequate intelligence and political restraints from Washington. Opinion: an unstated aim is the most comfortable kind a planner can have, since nobody gets fired for missing it. The question for any account of Instant Thunder or the Five Rings Model is short. Success at what, judged by whom?

The chain of effects makes the argument hard to answer. First order, planners aimed at a political system while their intelligence described a military one. Second order, the regime held, because Saddam's structure was far more complicated and resistant to precision bombing than assumed. Third order, Olsen concludes that the political outcome had little to do with sorties flown, buildings struck or tanks destroyed. Yet over the next decade air power became the instrument of choice in Deliberate Force, Desert Fox, Allied Force and Enduring Freedom, and we infer the untested assumption about regimes was carried into each of them. Politicians like the tool, Olsen notes, because it seems to offer "the pleasures of gratification without the burdens of commitment." Since he concedes the military success up front, the stock reply that the war was won anyway has nowhere to land.

Did the planners know what they were trying to break? A target list is a poor substitute for a theory of the enemy's politics. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 1, part 1

The Policy Was Working Because We Said So

A policy judged by belief instead of evidence will keep working right up until the tanks cross the border. Olsen's first chapter shows Washington courting Saddam Hussein for most of a decade and then misreading him in 1990. The Reagan tilt toward Iraq made realpolitik sense against Iran, he argues, but the Bush decision to keep improving relations was a serious misreading of Saddam's ambitions once the Iran war and the Cold War ended. Olsen borrows Bruce Jentleson's diagnosis, a perfect circle: "We know the policy is working because we believe it is the right policy."

The record is specific. On 26 February 1982 the Reagan administration took Iraq off the list of state sponsors of terrorism. In 1984 NSDD-99 authorized limited intelligence sharing. After the gassing of Halabja in March 1988, Secretary of State George Shultz called sanctions premature. On 2 October 1989 President Bush signed NSD-26, offering incentives for better Iraqi behavior, and a State Department memo soon recommended $1 billion in loan guarantees. Meanwhile Iraq owed some $80 billion and still kept over 1 million men in uniform. On 12 April 1990 Senator Robert Dole met Saddam in Mosul and reported a leader the United States could easily be in a position to influence. (Saddam, presumably, reached the same conclusion about Washington.)

The weak seminar comment is "Nobody saw it coming." The strong version: By late July 150,000 Iraqi troops sat on the border, all eight Republican Guard divisions were massed in one place, and the DIA climbed from Watchcon III to Watchcon I. The White House trusted Arab leaders who said Saddam had given his word, and the surprise was the scope of the invasion. Opinion: the most dangerous intelligence product is the one that confirms the policy. Saddam fed Ambassador April Glaspie false information and read her mild reply as a green light. She later admitted, "We never expected they would take all of Kuwait."

The chain runs straight into the air war. First order, years of credits, technology and intelligence sharing helped Iraq outlast Iran and emerge a major military power, broke and over-armed. Second order, Saddam was reluctant to demobilize an army that might stir unrest at home, and Kuwait's money and coastline looked like the answer, while Washington's faith in its policy muffled the alarms. Third order, oil revenue had let the regime pay for big army and security forces without taxing its people, and Olsen says the resulting regime proved so resilient that the 1991 air and ground campaign did not alter it. The United States, he adds dryly, helped enable that process. Because Olsen builds his case from Washington's own cables, alerts and reports, the excuse that nobody could have known has nowhere to stand.

Even the invasion misfired. The Emir escaped with some 25 family members, and Iraq could not find one senior Kuwaiti politician to front its puppet government, so it promoted a 31-year-old to colonel and put him in charge. Ten years of friendship had bought Washington plenty of access and very little understanding. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 1, part 2

On August 2, Saddam and the Pentagon Shared One Opinion

Read the plan on the shelf before you praise the plan that replaced it, because the shelf plan shows what the institution believed war was. Saddam Hussein misjudged almost everyone's reaction to his grab of Kuwait, yet his belief that air power could not decide a war did "coincide with overall US military thinking at the time." From 1973 on, Army-led doctrine cast air power as fire support for the ground commander, and the contingency plan for Iraq said so in writing.

In 1984 the Army and Air Force chiefs accepted the 31 Initiatives, which created battlefield air interdiction against targets the ground commander nominated, and Tactical Air Command, the keeper of air doctrine, agreed to the subordinate role. CENTCOM's OPLAN 1002-90 ran several hundred pages, assumed 30 days of warning, and covered where the soldiers would get their water. It never mentioned a distinct offensive air campaign. The exercise Internal Look, 23 to 28 July 1990, rehearsed a six-division Iraqi invasion, and Horner's margin note summed up his job: "Build a hose and point it where the ground commander sees that it's needed." Strikes on the homeland of RED (the exercise's code name for Iraq) were allowed only on the final two days.

The weak seminar comment is "Instant Thunder was the Air Force's plan all along." The strong version: the Air Force's own tactical command had signed up for a supporting role, and CENTCOM's war game held strikes into Iraq until the last hours, which Olsen reads as nervousness about bombing an Arab state plus a missing doctrine for conventional strategic attack. The strategic campaign broke with the plan, so ask who broke it. Opinion: a precedent sat on file. In April 1986 the Libya raid hit the Bab al-Aziziyya complex, Qaddafi's nerve center, to cut his means to operate whether or not he was home. Only two of nine planned F-111s bombed it, and Reagan's approval rating still rose to 70 per cent. (Leadership targeting with shaky precision and good polls: hold that thought for Baghdad.)

First order: doctrine written for a Soviet attack across the inter-German border defined air power as support for ground maneuver, and TAC accepted that. Second order: the Gulf plan and its July exercise inherited the idea, so when Iraq invaded, air power's planned role was to support arriving ground forces, with little thought for strategic targets in Iraq proper. Third order: on 4 August Horner briefed the National Security Council at Camp David on air options drawn from the unfinished plan, and four days later Schwarzkopf telephoned the Air Staff asking for a broader set of targets, opening the door for chapter 2's Checkmate planners. The evidence is the military's own paperwork, which pre-empts the comfortable claim that the strategic air campaign was simply doctrine finally applied.

Powell ordered the Gulf plans updated for an Iraqi threat in October 1989, so why did the plan still lack an offensive air campaign ten months later? It knew where every soldier would find water and had nothing to say about Baghdad. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 2, part 1

Warden's Plan Was Two Years Old When the Phone Rang

An idea wins in a crisis only if someone built it before the crisis and someone senior asks for it. Olsen argues that Instant Thunder was the product of two years of work in an Air Staff office called Checkmate, and that it reached the war because Colonel John Warden kept working after his boss told him to stop. On 8 August 1990 the customer phoned, and the product was on the shelf.

Warden arrived at that office bruised. As commander of the 36th Tactical Fighter Wing at Bitburg he issued some 90 initiatives of equal weight, from war plans to reserved parking and coat and tie after 16:00, and six months in General Kirk sent him to a Pentagon project. From July 1988 his directorate included Checkmate, a war-gaming division that reviews had said should be shut down. There he and David Deptula built the Five Rings: leadership at the center, then energy, infrastructure, population, and fielded forces, with the outer rings hit only as far as needed to expose the leadership. Lieutenant General James Adams told them to terminate the project. They declined. Then Schwarzkopf, with Horner swamped in Riyadh, called the Air Staff for a broader set of targets. General John Loh took the call, already knew a team was at work, and got a briefing within 24 hours; Schwarzkopf heard it on 10 August and Powell on 11 August. For Warden the call was heaven sent: "The man and the moment met and jumped as one."

The weak seminar comment is "Warden was a lone genius who saved the Air Force from itself." The strong version is that the idea had two years of staff work behind it and entered the war on an Army four-star's request, after his own staff could offer only AirLand operations. Opinion: getting fired was Warden's best professional education. Bitburg taught him to involve the people affected early, and in August he opened a big Pentagon briefing room and put everyone in on almost everything. Then run Olsen's check on the plan. Its fourth objective, rendering Saddam ineffective, began life as killing him, and Warden saw a high probability of a coup within a week, a belief Olsen says rested on intuition. On how paralysis becomes surrender, Warden "remained quite vague." General Loh, to his credit, felt uncomfortable with the optimism.

First order: Adams's order to stop was ignored, so the Five Rings and the Global Reach, Global Power white paper of June 1990 were alive in August. Second order: a concept of 84 targets over six to nine days reached the theater commander within two days of the call. Third order: the plan changed several times before execution, but its core stayed at the heart of the strategic air campaign that opened Desert Storm. That dated chronology pre-empts the claim that Instant Thunder was a hasty improvisation.

If the plan depended on someone in Baghdad overthrowing Saddam within a week, what was the plan for week two? Warden lost a wing partly over parking spaces and won an air campaign over the telephone. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 2, part 2

Everyone Said Yes to Instant Thunder. Nobody Bought the Same Plan.

When several bosses approve one plan, find out what each of them thinks he is buying, because the plan will be rebuilt around the answer of whoever holds the most power. In August 1990, Col. John Warden's Instant Thunder won fast approval from Gen. Norman Schwarzkopf and Gen. Colin Powell, and Olsen shows they approved it for reasons Warden did not share. Warden's Checkmate team thought it had sold an air campaign that could win the war by itself. Schwarzkopf and Powell thought they had bought a retaliation option for the President.

The briefings make the gap plain. Schwarzkopf had asked for retaliation options beyond the ones Lt. Gen. Charles Horner offered at Camp David on Aug. 4, and had not asked for a plan to throw Iraq out of Kuwait. He gave the 30-minute briefing on Aug. 10 his complete approval, partly because the Joint Staff had just judged that a ground campaign would cost unacceptably high casualties. Sixteen hours later Powell heard the same pitch, liked it, said he wanted Iraqi tanks left smoking all the way to Baghdad, and ordered the planning made joint and widened to hit ground forces. Tactical Air Command's operations director, Maj. Gen. Michael Ryan, Warden's Academy classmate, said he liked "everything after the last slide." By Aug. 17 the plan had 84 targets in ten categories and wide acceptance at the top, and Olsen says that acceptance came for different reasons.

The weak seminar comment is "Schwarzkopf bought Warden's plan." The strong version: Schwarzkopf bought a reprisal campaign, never believed air power could win a war alone, and while he sent Warden to Riyadh he had his own staff secretly draft a four-phase plan. Horner, on Aug. 20, heard Warden call him overly pessimistic about Iraqi tanks, got an apology, and called the briefing an academic study: "Now I need to turn that into reality." Olsen gives both memories of the question period: Horner says Warden could not answer, Warden says Horner never let him. (Kitfield told this war largely through Horner's eyes.) Opinion: Warden told Schwarzkopf how Nelson at Copenhagen put the telescope to his patched eye to avoid seeing the signal to break off. Horner proved he could also choose what not to see.

The second- and third-order effects show an approval given for one purpose getting spent on another. First order, Schwarzkopf and Powell approved Instant Thunder as retaliation. Second order, going joint grew the team from 20 to over 100, and the Aug. 17 briefing led Schwarzkopf to a four-phase strategy with Instant Thunder as Phase I. Third order, Horner kept the plan and three of its planners, sent Warden home, and handed the offensive to Brig. Gen. Buster Glosson, while Schwarzkopf and Powell grew reluctant to let air power be the only show as other options appeared. That chain answers anyone who says Warden was rejected: his concept went to war while its author went back to Washington.

Would Horner have kept anything had Schwarzkopf asked for a war winner? Warden got his plan into the war. He just had to watch it from Washington. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 3, part 1

Instant Thunder Survived by Changing Its Name

A plan survives a hostile new owner when its authors let the owner believe the plan is his. Olsen opens chapter 3 by arguing that the strategic air campaign hung on a very thin thread, and that individuals mattered far more than the organization's line diagrams. These pages show a few officers keeping Warden's ideas alive after Horner sent him home, by phone and unlabeled fax, while letting the theater planners feel the plan was theirs.

On Aug. 22, 1990, Horner put Brig. Gen. Buster Glosson in charge of a secret Special Planning Group with five days to produce an air tasking order. Glosson asked around for an offensive option and learned that nothing existed beyond defending Saudi Arabia, except Instant Thunder. He found it naive and thought "strategic" meant nuclear. Lt. Col. David Deptula converted him by late August, and said the planners now had "a 500-pound locomotive going in the right direction." (Small locomotive. It still pulled.) Deptula kept his contact with Warden secret, the faxes carried as little address as possible, and the trio hid the arrangement from Horner. Deptula called this the CENTAFisation of the plan. Horner banned the name Instant Thunder and the sign on the planners' door, then gave in on the word "strategic" with "All right. Just don't use it very often."

The weak seminar comment is "Horner killed Instant Thunder when he sent Warden home." The strong version: Schwarzkopf's Aug. 25 plan made Phase I a strategic air campaign based on Instant Thunder, the target list grew from 84 to 127 in four days, and the Sept. 2 operations order still aimed at the Iraqi leadership. Horner removed the label and kept the content. Ask of any planning history who was on the phone, since the org chart rarely says. (Kitfield credits Goldwater-Nichols for Horner's control; here the links run through back channels.) Opinion: it worked, at a price. The cell was nicknamed the Black Hole because people and information went in and never came out, and the back channel bred tension with the intelligence staff in Riyadh.

The chain of effects shows why the network mattered. First order, Checkmate fed the Black Hole intelligence that was sometimes unavailable in theater and sometimes arrived a day or two early. Second order, the plan won approval: Schwarzkopf called it the most impressive planning effort of his career, and after the Sept. 13 briefing he and Powell made the air campaign their primary response to further Iraqi offensive action. Third order, when Bush agreed on Oct. 30 to send Schwarzkopf's reinforcements, the objective grew to destroying the Iraqi armed forces, and the same network modeled 50 per cent attrition, letting Glosson promise it within 21 days. Schwarzkopf then ruled out a ground war until that figure was reached. The chain pre-empts the claim that the formal structure carried the plan: the structure had nothing offensive on the shelf until a few officers supplied it.

If officers hide their phone calls from the commander, who owns the plan? Instant Thunder lost its name, its author and its door sign, and still went to war. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 3, part 2

The Colonel Who Kept Turning Right

A plan that needs civilian approval needs its own campaign, and the briefing that wins approval can approve the rival plan too. Olsen follows John Warden running the political campaign in Washington while Buster Glosson and David Deptula run the operational one in Riyadh. Warden won his audience. He did not win the thing he most wanted, which was no ground war at all.

Warden's methods earned him a nickname, Right Turn, per David Halberstam: told no by a superior, he went one level higher, then another. Lt. Gen. James Adams called him uncontrollable. In early September, Warden and Secretary Donald Rice got Under Secretary Paul Wolfowitz down to Checkmate for a session they insisted was not a briefing. When Gen. Colin Powell objected to a second one in November, Warden went to Rice, Rice went to Cheney, and Cheney said yes. Cheney himself got the briefing on 6 November. Warden's arithmetic: military plans are five percent convincing soldiers and 95 percent convincing civilians. After Edward Luttwak phoned Sen. John Warner, Gen. Merrill McPeak was at Camp David on 1 December telling President Bush the war would take 37 days and cost at most 2,000 Iraqi civilians and 100 aircraft. Bush asked Brent Scowcroft who this McPeak was. (Fair question.)

The weak seminar comment is "Warden's lobbying sold Washington on air power." The strong one points to Glosson's briefing of 11 October 1990. Olsen calls it the moment the leadership accepted the strategic air campaign, and also the moment Schwarzkopf and Powell won acceptance for ground reinforcement. Once the shooting started, skipping a ground war became politically unacceptable. Powell had already called air-only strategies "designed to hope to win; they are not designed to win." Opinion: Warden lobbied for confidence while the decision rode on troops, and a corps arriving from Germany was going to be used. NSD 54, signed 15 January 1991, never mentioned needing ground forces. It did not need to.

Warden's salesmanship worked on belief more than on bombs. First order, Warden's end runs put the air plan in front of Wolfowitz, Cheney and, through McPeak, the President. Second order, Olsen suggests with labeled caution that the team's accumulated confidence in air power may have been one factor in Bush's decision to go to war. Third order, the plan that went to war had grown from 84 targets to 481, with as many rail and bridge targets as leadership targets, and only 740 of 43,123 strikes hit what Olsen calls the central nervous system. We infer that winning the audience did not mean owning the execution. The chain is hard to rebut because it measures Warden's influence on belief and on bombs separately, which answers fans and critics alike. Asked how the final plan differed from Instant Thunder, Horner replied, "How does a butterfly differ from a larva?"

Olsen calls Horner's decision to send Warden home on 20 August fortunate. Would the plan have survived Warden in Riyadh, or would Warden? Horner later compared the credit-seekers to 10,000 ants on a log, each sure he was steering. One ant kept walking to the front. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 4, part 1

Coup-Proofed Long Before the First Bomb

Before you aim at a regime's center of gravity, ask what that regime was built to survive. Olsen concedes that the official mission was to isolate and incapacitate, yet key Black Hole and Checkmate planners hoped the bombing would help remove Saddam Hussein by coup or popular revolt. The Coalition, he writes, did not understand how the regime worked, and its structure did not lend itself to collapse through air power. Since 1968 Saddam had made sure nobody could topple him, including the people the planners were counting on.

The regime was engineered against its own officers. Olsen describes five overlapping pillars: the state, the security and intelligence networks, the military, the Ba'ath Party and an informal kinship system, each a backup for the others. The Party grew from fewer than 1,000 members and supporters in 1968 to almost 2 million in 1990. On 16 July 1979, the day Saddam took the presidency, five of 22 senior Revolutionary Command Council members and some 50 senior Party officials were executed, and the surviving members performed the executions. The Party's Military Bureau controlled promotions and postings. On 10 December 1990, Saddam dismissed 12 senior officers and ordered some 16 executions for allegedly plotting. (Priorities.)

The weak seminar comment is "Saddam was an irrational madman, so decapitation was the obvious play." Olsen judges Saddam rational and calculating, a man who trusted nobody and misjudged his adversaries. When he asked his council about the UN withdrawal ultimatum, one member offered, "I saw you in my dream. You were victorious on your white horse." He did not grasp how bad the bombing was until the Russian envoy Yevgeny Primakov showed him pictures in mid February 1991. The strong comment: the planners were right that Saddam was the problem (his cousin Ali Hasan al-Majid put it as "Iraq is Saddam; Saddam is Iraq"), and that same centralization had already removed anyone who could replace him. Opinion: the target was right and the theory of collapse was wrong.

Coup-proofing cost Saddam an army and bought him a regime. First order, he put the Party in charge of the military and removed anyone who grew too popular. Second order, Olsen says this weakened Iraqi combat performance and left the army without combat intelligence, since Saddam feared informed subordinates, but it also reduced the chance that officers would turn on him. Organized opposition had been driven abroad by the 1980s, so when the leadership was most vulnerable, early in the campaign, nobody inside pushed. Third order, the regime survived the postwar uprising, and the rebels were massacred in the tens of thousands. The chain pre-empts the obvious rebuttal, more bombs on Baghdad: regime change needs a credible successor group, and the Party had made sure none existed. No sortie count manufactures one.

How do you grade an air campaign against an aim the planners never wrote down? Olsen notes that to Saddam, defeat in a foreign war probably mattered less than losing face before his own people. He lost Kuwait and kept Baghdad, which by his own accounting was the cheaper half. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 4, part 2

Five Rings, Seven Agencies, One Tribe

Before you bomb a regime's "leadership," draw the bodyguard system that keeps the leader alive, because that system is the real target and it never appears on an order-of-battle chart. Olsen maps Saddam Hussein's agencies and tribe in loving, slightly terrifying detail, then turns on the planners. When Instant Thunder was drafted, he writes, the airmen had "very limited understanding of the true centres of gravity."

The map is worth memorizing. Seven agencies reported straight to the Office of the Presidential Palace, bypassing the ministries of Interior and Defence, and their jobs overlapped on purpose so they would compete and spy on one another. The Amn al-Khass was a 5,000-man bodyguard; the Special Republican Guard ran to some 26,000 by 1990. Saddam used doubles, had dinner cooked in five or six places, and staged fake attacks on himself to see who would die for him. (Loyalty tests, Baghdad edition.) Even the air force was built mainly to prevent coups. Underneath sat the tribe of al-Bu Nasir, no more than 25,000 people, and an active core around Saddam of a few hundred. Since 1968, Olsen says, Iraq ran more or less as a family business.

The weak seminar comment is "Instant Thunder aimed at the leadership, so it understood the regime." The strong version notes that the plan never mentioned the Republican Guard, the Special Republican Guard, the Amn al-Khass, or the security network, the very things that linked the outer and inner rings of Warden's Five Rings model. The blind spot had causes. A 1988 memo from Secretary of State George Shultz barred US officials from contact with Iraqi opposition groups, and US military intelligence fixed on the order of battle and left the regime's construction alone. The Cockburns claim no analyst from State, CIA, DIA, or NSA had ever been inside Iraq. Opinion: a target list built from the order of battle will find the army every time and the regime almost never. Ask of any plan: which ring do the bodyguards live in?

The chain of effects explains why the regime survived. First order, Saddam split the armed forces between loyal non-Tikritis and Tikritis, so any coup needed groups that never worked together. Second order, the opposition was totally unprepared when its moment came in March 1991; the Shia uprising in the south gained momentum only after Iraqi radio was knocked off the air and replaced by BBC broadcasts. Third order, the clan network kept the elite in power through the Gulf War and the civil war that followed, and chapter 5 tests the air campaign against this unusually durable regime. The chain also pre-empts the excuse that nobody could have known. Olsen says the regime's overall architecture was nothing new, and his endnotes point to opposition sources and a book on the regime first printed in 1989.

Ordinary Iraqis, Olsen suggests, were "more afraid of Saddam Hussein than of the bombers." A precision bomb hits one building; the secret police typically kept a branch in every police station. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 5, part 1

Forty-Three Survivors and a Lot of Walkie-Talkies

Grade a strike on a regime's leadership by the friction it piles up in aggregate, and by how much of the plan the commanders actually let it fly. Toppling Saddam sat at the heart of Instant Thunder, yet it was never a declared aim, so Olsen cannot score it as a simple hit or miss. He measures it against Saddam's theory of the war and against what was really flown.

Saddam's theory was a ground war of blood. He told April Glaspie that American society could not accept 10,000 dead in one battle, and he ordered his commanders to sit still under the bombing because the ground battle would decide. The opening night of 17 January wrecked the premise: decoy drones baited the SAM radars, anti-radiation missiles killed them, and within three days Iraqi radar activity fell by over 90 percent. Deciding that sitting it out was failing, Saddam attacked al-Khafji on 29 January. His 3rd Corps commander asked to withdraw, was told to make it the mother of all battles, and replied, "Sir, the mother is killing her children." Iran ended up with 148 Iraqi aircraft. (A neighbourly favour, minus the returning part.)

The weak seminar comment is "Decapitation failed; Pape shows all 43 top Iraqi leaders were alive after 1 March." Olsen grants it and adds up the bill. Saddam met in farmhouses, his elite gave up cellular phones, the leadership fell back on walkie-talkies anyone could monitor, and food and clothes sat in Kuwait with no way to distribute them. Leadership and communications targets drew only 2 percent of all strikes. Weather cut Glosson to fewer than 100 Baghdad sorties by 28 January against 300 planned. Powell thought restriking only made the "rubble bounce." Opinion: you cannot fail Warden's promise on a test the inner ring only half took. Olsen adds an argument tool: the planners had made no provision for measuring their own effectiveness, and the intelligence community was built for battlefield assessment. Ask who was keeping score, and with what ruler?

The al-Firdos bunker shows how one constraint breeds the next. First order, the strike of 13 February killed some 300 people, and afterward Powell and Schwarzkopf approved every Baghdad strike, in effect a nine-day halt; F-117s hit 25 targets in metropolitan Baghdad in the two weeks before and five in the two weeks after. Second order, 90 percent of the air effort went to the Kuwaiti theatre, and by mid February the division commanders pressed Schwarzkopf into shifting bombs from the Republican Guard to frontline conscripts (we infer the two pressures fed each other). Third order, the Guard escaped largely intact and helped crush the southern uprising after the cease-fire, which Olsen says undermined the strategic campaign. That chain blocks the easy verdict that the theory simply failed: Schwarzkopf named the Guard a centre of gravity, then let it go untargeted in a systematic way. (Haun, recall, credits direct attack on the fielded army; here the best of that army got away.)

Saddam lost his phones, his radars, and the use of his air force, and stayed alive. The Guard went home largely intact. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, chapter 5, part 2

Saddam Haggled Over the Rug While Washington Raised the Price

When coercion starts to work, check whether the side doing the coercing still wants it to. Olsen applies that test to the end of the Gulf War. By mid-February 1991, he argues, the bombing had pushed Saddam Hussein toward leaving Kuwait, and by then Washington no longer wanted the war to end that way. The air planners came close to a win by coercion, and the Army got its ground war anyway.

The evidence runs on a tight calendar. Saddam toured his troops in Kuwait on 23 January and Basra four days later and saw the bombing of his formations getting worse. Until early February he believed he could sit out the bombing and then inflict heavy casualties; after that he looked for a way out. On 11 February the Soviet envoy Yevgeni Primakov met him in Baghdad, and Saddam agreed to withdraw without economic or territorial compensation. While Primakov was there, Cheney and Powell were in Riyadh reviewing the ground plan, and Washington was by now demanding that the Iraqi Army leave its tanks and artillery behind. Olsen gives two motives: an ending without the massed ground forces might have undercut the case for a large active Army, and it would have handed Moscow much credit. Warden's diagnosis of the Iraqis: "They were still trying to sell rugs."

Seminar question 6, why the war ended when it did, has two halves, and most answers grade only Iraq's. The weak seminar comment is "Saddam was stubborn, so the ground war was necessary." The strong version: Saddam was slow and lost valuable days bargaining, but the price rose while he haggled, and Olsen concludes Washington would not let the war end without ground forces seizing territory. Opinion: moving the goalposts is also a bargaining style; it just gets better press. (Haun would like that Saddam's change of heart followed a look at his battered troops.)

The second- and third-order effects show why the argument holds. First order, Washington's tougher terms meant Saddam's offer fell short, so he bargained. Second order, Iraq lost several days, and when it thought a Moscow deal was done its forces set Kuwait's oilfields on fire, which helped Washington argue for a ground war; even after that war began, Saddam took two more days to accept unconditional withdrawal. Third order, the divisions he had kept in the north and outside Baghdad saved the regime: those in the north slowed the Kurdish revolt, and those sent south crushed the Shia uprising, and the Coalition chose not to back the revolt for fear of a political vacuum. Built on Iraqi witnesses and Washington's own words, the chain means anyone claiming Saddam never meant to leave has to explain away Primakov.

Olsen's verdict on the strategic campaign itself is modest. It left the Iraqi leadership largely ineffective, came nowhere near changing the regime on its own, and used 2 per cent of air strikes, which he calls worthwhile. Overthrow was never a declared aim, so grading the campaign on it is grading a test nobody set. Rug merchants, it turns out, sit on both sides of the table. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Olsen, conclusions

Instant Thunder Had a Target List and No Successor

A plan to strike the enemy's leadership owes an answer to one question: who governs afterward, and will that person meet your terms? Olsen's conclusions say Instant Thunder never answered it, and neither did Washington. He still grades the concept a success in relative terms. It injected fog, friction and uncertainty into Iraqi decision-making and kept Saddam Hussein from decisive action, while leaving the regime free to secure its own survival.

The record shows where the theory ran out. Instant Thunder, as briefed between 9 and 20 August 1990, was bold and imaginative and out of step with what was politically acceptable and operationally attainable. Forty-three days of bombing against several hundred targets failed to collapse the regime's command and control; the Republican Guard withdrew in good order and the regime put down two revolts. The planners knew little about Iraq beyond the locations of buildings and communications, and they ignored the security agencies guarding the regime. Powell told the Senate in December 1990: "Such strategies are designed to hope to win; they are not designed to win." Then there was the successor: any new leader would have to look strong at home, so none could afford the concessions demanded.

The weak seminar comment on question 2 is "Warden promised paralysis, Saddam survived, so strategic airpower failed." That is Robert Pape's case, and Olsen answers it: survival does not prove paralysis was ineffectual, and regime change was never a Coalition objective, so the intelligence and resources for it never showed up. The strong comment concedes the overreach. Warden at first thought Instant Thunder alone could compel withdrawal, and Peter Faber judged that, for Warden, the elegance of the argument outran reality. Opinion: Warden's promise failed and his idea survived, largely because Deptula kept it alive in late August 1990 and shaped it into something Horner, Schwarzkopf and Powell would accept. Even General Michael Ryan, who opposed it in 1990, later called massive force from the opening moment classic doctrine. Converts are always the loudest.

The second- and third-order effects show the failure was political. First order, the planners hit buildings and communication lines with no theory of who would replace Saddam. Second order, when they raised war termination, senior military leaders replied it was not their job; Schwarzkopf's chief foreign policy advisor said the administration "never did have a plan to terminate the war," and Schwarzkopf dictated the cease-fire without State Department instructions. Third order, Bush urged Iraqis in late February 1991 to take matters into their own hands and did not act, and by the time they rose the bombing had stopped. Saddam lived to fight another day, Olsen notes. The chain pre-empts the claim that more bombing would have done it: Iraqi troops interviewed for the book said more of the same on the inner ring would not have mattered much.

Olsen's fix is homework: study the enemy's culture and how its value and power systems work, because strategic paralysis is no surgical exercise with clean cause and effect. Saddam called it the Mother of all Battles, not of all Wars, and Olsen suspects he understood the difference better than Washington did. (S)

Chapter recall

  • According to Olsen, what did the strategic air campaign achieve, and what did it miss?

    It degraded Iraqi war-making capability and induced strategic fog and friction, but the attacks on Saddam's power base did not meet the hoped-for, unstated objective of changing the regime. O 1

  • Why, in Olsen's view, did planners misjudge the Iraqi regime?

    US intelligence focused on order-of-battle assessments over the nature of political regimes, so planners had little insight into Saddam's political system. O 1

  • What does Olsen name as the Iraqi regime's center of gravity?

    The relationship between Saddam Hussein, the sole leader, and his personal and political power base. O 4

  • What tautology did Jentleson find in the Bush administration's Iraq policy?

    We know the policy is working because we believe it is the right policy, and we believe it is right because we know it is working. O 32

  • What, according to Olsen, actually surprised Washington in August 1990?

    The scope of the invasion. The White House had trusted Arab leaders who argued a full occupation of Kuwait was not conceivable. O 31

  • How did oil shape the Iraqi regime's resilience?

    Oil revenue let it pay for large army and security forces without taxes or foreign subsidies, making the state independent of society. O 18

  • What did CENTCOM's OPLAN 1002-90 say about an offensive air campaign?

    Nothing distinct. It was mainly a deployment plan of several hundred pages that assumed 30 days of warning. O 47

  • What was Horner's margin note during Internal Look in July 1990?

    Build a hose and point it where the ground commander sees that it's needed. Strikes on Iraq (RED) came only in the final two days. O 48

  • How did the 1986 Libya raid preview leadership targeting?

    It struck Qaddafi's Bab al-Aziziyya complex to cut his means to operate, but only two of nine planned F-111s hit it. O 44

  • What are Warden's Five Rings, from the center out?

    Leadership, energy, infrastructure, population, and fielded forces. The outer rings were to be hit only as far as needed to expose the leadership. O 83

  • Who called the Air Staff on 8 August 1990, and what did he want?

    Schwarzkopf, asking General Loh for a broader set of targets than his AirLand plan offered. Warden briefed Loh within 24 hours. O 92

  • What did Warden learn from being removed at Bitburg?

    To involve the people affected by new ideas early, which became his open planning for Instant Thunder. O 79

  • What did Schwarzkopf think he was getting from Instant Thunder in August 1990, and what did Warden think he was selling?

    Schwarzkopf (and Powell) wanted a retaliation option to give the President; Warden believed he was offering an air campaign that could win on its own. Olsen says acceptance was widespread but came for different reasons. O 106

  • What did Powell add to the plan on 11 August 1990?

    He wanted Iraqi ground forces destroyed as a political signal, and he ordered the planning made joint and widened to target ground forces. O 102

  • How did Horner resolve his clash with Warden after the 20 August briefing?

    He kept the plan and three planners (Deptula, Harvey and Stanfill), sent Warden home, and gave the offensive planning to Glosson. O 111

  • What was the "CENTAFisation" of the plan?

    Warden and Deptula's effort to make the Black Hole staff in Riyadh feel the plan was their own, while Deptula secretly kept Checkmate feeding it. O 130

  • What did Glosson find when he asked for an existing offensive option in August 1990?

    Nothing existed beyond the defence of Saudi Arabia, except Instant Thunder. O 128

  • What condition did Schwarzkopf set before starting a ground war?

    He would not engage in a ground war until air power had achieved 50 per cent attrition of Iraqi forces. O 147

  • Which briefing does Olsen name as the moment the strategic air campaign was accepted, and what else did it settle?

    Glosson's 11 October 1990 briefing. It also gave Schwarzkopf and Powell acceptance for ground reinforcement, so a ground war became politically unavoidable once the war began. O 166

  • What was Warden's nickname, and why?

    Right Turn, per Halberstam: when a superior rejected an idea, he went to the next higher level. O 150

  • How many Desert Storm strikes hit the "central nervous system"?

    Only 740 of 43,123. O 167

  • What were the five pillars of Saddam's power in Olsen's model?

    The formal state, the security and intelligence networks, the military, the Ba'ath Party and an informal kinship system, overlapping to give the regime redundancy. O 176

  • Why did letting the Party run the military help Saddam survive Desert Storm?

    It weakened combat performance but reduced the chance that officers would turn on him, and it helped him survive the uprising that followed. O 193

  • Who planned the full invasion of Kuwait with Saddam?

    Only three: Hussein Kamil, Ali Hasan al-Majid and Republican Guard commander Iyad al-Rawi. The RCC and Regional Command were not told. O 198

  • What did Olsen say Instant Thunder left out of its picture of the Iraqi regime?

    It never mentioned the Republican Guard, Special Republican Guard, Amn al-Khass, or the security network that linked the Five Rings model's outer and inner rings. O 224

  • Why did US planners know so little about Saddam's inner workings?

    Shultz's 1988 memo barred contact with Iraqi opposition groups, and US military intelligence focused on the order of battle. O 214

  • How big was the tribal core that ran Iraq?

    Al-Bu Nasir numbered no more than 25,000, and the active core in politics and security was a few hundred people. O 218

  • What did Pape's count of Iraqi leaders show, and how does Olsen answer it?

    All 43 top leaders were alive after 1 March; Olsen counts the aggregate friction instead, from walkie-talkies to food stuck in Kuwait. O 259

  • What changed in Baghdad targeting after al-Firdos?

    Powell and Schwarzkopf approved every Baghdad strike, and F-117 targets there fell from 25 to five across two-week windows. O 253

  • Why did the Republican Guard escape largely intact?

    Division commanders pressed Schwarzkopf to shift bombing to frontline conscripts in mid February, so the Guard was never targeted systematically. O 255

  • What did Saddam agree to when Primakov visited Baghdad on 11 February 1991, and what was Washington demanding by then?

    He agreed to withdraw from Kuwait without economic or territorial compensation; Washington was by then demanding the Iraqi Army leave its tanks and artillery behind. O 267

  • What two reasons does Olsen give for Washington wanting a ground war?

    Ending without the massed ground forces might have undercut the rationale for a large active Army, and it would have given the Soviet Union much credit. O 268

  • What share of air strikes went to the inner-ring leadership target sets, and how does Olsen judge it?

    About 2 per cent; far from getting Saddam, reasonably successful at strategic incapacitation, and worthwhile. O 265

  • What did Powell tell the Senate about strategies like Instant Thunder in December 1990?

    That they were designed to hope to win, not designed to win; the planners had no idea how the regime would fall beyond assuming paralysis would do it. O 291

  • Who kept Instant Thunder alive in theater in late August 1990, according to Olsen?

    Deptula, who helped form it, joined the Black Hole cell, and adapted it into a plan Horner, Schwarzkopf and Powell would accept; Glosson became its advocate. O 289

  • Why can no new Iraqi leader simply accept Coalition terms, in Olsen's view?

    Face-saving: a successor would have to look strong before his own people, so he could not afford the concessions demanded. O 291
