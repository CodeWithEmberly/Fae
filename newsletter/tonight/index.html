Next Reading · for Monday, November 30

Sekirin, Rise of the Machines: The Book Arrives Before the Text Does

The next seminar is Monday, November 30, after the Thanksgiving break, and its book is Illya Sekirin's Rise of the Machines: Drone Warfare in the Russia-Ukraine War, edited by Andrew Simms. Tonight's page has a gap: the book is not in the 628 folder, on Drive, or on this computer, so we have not read a page of it. Everything below about the book's argument comes from the publisher and from reviewers, and it is labeled that way. Get the PDF into the 628 folder this weekend and the next afternoon run will build the chapter lessons from the text.

The argument, as the publisher states it: unmanned systems have changed war at the tactical, operational, and strategic levels. FPV attack drones, reconnaissance drones, electronic warfare, naval drones, and autonomous systems now watch everything, and tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft struggle to survive under that watch. Ukraine's experience, the publisher says, is a blueprint for how armies should reorganize, buy, train, and fight. The case studies named are the early fighting of 2022, Bakhmut, Avdiivka, the Kursk offensive, and the defense of Southern Donbas.

Its target is the kind of force this course has studied for eight weeks: armies built around tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft. The syllabus calls it "a bold prediction" about the character of war and asks whether the character has changed as much as the author claims. That question matters for 628 because every technology in this course arrived with the same claim. Sputnik, the laser-guided bomb, stealth, and the Predator each came with a book announcing a new kind of war. Our working view, before reading: Sekirin will win the tactical argument easily and will have to fight for the strategic one, because the war he describes is, by the syllabus's own account, a stalemate. (S)

The World Around the Book

The Author Helped Build the Branch He Is Writing About

Illya Sekirin is an advocate first and a historian second, and that is the most useful thing to know before you open the book. He is a Ukrainian-born, naturalized Canadian who volunteered to fight in March 2022 and was trained as a drone operator. He served at the front, worked as a translator for the Ukrainian General Staff, and became an unofficial adviser to several members of the Stavka, the high command that President Zelensky chairs. His publisher says he wrote a paper that informed Zelensky's decision to create the Unmanned Systems Forces as a separate branch. (Few authors get to review their own reorganization.)

The axe is documented, so we can name it. A local newspaper interview with his editor reports that Sekirin believes Ukraine could have won earlier had his drone "blitz" been fully carried out, and that he now sees a costly stalemate and favors a negotiated settlement. That is the claim of a man who thinks his idea was right and was under-used. Read every judgment about Ukrainian commanders with that in mind. Our take: an insider's access is the book's strength, and an insider's stake is its blind spot.

The editor matters too. Andrew Simms spent twelve years at the British Army's Soldier magazine, rising to editor-in-chief, and now edits the British Army Review for the Army's historical analysis center at Sandhurst. Sekirin first reached him by submitting articles to that journal. Expect a book shaped for a Western army audience: lessons, recommendations, and a warning.

The moment is a war still being fought. Helion lists publication on January 30, 2026, and its U.S. distributor lists April 16, 2026, so the book went to press with no ending to write. The publisher's description pitches it against an orthodoxy: armies built around tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft, which it says now struggle under constant observation and cheap, expendable attack. It also sets the war inside a wider contest with China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia, which it calls an "axis of upheaval," and argues the West must adapt or face strategic obsolescence. Those are the publisher's words for the argument. We will judge the argument itself once the text is on the desk.

The reception is thin and friendly. The publisher's pages quote the Journal of the Air Force Historical Foundation, which called it "a timely and ambitious contribution" to the literature on unmanned systems. The only independent review we could open, by Robert McKie on a New Zealand army logistics history blog in April 2026, strongly recommends it and reads it as a book about logistics: when everything on the battlefield can be seen, the rear area stops being safe. McKie's only caveat is that it offers no neat answers. We found no scholarly review and no critical one, so the critical reception is undocumented.

Our verdict: the friendly reviews are probably right that the book is valuable, and they have not yet tested its biggest claim. "The character of war has changed" is the sentence every technology enthusiast in this course has written, from the space race to Lambeth. The seminar question is whether Sekirin proves it or only reports it. (Mark the pages where he counts results, and the pages where he predicts them.) (S)

Read For

Five Things to Catch Him Doing

  1. Counting versus predicting. Mark every passage where he gives a number for what drones did, and every passage where he says what they will do. The seminar's first question lives in the gap between the two.
  2. The stalemate problem. The syllabus says the war has outlasted the First World War as a stalemate. Watch whether he explains why a revolution in weapons produced a frozen front.
  3. His own role. He helped create the branch he writes about. Watch how he treats commanders who resisted his ideas, and whether he cites anyone who disagrees with him.
  4. The deep strikes. The syllabus says Ukraine now strikes commercial infrastructure deep inside Russia. Read it next to Clodfelter and Olsen: what result does he claim, and how does he measure it?
  5. Cheap against expensive. The publisher stresses cheap, expendable attack. Track whether he counts the cost of the defense too: jammers, interceptors, and the people who fly the drones.
Seminar Questions · Monday, November 30

Four Questions and Where the Answers Will Live

These come from the syllabus page for Seminar 17, "The Future Character of War?" Chapter locations follow once the text is on disk; for now, here is where each answer will come from.

  1. Has the character of war changed to the extent the author claims? His introduction and conclusion for the claim; his case studies for the proof. Lambeth and McDougall are the best rival witnesses.
  2. If paired with AI for autonomous strikes, can drone technology alter the nature of war? His chapter on autonomy, if there is one. Note the shift in the question from "character" to "nature," which is Clausewitz's line between how war is fought and what war is.
  3. What other technological advances should be compared to Ukraine's drones? The course itself: the jet over MiG Alley in Zhang, the laser-guided bomb in Randolph, stealth and precision in Lambeth, the Predator in Fuller.
  4. What opportunities or dangers does this pose for strategists? His recommendations to Western armies, and the publisher's "axis of upheaval" framing.

Opinion: prepare question two. The others ask about drones; that one asks about war, and the room will need someone who knows the difference. (S)

Bridge From Today · Wasser to Sekirin

An F-15E, a Missile, and a Drone Worth Rather Less

Wasser's report opens with the drone war arriving early. On June 8, 2017, near the al-Tanf garrison in Syria, F-15E Strike Eagles found an Iranian-made Shahed 129 armed with two Hellfire-like missiles, and after it turned hot one of the pilots shot it down with an AIM-120C. Twelve days later, another F-15E shot down a second Shahed 129. ISIS itself flew only small drones for reconnaissance and attacks, so the coalition's air defense worried far more about Russia. In 2017, then, the drone was a nuisance that a fighter could swat, one expensive missile at a time.

Sekirin writes about the war where that arithmetic broke. If drones come by the thousand, a fighter and a missile per drone is a way to lose slowly. (The Strike Eagle crews at al-Tanf were not, to be fair, solving for 2026.) Keep one question in mind while reading: what did Wasser's coalition have that Ukraine does not, and is it the airspace, the budget, or the enemy's restraint? (S)

Political Strategy × Sekirin · via Punchbowl News AM and electoral-vote.com, November 19

Three Hundred Fifty Billion Dollars and No Shopping List

The Pentagon's $350 billion plus-up is all but dead, and the administration is blaming Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Punchbowl News reported this morning. Senate Republicans sidelined the defense reconciliation package days ago. The Pentagon still says it wants the full amount by year's end and warns that without it the supplier base will suffer and adversaries could outpace the United States in next-generation technology. Republicans in both chambers are pushing back. Sen. Mitch McConnell said reconciliation "should never have been a substitute" for regular defense spending, and House Armed Services Chair Mike Rogers told the Pentagon to separate what it would like to have from what it has to have. (A Senate Republican aide pointed out that House Republicans adjourned early, which settles who was going to finish first.)

Electoral-vote.com's latest page, dated November 19, supplies the geopolitics. President Trump arrived in China, where Xi Jinping greeted him, and Vladimir Putin is attending a three-way summit. EV's writer expects Xi and Putin to strike a deal that serves them at American expense. That is EV's opinion, and the summit had not ended when it was written.

Sekirin's book is about exactly Rogers's question. His publisher says Ukraine's war shows how militaries should reorganize and buy weapons, and it names China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia as the challenge. A one-time lump sum is a strange fit for that lesson. The drone advantage the publisher describes comes from changing designs as fast as the enemy changes jammers, and a package passed once in a lame-duck session buys a snapshot. (That is our inference; read the book's procurement chapter and see whether Sekirin agrees.)

Our take: the fight over $350 billion is about how to pay, and Rogers is the only person quoted who asked what to buy. If Sekirin is right, that list looks very different from the one the Pentagon wrote. The most expensive answer is rarely the one the war chose. (S)

Spaced Review · six earlier books against Sekirin's question

Six Authors Who Already Heard "Everything Has Changed"

  1. McDougall, the space race. The rivalry that peaked with Sputnik made the state the main manager of technological change, and that race for security through technology risks eroding what it defends. The link: McDougall's sharpest line is that American technocracy aimed "to avoid defining its objective." Ask whether Sekirin's drone program defines its objective, or only its production numbers.
  2. Zhang, the Korean air war. China built an air force in a year and learned that new jets do not make a skilled force. The link: Zhang finds that inexperienced pilots could not use the MiG-15 well and that their proficiency "showed no significant improvement." Watch whether Sekirin credits the drone or the trained operator.
  3. Randolph, the Easter Offensive. Nixon's airpower blunted a conventional invasion but could not force a lasting settlement. The link: in 1972 the Americans had precision weapons, sensors, and satellite communications but lacked the command systems, doctrine, and training to use them together. Sekirin's case for a separate drone branch is an argument about exactly that gap.
  4. Lambeth, transformation. Stealth, standoff precision, and battlespace awareness made airpower's contribution grow in a nonlinear way. The link: Lambeth is the last author in this course to announce a new kind of war, and his own Kosovo chapter shows Serb units hiding in barns and forests and decoys made of milk-carton material cutting NATO's tank-kill claim from 150 to 110. Constant watching invites constant hiding.
  5. Fravel, active defense. China changes military strategy when a foreign war shows the nature of warfare has changed. The link: Jiang Zemin said in 1991 that "backwards technology means being in a passive position and taking a beating," and the PLA concluded it needed new concepts as well as new equipment. Every army in the world is reading Ukraine the way the PLA read the Gulf War, which is the syllabus's point about who is watching.
  6. Fuller, the CIA's drones. The armed drone carried out a policy of killing terrorists that Reagan-era hardliners set long before the Predator could fly. The link: Fuller shows the policy came before the technology, and that the CIA's "technological capacity was ahead of its human intelligence." Ask Sekirin which came first in Ukraine, the drones or the plan for them.

Opinion: five of these six would tell Sekirin that the machine matters less than the organization flying it. His career suggests he agrees, since his best-known achievement is a reorganization. (S)
