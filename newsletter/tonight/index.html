Sekirin, Rise of the Machines: The PDF Made It to Drive and Stopped for Thanksgiving

We still have not read a page of Illya Sekirin's book, so everything below about its argument comes from the publisher and reviewers. The PDF reached the 628 Drive folder on November 21, but at 33 megabytes it is too large for our Drive connection to read whole. Save a copy to the 628 folder on this computer and the next afternoon run will build the chapter lessons from the text.

The publisher states the argument this way: unmanned systems have changed war at the tactical, operational, and strategic levels. FPV attack drones, reconnaissance drones, electronic warfare, naval drones, and autonomous systems now watch everything, and tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft struggle to survive under that watch. Ukraine, on this telling, is the blueprint for how armies should reorganize, buy, train, and fight. The named cases are the fighting of 2022, Bakhmut, Avdiivka, Kursk, and the defense of Southern Donbas.

The book's target is the force this course has spent eight weeks studying. The syllabus calls it "a bold prediction" and asks whether the character of war has changed as much as the author claims. Our working view, which the text can overturn: Sekirin wins the tactical argument easily and has to fight for the strategic one, because the war he describes is, by the syllabus's own account, a stalemate. (A revolution that holds the front line in place is an unusual sort of revolution.) (S)

The World Around the Book

The Author Helped Build the Branch He Is Writing About

Illya Sekirin is an advocate first and a historian second, and that is the most useful thing to know before you open the book. He is a Ukrainian-born, naturalized Canadian who volunteered to fight in March 2022 and was trained as a drone operator. He served at the front, worked as a translator for the Ukrainian General Staff, and became an unofficial adviser to several members of the Stavka, the high command that President Zelensky chairs. His publisher says he wrote a paper that informed Zelensky's decision to create the Unmanned Systems Forces as a separate branch. (Few authors get to review their own reorganization.)

The axe is documented, so we can name it. A local newspaper interview with his editor reports that Sekirin believes Ukraine could have won earlier had his drone "blitz" been fully carried out, and that he now sees a costly stalemate and favors a negotiated settlement. That is the claim of a man who thinks his idea was right and was under-used. Read every judgment about Ukrainian commanders with that in mind. Our take: an insider's access is the book's strength, and an insider's stake is its blind spot.

The editor matters too. Andrew Simms spent twelve years at the British Army's Soldier magazine, rising to editor-in-chief, and now edits the British Army Review for the Army's historical analysis center at Sandhurst. Sekirin first reached him by submitting articles to that journal. Expect a book shaped for a Western army audience: lessons, recommendations, and a warning.

The moment is a war still being fought. Helion lists publication on January 30, 2026, and its U.S. distributor lists April 16, 2026, so the book went to press with no ending to write. The publisher's description pitches it against an orthodoxy: armies built around tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft, which it says now struggle under constant observation and cheap, expendable attack. It also sets the war inside a wider contest with China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia, which it calls an "axis of upheaval," and argues the West must adapt or face strategic obsolescence. Those are the publisher's words for the argument. We will judge the argument itself once the text is on the desk.

The reception is thin and friendly. The publisher's pages quote the Journal of the Air Force Historical Foundation, which called it "a timely and ambitious contribution" to the literature on unmanned systems. The only independent review we could open, by Robert McKie on a New Zealand army logistics history blog in April 2026, strongly recommends it and reads it as a book about logistics: when everything on the battlefield can be seen, the rear area stops being safe. McKie's only caveat is that it offers no neat answers. We found no scholarly review and no critical one, so the critical reception is undocumented.

Our verdict: the friendly reviews are probably right that the book is valuable, and they have not yet tested its biggest claim. "The character of war has changed" is the sentence every technology enthusiast in this course has written, from the space race to Lambeth. The seminar question is whether Sekirin proves it or only reports it. (Mark the pages where he counts results, and the pages where he predicts them.) (S)

Read For

Five Things to Catch Him Doing

  1. Counting versus predicting. Mark every passage where he gives a number for what drones did, and every passage where he says what they will do. The seminar's first question lives in the gap between the two.
  2. The stalemate problem. The syllabus says the war has outlasted the First World War as a stalemate. Watch whether he explains why a revolution in weapons produced a frozen front.
  3. His own role. He helped create the branch he writes about. Watch how he treats commanders who resisted his ideas, and whether he cites anyone who disagrees with him.
  4. The deep strikes. The syllabus says Ukraine now strikes commercial infrastructure deep inside Russia. Read it next to Clodfelter and Olsen: what result does he claim, and how does he measure it?
  5. Cheap against expensive. The publisher stresses cheap, expendable attack. Track whether he counts the cost of the defense too: jammers, interceptors, and the people who fly the drones.
Seminar Questions · Monday, November 30

Four Questions and Where the Answers Will Live

These come from the syllabus page for Seminar 17, "The Future Character of War?" Chapter locations follow once the text is on disk; for now, here is where each answer will come from.

  1. Has the character of war changed to the extent the author claims? His introduction and conclusion for the claim; his case studies for the proof. Lambeth and McDougall are the best rival witnesses.
  2. If paired with AI for autonomous strikes, can drone technology alter the nature of war? His chapter on autonomy, if there is one. Note the shift in the question from "character" to "nature," which is Clausewitz's line between how war is fought and what war is.
  3. What other technological advances should be compared to Ukraine's drones? The course itself: the jet over MiG Alley in Zhang, the laser-guided bomb in Randolph, stealth and precision in Lambeth, the Predator in Fuller.
  4. What opportunities or dangers does this pose for strategists? His recommendations to Western armies, and the publisher's "axis of upheaval" framing.

Opinion: prepare question two. The others ask about drones; that one asks about war, and the room will need someone who knows the difference. (S)

Bridge From Friday · Wasser to Sekirin

An F-15E, a Missile, and a Drone Worth Rather Less

Wasser's report opens with the drone war arriving early. On June 8, 2017, near the al-Tanf garrison in Syria, F-15E Strike Eagles found an Iranian-made Shahed 129 armed with two Hellfire-like missiles, and after it turned hot one of the pilots shot it down with an AIM-120C. Twelve days later, another F-15E shot down a second Shahed 129. ISIS itself flew only small drones for reconnaissance and attacks, so the coalition's air defense worried far more about Russia. In 2017, then, the drone was a nuisance that a fighter could swat, one expensive missile at a time.

Sekirin writes about the war where that arithmetic broke. If drones come by the thousand, a fighter and a missile per drone is a way to lose slowly. (The Strike Eagle crews at al-Tanf were not, to be fair, solving for 2026.) Keep one question in mind while reading: what did Wasser's coalition have that Ukraine does not, and is it the airspace, the budget, or the enemy's restraint? (S)

Political Strategy × Sekirin · via Punchbowl News AM and electoral-vote.com, November 23

The Watchers' Permission Slip Expires in March

Congress will miss the Thanksgiving deadline the intelligence community gave it to renew FISA Section 702, Punchbowl News reported this morning, and nobody has a clear way out. The authority lapsed in June. The program keeps running because the surveillance court recertified it through March 17. Sen. Mark Warner (D-Va.) says the communications providers have kept cooperating, "but that all turns off in March."

The blame is bipartisan, which is rarer than the surveillance. House Intelligence Chair Rick Crawford (R-Ark.) and ranking member Jim Himes both point at the Senate and its failure to take up the House bill. That bill carries a ban on a Federal Reserve digital currency, which makes it a non-starter in the Senate. (Nothing says urgent national security like a rider about central bank money.) Armed Services leaders Mike Rogers and Adam Smith agree on one thing: keep FISA away from the NDAA.

Electoral-vote.com's Monday page shows how crowded the exit is. Government funding runs out December 11, and EV sees a one-week continuing resolution as possible, with another running into January after that. The president's December list also includes judges, a farm bill, the surveillance extension, and the $350 billion military package. House Republicans are short-handed because defeated members have gone home. EV's read is that the lame duck may accomplish less than anyone planned.

Sekirin's publisher argues that tanks, artillery, and manned aircraft now struggle to survive under constant observation. Section 702 is foreign intelligence collection, a different thing from a drone over Bakhmut, but the two share a dependency that the drone enthusiasts tend to skip. Somebody has to authorize the watching and pay for it, on a schedule.

Our take: a sensor advantage is only as durable as the statute and the budget line under it. Ukraine's drone force answers to a government at war. America's watchers answer to a floor calendar. Persistent surveillance, it turns out, has an expiration date. (S)

Spaced Review · five earlier books against Sekirin's question

Drones Have Been the Future Since 1952

  1. Crane, Korea. The Air Force entered Korea with inflated expectations, could not reach the true sources of enemy power, and let an ambiguous armistice excuse it from the lessons. The link: in 1952 the Navy launched six radio-controlled Hellcat drones from the carrier Boxer. One hit an undefended bridge, one was ditched, and the rest missed because of enemy jamming or human error. Evaluators saw more promise in reconnaissance. Ask what Sekirin's FPV pilots have solved that those crews could not.
  2. Clodfelter, Vietnam. Bombing's value depends on the goals sought and the kind of war being fought. The link: in Linebacker II, SAC's long planning lead time locked in repeated routes until losses forced a change. Ukraine's drone war is a test of the same idea, that tactics must adapt faster than headquarters can plan.
  3. Haun, Vietnam. Airpower works best against fielded forces alongside a capable ground force, because it makes the enemy disperse and hide or die massed. The link: this is the closest thing in the course to Sekirin's battlefield. Haun also shows Assault Breaker, a sensor-to-missile idea, dying largely because the Air Force disliked a concept with no role for its strike aircraft. Watch for the Ukrainian version of that fight.
  4. Olsen, Desert Storm. The strategic air campaign degraded Iraq but did not achieve its unstated aim of changing the regime. The link: unmanned decoys on opening night drew out Iraqi radars for HARMs to kill. Cheap, expendable aircraft were already doing serious work in 1991, and the war's political result still depended on something else.
  5. Wasser, Inherent Resolve. Airpower was indispensable because it enabled partner ground forces, and those partners set the pace. The link: in 2017, MQ-1s and MQ-9s flew more than 12,000 sorties and demand still outran supply. Sekirin's answer to that shortage is mass. Ask what mass costs when the enemy can jam it and shoot it down by the thousand.

Opinion: every one of these authors found the machine impressive and the strategy harder. Sekirin has to be the exception to earn his title. (S)

The Bedtime Version

Many Wings Make Light Work

One summer, Big Bear decided the whole berry bank at Quiet Pond was his. He came padding around the water, very big and very sure.

Little Otter lived on the berry bank. She was small. She could not push a bear. So she asked the fireflies for help.

The fireflies were tiny. They cost nothing but a kind word. And there were so many.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Nobody could hide.

When Big Bear tiptoed left, the fireflies saw him. When he tiptoed right, the fireflies saw him. Otter moved her berry baskets just in time.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Nobody could hide.

Soon Big Bear found some fireflies of his own. Now Otter could not sneak either. Bear stayed on his side. Otter stayed on hers. The berries sat in the middle, and nobody got them.

Juniper the small owl watched from the sky. "You can both see everything," she said softly. "But seeing doesn't share the berries. Only talking does."

Bear yawned. Otter yawned. Maybe they would talk in the morning.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Everyone slept.

For the grown-up: Big Bear is Russia, Little Otter is Ukraine, and the fireflies are cheap drones that make hiding impossible. The ending is the reported view of the author, who sees a costly stalemate and favors a negotiated settlement. We built this from the publisher and press accounts, so check it against the book. (S)
