Tonight's Reading · for Friday's seminar

McDougall, ...the Heavens and the Earth: The Race Nobody Is Allowed to Quit

McDougall argues that the rivalry that peaked with Sputnik made the state the main sponsor and manager of new technology, and that this change, which he calls technocracy, outlasted the race that started it. In the ten years up to 1965, he says, the West dropped its postwar belief that state-directed science defeated itself, and Western governments quintupled their research spending for national goals. He calls it a saltation, an evolutionary leap. The space race is his case study. The moon is almost a side issue.

The book alternates between the two capitals, which makes it easy to read in pieces. Part I follows Soviet rocketry to Sputnik, Part II covers America before Sputnik, Part III is Eisenhower's space policy, Part IV is Khrushchev's, Part V is Kennedy and Johnson and what McDougall calls the technocratic temptation, and Part VI surveys the first twenty-five years. Each Part ends with a short conclusion, and those few pages are where McDougall states his argument most plainly. Read them even when you skip the chapter in front of them.

He argues against two comfortable stories. The first is that Sputnik proved the Soviets were ahead. McDougall shows a primitive booster, a missile force that barely existed, and a bluff that lasted about seven years. The second is that Apollo proved American management could do anything. He shows a program with no defined objective, $146 billion in government research spending from Sputnik to the landing with no national plan, and a country that stopped for a nap once it won. (He is equally rude to both superpowers, which is how you know it is history and not a brochure.)

For this course, the book moves the limited war from the battlefield to the budget and the newspaper. Nobody in it drops a bomb, yet the stakes are deterrence, reconnaissance, and who gets to fly over whom. Read it asking which side knew the truth, which side could prove it, and why those were different questions. His warning is the one seminar will chew on: the race threatens to "erode the very values that make one's society worth defending." (S)

Bridge From Today · Zhang to McDougall

Same Stalin, Different Disguise

Zhang and McDougall describe one Soviet habit from two angles: show the world a strength you do not have, and hide the one you do. In Zhang's Korea, Soviet pilots flew MiGs over the Yalu in Chinese uniforms, Stalin's promise of air cover was never defined, and both sides inflated their kill claims, the Americans at eight or ten to one before some revised it to about two. McDougall finds the same Stalin after Hiroshima telling his scientists "The equilibrium has been destroyed" and demanding a bomb, which arrived a year earlier than Kurchatov predicted. Khrushchev inherits the habit and runs it the other way, advertising an ICBM force he had not built. The Americans were good customers both times. In November 1951 General Vandenberg called Communist China "one of the major air powers of the world," and by 1960 CIA worst cases had the Soviets at up to 400 ICBMs in 1961. The two books also meet in 1953. Zhang argues Stalin's death that March broke the armistice deadlock, and McDougall reports that Soviet scholars dated the American scientific-technical revolution from that year, because the Korean War had locked large-scale government research into the American system.

Keep one question in mind while reading: if the image of air and missile power can be faked in both directions, what does each side need to see before it can call the bluff, and who pays while it waits? Chapter 16 has McDougall's answer, and it orbits. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: McDougall, ...the Heavens and the Earth

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, introduction and chapter 1

The Prison Bureau Delivered on Time

Never assume a repressive rival cannot innovate in the one sector it truly cares about. McDougall's introduction sets the frame: for most of history states reacted to new tools and left the inventing to others, the world wars made state research a duty, and Sputnik pushed the West into what he calls technocracy, where the state sponsors technological change on purpose. Lyndon Johnson put the new creed in one sentence: "first in space means first, period." Chapter 1 shows that Russia got there first, by the cruelest route available.

Russia's road to Stalin's research system was long. Part I opens in 1881 with Tsar Alexander II killed by a People's Will bomb, whose maker, Nikolai Kibalchich, spent his last days in prison designing a rocket-propelled aircraft. Tsiolkovsky received a single grant from the old regime, 470 rubles. Lenin drew the lesson of the Great War in 1919, master the highest technology or be crushed, and a 1924 rocket bureau made the Soviet Union the first government to back spaceflight. Stalin then supplied money and terror together. The Academy of Sciences grew from 1,000 people in 1927 to over 16,000 by 1940, and a failed experiment could get a scientist branded a "wrecker." Civilian research suffered. McDougall names the exception: "That sector was defense." By 1939 Soviet factories turned out 700 to 800 planes a month. Korolev followed Tupolev into prison in 1938, and the writer Ozerov, finding the elite of Soviet aircraft design at work as prisoners, called it a catastrophe for Soviet aviation. McDougall answers in five words: "In fact, it did not." The prisoners designed fighters and dive bombers, and Langemak, reprieved from a death sentence, designed the Katyusha. (It was the most exclusive engineering faculty in the world, with admission by arrest warrant.)

The weak seminar comment is "The Soviet system was too repressive to innovate, so Sputnik was a fluke." It is weak because it ignores the author's own counterevidence: McDougall spends a chapter showing that terror hurt civilian science and spared defense. The strong version says terror and innovation coexisted in the sector the state prized, and it cites the prison bureaus, the Katyusha, and 700 to 800 planes a month. Opinion: "they can't innovate" is the most comfortable sentence in threat assessment, and 1930s Russia is McDougall's proof that it is wrong.

The strong version wins because the chain runs all the way to Sputnik. First order, the prison bureaus kept military designs coming while their designers were under guard. Second order, Korolev survived to build the R-7, twenty engines and 1.1 million pounds of thrust, which flew in August 1957, and McDougall's Part I conclusion says war, not terror, was what slowed Soviet rocketry. Third order, that rocket launched Sputnik, and the introduction says Sputnik set off the leap in which Western governments quintupled their research spending. A critic who calls Sputnik a fluke has to explain away a forty-year pipeline. The introduction's last page explains why nobody gets to stop: a state can switch off its own research, but not its rivals'.

If terror did not stop Soviet military innovation, what advantage exactly is a free society claiming in a race nobody may quit? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 2 and 3

A Monopoly With a Dozen Bombs and No Plan

Before you lean on a deterrent, count what stands behind it, because the other side is counting too, and its fear sets the pace of the race. Chapter 2 shows a frightened Moscow sprinting after the American bomb. Chapter 3 shows a comfortable Washington holding the bomb with very little behind it. Side by side, they explain why the race started when it did.

After Hiroshima, Stalin told his munitions commissar and the physicist Kurchatov, "The equilibrium has been destroyed," and demanded a bomb. Kurchatov guessed five years. The Soviet bomb came in August 1949, a year early. Soviet research spending rose from 6,300 million rubles in 1946 to 9,000 million by 1950, and McDougall judges Soviet military research perhaps six times the American effort as a share of national product. In Washington, the Finletter Commission of 1947 asked to see the war plan, and after a Pentagon runaround Eisenhower told his colleagues they owed the civilians the truth: "there is no war plan." Spaatz figured there were a dozen or so warheads. Only six of twenty-six B-29 groups had aircraft, and they were based in the Southwest, where they "threatened no one but Canada and Mexico." American science, McDougall says, "grew up an orphan." Robert Goddard earned eighty-three patents and was dismissed as a sort of crackpot, and peace put the orphan back on short rations. A $14.4 billion ceiling on the 1950 defense budget left the Joint Chiefs unable to hold a foothold in Europe, and in July 1949 Truman concluded "we must be strongest in atomic weapons."

The weak seminar comment is "The United States was far ahead after 1945 because it had the bomb." It is weak because it describes instead of arguing: it counts the invention and skips the inventory, the plan, and the delivery force. The strong version says the monopoly was thin, a dozen warheads and no war plan, and the budget ceiling made the bomb the cheap option. Opinion: Washington chose its strategy by subtraction, and Moscow set its schedule by fear. Neither belongs in a recruiting video.

The chain runs into the next decade. First order, the ceiling pushed Truman toward atomic weapons as the affordable defense. Second order, the Soviet bomb of August 1949 brought Truman's hydrogen bomb decision in January 1950, then NSC-68 and Korea, and defense spending tripled. Third order, McDougall's Part II opening says nuclear deterrence chained American defense to fast technological change, and that the restraint of these years helped make the country second into space. That chain pre-empts the reply that the monopoly bought safety. It bought a few years and a race. Chapter 2 closes the loop at a Soviet Embassy party in October 1957, where an émigré said "Poor Tsiolkovsky" was turning in his grave, a Soviet guest promised he would eat his words, and Radio Moscow soon announced a satellite in orbit. Khrushchev, told the news, went to bed. He could afford to.

If Truman's budget picked the strategy and Stalin's fear set the pace, who actually started the race that ended with Sputnik? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 4 and 5

Eisenhower Lost the Race He Wasn't Running

Before you call a loss a blunder, find out which race the loser thought he was running. Chapter 4 shows the United States shelving its rockets under Truman's austerity and reviving them only when Soviet bombs forced the issue. Chapter 5 argues that Eisenhower lost the race to orbit because he was running a different one, for the legal right to fly spy satellites over the Soviet Union.

RAND's October 1950 report, which McDougall calls the "birth certificate of American space policy," saw satellites mainly as reconnaissance tools, expected a closed Soviet Union to treat spying from orbit as an attack on its secrecy, and noted that nobody knew whether national airspace had a ceiling. Its fix was an "experimental" satellite on an equatorial orbit that never crossed Soviet territory, to establish "freedom of space" first. The report reached the Air Force on October 4, 1950, seven years to the day before Sputnik. Eisenhower's New Look needed hard intelligence to cut defense safely, so the U-2 was built in eighty days, and the National Security Council put protecting the reconnaissance satellite ahead of speed. On August 3, 1955, the Stewart committee chose the navy's Vanguard over von Braun's Redstone, a choice McDougall calls ideal politically, if not technically. In September 1956 a Jupiter-C flew 682 miles high and came down 3,355 miles downrange, and inspectors had made sure its upper stages were dummies so von Braun could not orbit anything "accidentally." On the night of Sputnik, von Braun told Defense Secretary McElroy at Huntsville that he could orbit a satellite in sixty days, and his commander, Medaris, corrected him to ninety. Four days after Sputnik, Quarles told Eisenhower the Redstone could have done the job a year or more earlier, then looked on the bright side: the Russians had "done us a good turn" by establishing freedom of space. Eisenhower asked about a reconnaissance vehicle.

The weak seminar comment is "Eisenhower blundered and let the Soviets win the space race." It is weak because it ignores the author's evidence of a deliberate choice: the RAND logic, the NSC priority, and the dummy stages. The strong version says Eisenhower traded first place for a legal precedent that protected his spy satellites, and Quarles's "good turn" shows the administration saw it that way within the week. Opinion: few presidents have been so pleased with a defeat, and fewer still had to keep the reason classified.

The strong version also survives McDougall's own objection, which is what makes it hard to rebut. First order, Sputnik flew over everyone and nobody protested, so the overflight precedent existed. Second order, McDougall's Part II conclusion calls Sputnik "the greatest defeat Eisenhower could have suffered," because it handed critics a weapon against his whole philosophy of government. Third order, the precedent paid off in 1961, when reconnaissance satellites cut the count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen. A comment that concedes the political cost and still defends the trade is harder to attack than either "genius" or "blunder."

Was the freedom of space worth what a public defeat cost the policies Eisenhower was protecting? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 6 and 7

Count the Panic Before You Report It

Measure a public panic before you describe one, because the noise and the numbers often disagree. McDougall does this in chapter 6, setting the Sputnik headlines beside a Gallup poll and calling the result a "media riot." Chapter 7 follows Eisenhower's answer, four measures each built to be temporary or limited, and shows each one opening a door he could not shut.

The poll, taken October 11 to 14, 1957, found half the sample calling Sputnik a serious blow to American prestige, 46 percent saying it was not, and 61 percent expecting satellites to be used for good. That is a divided, mostly hopeful public. The press was another matter. Life told readers, "Let us not pretend that Sputnik is anything but a defeat for the United States." Defense Secretary Wilson called Sputnik a "neat scientific trick," and Sherman Adams said the goal was not a high score "in a celestial basketball game," which helped about as much as you would expect. A memo to Lyndon Johnson noted the issue could make him President, and his hearings opened November 25 with Sputnik called a "technological Pearl Harbor." The administration supplied the rest. The White House insisted that a Vanguard test be billed as a real satellite attempt, and on December 6 the rocket rose four feet, burst into flame, and settled back, leaving its little satellite chirping on the ground. The press named it Flopnik and Kaputnik, and Americans drank the Sputnik Cocktail, "two parts vodka, one part sour grapes." When the army's Explorer 1 reached orbit on January 31, 1958, Eisenhower said, "Let's not make too great a hullabaloo over this." He may have been the only American who wanted less hullabaloo.

The weak seminar comment is "Sputnik panicked the American people." It is weak because it asserts without evidence and overgeneralizes; the only poll in the chapter shows a public split down the middle. The strong version says editors, senators, and an administration that publicized a test made the panic, and it puts the 46 percent and Life's headline side by side. Opinion: this is McDougall's best method in the book, and it is worth stealing. Historians who count the panic first write better history.

The chain shows why the riot mattered anyway. First order, the riot set the agenda, and Eisenhower had to answer it, though his first post-Sputnik budget rose only 1.5 percent. Second order, he answered with four measures designed to limit themselves: the National Defense Education Act, written to run seven years, Pentagon reorganization, NASA, and the Space Act, which split space into an open civilian program and a closed military one to keep the country "a leader, not the leader." Third order, the limits failed. A $60 million rider widened the education act, and McDougall says the Space Act started the civil-military fight instead of ending it. That chain pre-empts the reply that a media riot is harmless noise, because this one wrote law. If the public was not panicking, who owned the riot, and can a government whose best facts are secret ever win one? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 8 and 9

Fly First, Define Later

When you need a right your adversary will not grant, establish it by doing, and keep its definition vague until the practice is settled. Chapter 8 argues that Eisenhower's space strategy had a public face, "space for peace," and a private one, freedom to fly the missions deterrence needed, above all spy satellites. Chapter 9 adds a warning about the agency he built to carry the public face: NASA had every reason to want the race to go on.

The real policy was NSC 5814/1, approved in mid-August 1958. It handled missiles separately from space, so a UN space deal could not freeze missiles, called reconnaissance satellites critical to national security, and declined to say where outer space began, preferring a functional test: if it is in orbit, it is in space. Eisenhower's Operations Coordinating Board asked about "a cover for such reconnaissance satellites," and its answer was rhetoric: call the military program vital to deterrence and therefore peaceful. At the UN, the General Assembly backed the Western resolution fifty-four to nine, with eighteen abstentions, and Washington won the wording. Space would serve "peaceful" purposes instead of "nonmilitary" ones, and that gap sheltered the Pentagon. Chapter 9 turns to NASA. Its first choice for administrator, Hugh Dryden, sank himself by telling Congress that putting a man in a Redstone nose cone had the technical value of "the circus stunt of shooting a young lady from a cannon." Glennan got the job, and the agency got muscle by raiding the Army, which lost the von Braun team to NASA effective July 1, 1960. Glennan admitted in May 1959 that the agency "had no objective," and McDougall's verdict is blunt: "NASA clearly needed the Soviets." At a White House dinner in December 1959, Vice President Nixon said prestige was the real motive and the question would be "how many more Soviet successes can we stand." NASA's 1961 budget came to $964 million after Congress added $50 million. For an agency told to be sensible, it ate well.

The weak seminar comment is "Eisenhower wanted space to be used for peace." It is weak because it takes the public face at its word and ignores the author's evidence of the private one. The strong version says "space for peace" was a cover the administration asked for by name, and the deliberately vague boundary let spy satellites fly before anyone could rule them illegal. Opinion: the most useful word in the policy was the one it refused to define.

The chain runs a decade. First order, the "peaceful" wording and the functional definition kept military satellites lawful while American hardware caught up, which took a while; Discoverer 2's capsule came down somewhere in northern Norway and was lost. Second order, the Soviets protested for three years, then dropped the campaign in December 1963, and a UN resolution declared outer space free for all and outside national sovereignty. Third order, the 1967 Outer Space Treaty banned nuclear weapons in orbit and left orbit open to military use. Every link is in the book, which pre-empts the reply that the strategy was mere hypocrisy. It was hypocrisy that worked. If NASA's budget rose with the Soviet challenge, did Eisenhower build an agency with every reason to keep the race going? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 10 to 12

The Truth Was Classified, the Bluff Was Not

A secret fact cannot win a public argument, so a leader who relies on classified evidence should expect to lose the debate while winning the policy. Chapter 10 shows Eisenhower right about Soviet missiles and unable to prove it. Chapters 11 and 12 show Khrushchev wrong about his own missiles and able to sell them for years.

CIA worst cases ran as high as 100 Soviet ICBMs by 1960 and 400 in 1961, while U-2 photographs seemed to show none deployed. The photographs were classified. So, pressed on urgency, Eisenhower told reporters "What you want is enough," and added that a deterrent gains no power once it is fully adequate. That is good strategy and terrible television. Kennedy raised the "space gap" over twenty times in the 1960 campaign. After the election a panel priced a manned lunar landing at an extra $26 to $58 billion, and Eisenhower said he was "not about to hock his jewels" and vetoed Apollo, while approving the Saint satellite interceptor on his way out. Khrushchev, meanwhile, told his party congress that ICBMs were coming off the line "like sausages." Chapter 12 opens with McDougall's verdict: "For it was all a bluff." The Soviet Union had yet to deploy a single ICBM, the first confirmed total was 150 in 1965, and in late 1962 the Americans led by about 194 to 72. About thirty-five of the big SS-6 rockets went into space shots, which bought more headlines than a small missile force could buy security. The spectaculars also had a body count. In October 1960, with two Mars-bound rockets already fizzled, a third failed to ignite, Marshal Nedelin of the new Strategic Rocket Forces ordered technicians out of their blockhouses to examine it, and it exploded. Estimates of the dead range from scores to hundreds, and Moscow reported that Nedelin had died in a plane crash. When Khrushchev showed the film from Powers's U-2 to prove it had found no missile bases, McDougall notes, "there were none to photograph."

The weak seminar comment is "The missile gap was a hoax, so it did not matter." It is weak because it confuses the facts with their effect; the bluff was false and still moved people. The strong version says the bluff worked on audiences who could not look: in June 1955 only 6 percent of West Europeans thought the West weaker, and by 1960 Britons judged the Soviets stronger by 59 to 15 percent. Opinion: sausages sell, as long as nobody tours the factory. The Soviets also understood the arithmetic better than their customers did, which is why the big rockets went to space shots instead of silos.

The chain shows where the bluff ran out. First order, it won allied opinion and handed the Democrats an election issue. Second order, it had costs: the Americans did not blink over Berlin, the Chinese demanded to know why Moscow was so timid if it was so mighty, and Khrushchev could not cooperate in space without "both giving away our strength and revealing our weakness." Third order, the spy satellite was the real threat to the bluff, which McDougall gives seven years, from Sputnik to the aftermath of the Cuban crisis. That chain pre-empts both "the gap was real" and "the gap was harmless." Get the numbers straight, too: cite the 400 as a count and a classmate will correct you with the 150 all the way to lunch. If the bluff lasted only while the Soviet Union stayed closed, did American reconnaissance do more to end the missile gap than any American missile? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 13 to 15

Khrushchev Believed His Own Brochure

A bluff turns dangerous when its author starts making policy as if it were true. Chapters 13 and 14 show Khrushchev cutting his army on the strength of rockets he did not have, then losing his job over the rockets he did. Chapter 15 shows Kennedy making the opposite error: he picked an end and left the means for later.

In January 1960 Khrushchev justified a one-third cut in the standing army by telling the Supreme Soviet that "almost the entire air force is being replaced with rockets." It was false, and it turned nearly every branch of the military against him. Within a year he retreated, the defense budget shot up 29 percent in a single year, and the army cuts were restored. When Marshal Sokolovsky's Military Strategy appeared in the summer of 1962, the Soviets were finally ready to deploy ICBMs, and they found "it was the space program that represented a bluff," at least five years behind in military satellites. The worse things went, the more he leaned on spectaculars: in August 1961 the cosmonaut Titov called him the cosmonauts' "space father," and a week later Soviet troops sealed off East Berlin. By October 1964 no power group of any size would back Khrushchev. He wanted a three-man capsule to beat Gemini, so Korolev's team stripped a one-man Vostok, crammed in three seats, and left out the space suits. Voskhod I flew on October 12, 1964, and Pravda headlined "Sorry Apollo!" On October 13 Khrushchev was relieved of all his party posts. McDougall's summary: "So Space Age communism fizzled like a capitalist rocket."

The weak seminar comment is "Khrushchev's space program was a propaganda triumph." It is weak because it is true but unarguable, and it stops at the first-order effect. The strong version says the propaganda worked abroad and wrecked him at home, because he cut real forces for imaginary ones and alienated the army, heavy industry, and even the scientists. Chapter 15 supplies the mirror image. Kennedy's team thought of space "in terms of ends," set aside Wiesner's warning against a prestige race, and after Gagarin's flight Kennedy asked, "Is there any place we can catch them?" The final moon message asked for upward of $20 billion, and James Webb later reminded Johnson that "you had to almost drive me" to recommend it. Opinion: one leader bought means for an end he invented, and the other bought an end and assumed the means would turn up.

Every link of the Soviet chain is in the book. First order, the rockets-for-army claim justified the troop cut. Second order, the military turned hostile, the defense budget jumped 29 percent, and the cuts reversed. Third order, a leader with no coalition left launched a stripped capsule for a headline and was removed the next day. Gaddis defines strategy as relating ends to means, and Khrushchev's chain shows what happens when the means are imaginary. That pre-empts the claim that the spectaculars were cheap wins. They were bought on credit, and the bill arrived on October 13. If Kennedy settled the end and never seriously asked about the means, was the moon decision a strategy at all? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 16 to 18

The Film Came Back, the Order Stood

Evidence rarely cancels a program once the program has a constituency, so ask what would change the buy before the first contract is signed. Chapter 16 shows reconnaissance satellites killing the missile gap and the missile buildup going ahead anyway. Chapters 17 and 18 show why: American space policy had learned to keep the public uninformed and spread the money widely.

In June 1961 the CIA's estimate of Soviet missiles dropped, without explanation, from 120 to 60. Discoverer 25 flew on June 16, missed its recovery area, and plunged into the sea, and navy frogmen labored to retrieve it. The count fell to at most fourteen. McDougall's sentence is the one to quote: "The first satellite sparked the missile gap; the first reconnaissance satellites snuffed it out." The buildup went ahead. SAC had asked for 10,000 ICBMs, which at least showed ambition, and McNamara told Kennedy that 950 Minutemen was the lowest he could request and "not get murdered" in Congress. The country got 41 Polaris submarines and exactly 1,000 Minutemen. Chapter 17 explains the silence: a January 1961 blackout forbade the Air Force even to mention satellite observation, though Moscow knew, and "Only the American public was being fooled." McDougall calls the whole posture "benign hypocrisy": cooperation in science, competition in engineering. The INTELSAT deal of July 1964 gave the American Comsat Corporation 61 percent, the Europeans 30.5 percent, and Washington a veto, which is sharing in the American sense. The emblem of the era was Telstar, built by AT&T and launched by NASA in 1962, which carried the first live television between continents and symbolized space uniting the world. Six months later radiation from American high-altitude nuclear tests knocked it out. Chapter 18 explains the momentum. Webb sent 90 percent of NASA's funds to private firms and spread grants to more universities in more regions, and by fiscal 1964 the NASA budget passed $5 billion, five times what it was three years earlier. When North American, five times Martin's size, won a contract, a lobbyist explained that five times as many people were happy: "That's democracy in its finest form."

The weak seminar comment is "Better intelligence would have prevented the overbuild." It is weak because it ignores the author's own counterevidence; the intelligence arrived in 1961 and the order stood. The strong version says the satellites answered the question and politics overruled the answer, because a constituency and a classified source do not debate on equal terms. Opinion: the frogmen did their part. The film changed nothing.

The chain runs through the decade. First order, the satellites cut the count to fourteen. Second order, the buy went ahead at 1,000 Minutemen, and the "no-cities" counterforce strategy unveiled in June 1962 was going obsolete by late 1963, replaced by assured destruction, which needed no new missiles. Third order, in 1967 McNamara admitted the force was "more than we require," a force built on "worst plausible case" analysis. That chain pre-empts the excuse that nobody knew. They knew in 1961, and the people who did not know were the voters, which chapter 17 shows was a policy choice. The question to ask of any program, then or now, is what evidence would shrink it, and who has been cleared to see that evidence. If the satellites showed no gap, was the 1,000-Minuteman force a hedge against surprise or a purchase that invited the Soviet buildup? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapters 19 to 21

What Would You Give Up for the Moon?

A program's real objective is whatever its managers will knowingly sacrifice other goals to achieve, so ask what was given up before you accept what was promised. The test belongs to the systems theorist C. West Churchman, and McDougall applies it in chapter 21. Chapters 19 and 20 set it up: the Apollo debate of 1962 to 1964 was loud and settled little, because nearly every side wanted the money spent and argued only about what it should buy.

Apollo had critics everywhere. One straw poll found non-NASA scientists opposed to it 110 to 3, and Eisenhower in June 1963 called spending $40 billion to reach the moon "just nuts." When Kennedy proposed at the UN in September 1963 that the two countries go together, Moscow ignored him, and the Senate defeated a further 10 percent cut 36 to 49 and barred a joint landing without the consent of Congress. Offering to share the moon persuaded the Senate to keep it. The Outer Space Treaty hearings in 1967 had the same flavor. Senator Gore found the treaty's language so indefinite that he could "almost use the word fuzzy," and when General Wheeler said the Joint Chiefs backed the treaty but it required better ways to detect orbiting nuclear weapons, Senator Cooper asked whether that meant more money for military space research. "That is correct, Senator." The treaty passed 88 to 0. McDougall's diagnosis of the era is "a technocracy of politicians," setting a national agenda and ordering up the hardware to deliver it. The government spent $146 billion on research from Sputnik to the landing with no national plan, and Panofsky dismissed the spin-off defense: "If you want the by-product, you should develop the by-product." Churchman's test does the rest. American technocracy promised guns and butter, so McDougall concludes that "The objective of American technocracy was to avoid defining its objective."

The weak seminar comment is "Apollo succeeded, because Americans landed on the moon." It is weak because it confuses a first-order effect with the outcome; the landing was the method's success, and McDougall is asking what the method was for. The strong version applies Churchman: name what the program knowingly sacrificed, find nothing, and conclude that the objective was never defined. Opinion: a goal that costs nothing else is a wish with a launch window.

The chain shows what an undefined objective costs. First order, Apollo reached the moon. Second order, civilian space spending had already peaked in fiscal 1965 ("Some people's feet got tired," Johnson recalled), and McDougall writes that even before the first landing "the American hare had stopped again to take a nap." Third order, the tortoise kept walking: in 1973 the Soviets put 124 spacecraft in orbit or beyond to 23 American, and the method that solved an engineering problem then failed on the new national agenda, since ending poverty was not an engineering problem. That chain pre-empts the reply that the landing proves the policy. The landing proves the engineering. By Churchman's test, has American air or space policy had a real objective since Sputnik, and what did it knowingly give up to get it? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · McDougall, chapter 22 (conclusion)

Contempt for Our Works, Value for Ourselves

Value the people who use the machines above the machines, and say out loud what the machines cannot do. McDougall's conclusion restates his thesis one last time: the rivalry that began in 1917 and peaked with Sputnik made the state the main promoter and manager of technical progress. He borrows Alexander Gerschenkron's rule that the more backward a country, the more the state must force change, and adds that in an age of perpetual innovation every country is permanently backward. Wartime methods became normal, and the line between state and society blurred.

He then sorts the answers people gave. Some surrendered to the machines; Churchman decided the real question was "how to design a god." Liberal clergy turned to counseling, and McDougall likens them to the Wizard of Oz: behind the curtain stands the same old social engineer. The orthodox, from Pius XII to von Braun, called technology a neutral tool, and McDougall calls that naive, because technology has a logic of its own. He predicts science will reach hard limits; the modest Uhuru satellite of 1970 sensed 161 X-ray sources, and physicists will keep splicing the atom until there is nothing left to splice. When that day comes, he suggests, science may become a branch of moral philosophy again. He also cuts human power down to size: Mount St. Helens erupted with the force of hundreds of megatons, migrating plovers navigate more precisely than a Minuteman, and Oppenheimer was no destroyer of worlds. Spaceflight, he says, "will teach us to have contempt for our works and value for ourselves," which he calls true humility. "Technology is our subcreation," to be loved while its limits are confessed. His prescription is to avoid both mellonolatry, worship of the future, and misoneism, hatred of change (two words that will make your text-to-speech app earn its keep), and to "pursue knowledge without mistaking knowledge for truth." He even rescues Frankenstein: the creature ran amok, he notes, only after people turned on it in hatred and fear.

The weak seminar comment is "McDougall is anti-technology." It is weak because it ignores the author's own counterevidence; he rejects hatred of change as firmly as worship of the future, and he says we must compete for power without making power an end in itself. The strong version says his target is the habit of treating new technology as its own justification. Opinion: that charge is harder to dodge, because it lands on everyone at the table who has ever written a requirement.

The chain is his thesis, and the earlier chapters supply the links. First order, the race made the state the sponsor of technical change, and Western governments quintupled their research spending. Second order, the methods outlived the purpose: $146 billion spent with no national plan and an objective nobody defined. Third order, the race threatens to "erode the very values that make one's society worth defending." Crane's Korea offers a service-sized version. FEAF's General Smart said "we certainly don't advertise our limitations," and the war's ambiguous ending let the Air Force go back to planning for general war, a bill it paid in Vietnam. McDougall's advice runs the other way: admit the limits early, while it is cheap. Is that a real risk for the airpower enterprise, or a luxury no Cold War service could afford? (S)

Three Old Friends Who Would Recognize Khrushchev

Only three 628 books come before McDougall, so the list is short and the links are strong.

  1. Gaddis, containment. Strategy is relating ends to means, and American policy swung between meeting every threat everywhere and picking the ground, mostly according to how leaders saw their resources. The link: Eisenhower's "What you want is enough" is the resource-limited swing in one sentence, and Kennedy's moon decision, made "in terms of ends," is the swing back. Gaddis would also recognize the 1950 budget fight in chapter 3, where a $14.4 billion ceiling picked the strategy before anyone debated it.
  2. Crane, airpower in Korea. The Air Force fought Korea with inflated expectations, could not reach the sources of enemy power, and let the ambiguous ending convince it that it had succeeded. The link: Crane's subject is "the image and reality of American airpower," and McDougall's is the image and reality of Soviet rocket power. Both show a service that would rather not advertise its limits. Crane's April 1952 FEAF study found that rail interdiction had failed despite more than 15,000 rail cuts, and McDougall's 1961 satellites found no missile gap behind years of headlines. In both books the data arrived, and the image outlived it.
  3. Zhang, the Chinese air force in Korea. The PLAAF entered the war hastily built, leaning on a Soviet patron who limited his own air war, and the Soviets flew most of the MiG war in disguise. The link: Stalin's vague promise of air cover in Zhang and Khrushchev's ICBMs "like sausages" in McDougall are the same Soviet move, a commitment that sounds bigger than what stands behind it. Zhang also explains the date that keeps turning up in McDougall: after Stalin died in March 1953, Moscow made ending the war a priority.

Opinion: all three would tell McDougall the bluff is older than the rocket. Gaddis would add that the budget always calls it eventually. (S)

  • Chs. 4 and 5: What did Quarles tell Eisenhower four days after Sputnik?

    That the Redstone could have orbited a satellite a year or more earlier, and that the Soviets had "done us a good turn" by establishing freedom of space. M 133

  • Chs. 6 and 7: What did the October 1957 Gallup poll show?

    Half called Sputnik a serious blow to prestige, 46 percent said it was not, and 61 percent expected satellites to be used for good, which is why McDougall calls the uproar a "media riot." M 144–145

  • Chs. 8 and 9: How did NSC 5814/1 handle the boundary of outer space?

    It refused to fix one and used a functional test (if it is in orbit, it is in space), and it treated missiles separately so a space deal could not freeze them. M 179–180

  • Chs. 10 to 12: How many ICBMs had the Soviets deployed during the bluff?

    None at first; the first confirmed total was 150 in 1965, and the Americans led about 194 to 72 in late 1962. M 249–252

  • Chs. 13 to 15: What happened the day after Voskhod I launched?

    Khrushchev was relieved of all his party posts on October 13, 1964. M 291–294

  • Chs. 16 to 18: What did reconnaissance satellites do to the Soviet missile count in 1961?

    Cut it from 120 to 60 to at most fourteen, yet the United States still built 41 Polaris boats and exactly 1,000 Minutemen. M 327–330

  • Chs. 19 to 21: What is Churchman's test, and what does McDougall conclude from it?

    A system's real objective is what its managers will knowingly sacrifice other goals to get; American technocracy promised guns and butter, so its objective was to avoid defining one. M 447–448

  • Ch. 22: How does McDougall reverse the "enlightened" lesson of spaceflight?

    He says it should teach contempt for our works and value for ourselves, which he calls true humility. M 455–458
