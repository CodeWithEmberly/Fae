Counterpoint · Zhang against Crane · review for the Korea model

Counterpoint: Stalin's Funeral Did What the Dam Raids Couldn't

Zhang is right and Crane is too polite to air pressure: Stalin's death broke the Korean deadlock, and the bombing did not. Crane allows that the last phase of the air pressure campaign "may have contributed" to ending the war, and that the dam raids might have moved the enemy's position "to some degree." Zhang reads the same months from the Communist side, and the calendar is against the bombers.

Stalin died on March 5, 1953. He had been holding a hard line at the talks, believing the war was getting on "America's nerves," and Zhang counts his wish to keep fighting as a major reason the war dragged on. On March 19 a Soviet government resolution made ending the war a high priority. Mao gave way after Moscow said it could not increase aid for China's First Five-Year Plan unless the war ended. On March 28 Beijing accepted the UN proposal on repatriating prisoners, and the talks reopened. The irrigation-dam raids, FEAF's last big push to bomb out a settlement, began in May, two months after Moscow decided. (Cause, as a rule, arrives before effect.) Zhang even cites Crane's own page to say the dam raids mostly showed that Washington was losing patience.

The bombing also failed to stop the Chinese army. In the last two and a half months, UN air forces flew as many as 2,600 sorties a day, and the Chinese still supplied 530,000 troops in Korea. On July 13 they massed 1,360 guns and fired 1,900 tons of shells in thirty minutes, their first firepower advantage of the war. In the last thirteen days they used more than twice the ammunition of the war's first eight months, with eight months of food in storage. At the signing, Mao was saying the war should go on another year. A beaten army does not usually ask for a sequel.

Crane's best case is real. He describes plenty of signs that American patience was wearing thin and the war might widen, and rumors that Eisenhower would "raise the ante unless a cease fire was negotiated" ran all over Korea. The Sabres were claiming sixteen MiGs for every F-86 lost from January to April. Zhang himself concedes that the dam raids may have worn down Kim Il Sung, whose people were dying at three hundred to four hundred a day and who wanted the war over. We concede that point in full: the bombing made the war very expensive for Pyongyang.

It still loses, because Kim did not hold the pen. Moscow and Beijing did, and Chinese commanders were attacking in June and July to force the peace on their own terms; Peng Dehuai even put off the signing to punish South Korean troops. Crane's own list of the other causes starts with Stalin's death, the instability in the Kremlin, and late ground gains that let the Communists save face. First order, the bombing hurt North Korea. Second order, the Air Force credited the bombing anyway, and in 1955 its public information office was assembling a book titled Airpower, the Decisive Force in Korea. Third order, Tactical Air Command rebuilt itself for nuclear delivery, and the F-105 it sent to Vietnam was, in Crane's words, "unsuitable both for air combat and for true close air support."

What would change our mind? Chinese or Soviet records showing the dam raids or the nuclear rumors entered the leaders' talks between March and July. Zhang finds no evidence that Soviet and Chinese leaders even knew of the nuclear preparations. The line for seminar: "The armistice followed Moscow's calendar, not FEAF's: the Soviet resolution came on March 19 and the dam raids in May." In plain terms, coercion works only on whoever makes the decision, and the bombers spent 1953 pressing on the wrong capital. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Crane · extrapolation

The Hold Nobody Wrote Doctrine For

Crane would tell Shaun that the Korea model is sound and that the Air Force is the part of it most likely to forget. The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the Iran war without a settlement, and his thesis is that Airmen should prepare now to hold the strait the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Crane, not Shaun's answer. It tests two preparations: the small American footprint and the open-seas narrative.

Start with the footprint. After the armistice, Weyland asked for new ways to use airpower for limited aims, and his Composite Air Strike Forces were built to reach "brush-fire" wars quickly. The New Look budgets then pulled Tactical Air Command toward nuclear delivery, and Crane says it became a "junior SAC." Air Force policy held that "The best preparation for limited war is proper preparation for general war," and Dennis Drew, quoted by Crane, says 1950s doctrine seemed to assume "that the Korean War had not happened." (The longest hold in American history, and the doctrine writers missed it.) Opinion, extrapolated: this supports Shaun's word "now." The forces for a cheap hold need their own budget line and their own champion, or the big war absorbs them, and today the big war is the Pacific.

The narrative is harder. In 1953 the JCS barred public statements tying the raids to pressure for an agreement, so that Communist prestige would not get "seriously engaged." Crane's verdict is that the more the raids aimed at a political settlement, "the less this could be admitted" in public. A hold whose real purpose cannot be said out loud will struggle to win a renewal vote. Shaun's open-seas story is the right fix, and Crane suggests the pressure to go quiet will come from Washington, not Tehran.

Our take: Crane supports the hold and doubts the institution that has to run it. Two questions for the draft:

  1. Which of the five preparations survives a budget year in which the Pacific wants every drone and every controller?
  2. Can the public story stay "open seas" while the hold is also squeezing Tehran toward a settlement, or will officials have to say less, as the JCS did in 1953? (S)
The Readings So Far · Zhang, Crane, Nguyen, Sekirin, and Wasser

The Patron Holds the Pen

On this shelf, limited wars end when the patron changes its mind, and bombing rarely changes the patron's mind. Today's Counterpoint made the case for Korea: a Soviet resolution on March 19, 1953, and Mao giving way because Moscow tied his Five-Year Plan aid to ending the war. Nguyen shows the patrons working the other way in 1973. Zhou Enlai told Le Duc Tho that "The most important [thing] is to let the Americans leave," because the situation would change in six months or a year.

Yesterday's Counterpoint on Linebacker II and today's on the dam raids share a shape. In both, the heaviest bombing came late and took credit for a decision that was really made in another capital. (Twice is a pattern. Three times and it goes in a doctrine manual.) Crane's Air Force wrote the Korea story as "the decisive force," and Clodfelter's air leaders later held up Linebacker II as proof that bombing works in limited war. Each story put the bombers at the center of a decision the patron had already made.

Iran sits in this web as both patron and supplier. Sekirin's Ukrainians designed their long-range drones to match Iran's Shahed 136, which Russia renamed and fires at Ukraine. Wasser opens with an Iranian Shahed 129 firing on American advisers and their Syrian partners near al-Tanf in June 2017. Wednesday's news item on Colombia's drone war ended on the same point: find the network and the supplier before the drone flies. Today's item on the MQ-9 fleet shows the American side of that ledger.

Our take: before asking whether airpower can coerce, ask who actually decides on the other side, and who keeps them supplied. Korea's answer was Moscow, Vietnam's was Hanoi with Beijing and Moscow advising patience, and a Hormuz hold will have to answer it for Tehran. Bombing the client to move the patron is how you get a very expensive return address. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 5, Day 4 · the thesis and the argument

Just One More Thing: Say Whodunit First

Put the thesis in the first paragraph, because suspense belongs in the evidence. Zachary Shore, in Grad School Essentials, calls this the Columbo Principle. Every episode of the old detective show let viewers watch the murder in the first few minutes, and the fun was watching Columbo work it out. His instruction is blunt: "Don't keep your readers guessing what your thesis is." The tension, he writes, comes from watching how you reached a surprising answer.

He gives formulas. In the first, the opening sentence is the question, the next sentences narrow it, and the last sentence of the paragraph answers it. In the second, the first sentence is the thesis itself, and the rest of the paragraph explains the question and why it matters. Either way, he says, the question should not have an obvious answer, or you must show quickly why the obvious answer is wrong.

Before: "This paper will examine the role of airpower in the Korean War armistice negotiations." (It names a topic and makes no claim. Thoroughness is not a thesis.) After, using the first formula: "Did air pressure end the Korean War? General Weyland thought so, and the Air Force published a book calling airpower the decisive force in Korea. The timing says otherwise. Moscow resolved to end the war on March 19, 1953, two months before the dam raids began, so the armistice followed Moscow's calendar, not FEAF's." A reader now knows whodunit and wants to see the proof.

An old editor's trick: put the last sentence of your first paragraph next to the first sentence of your conclusion. If they say different things, one of them is wrong, and it's usually the introduction, written before you knew what you thought. A paper that saves its thesis for page nine is a mystery novel, and your grader has already read the last page. (S)

Reaping What We Didn't Build

The Air Force has lost about a quarter of its MQ-9 fleet in the Iran war and cannot buy enough to replace them. Greg Hadley reports in Air & Space Forces Magazine that the White House budget office has asked Congress for exceptions in the next stopgap funding bill, due by December 11, including $5 billion for Golden Dome and authority to help rebuild the Reaper fleet. At least 45 MQ-9s have been lost since the Iran war began in February, and at least a dozen more went down fighting Yemen in 2025. General Atomics can provide fewer than ten Reapers from what it has left, about five of them built from parts on hand. The request says that "Recent attrition in multiple theaters has severely degraded this capability." Its low-cost replacement, the Massed Modular Aircraft, is still years from production at scale.

Sekirin explains the problem in one word: discardability. He lists it as one of the drone's four defining traits, and he says it comes from dramatically lower prices for mass-produced drones, which let them take on missions that are expected to lose a lot of airframes. The Reaper was never built that way. It is a drone you can lose but cannot replace, an expensive aircraft without a pilot. Sekirin warns that money spent on exquisite hardware is wasted if cheap drones can quickly destroy it, and he notes that Russia alone turns out around one million new drones a year. (General Atomics, for comparison, has about five Reapers in the parts bin.)

He also warns against the easy fix. Cheap drones do not make a cheap force: building a commanded, jam-resistant drone network "will not come cheaply," he writes, and paying for it means cutting tanks, artillery, and even fighter jets. Fuller once called the drone war relatively cheap in American lives and cash. (Cheap, it turns out, depends on who is shooting back.)

Opinion: Congress should grant the Reaper exception, because units in the field need the coverage now, but the real lesson is about production. A fleet that can't be replaced at its rate of loss is no longer discardable, and so commanders will start to ration it. For Shaun's Hormuz hold, this is preparation (a) failing its first test in real life: a cost per kill means little if you can't buy the next airframe. In plain terms, the cheap part of a drone war is the drone you can replace, and right now the United States can't replace this one. (S)

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
