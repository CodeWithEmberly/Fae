Next Reading · for Monday, November 30

Sekirin, Rise of the Machines: The Drone Is the New Tank, and the Tank Would Like a Word

Illya Sekirin argues that the drone is the new tank, and that the army that reorganizes around it will win the next war. Massed drones, he says, can win control of a stretch of front and open corridors for ground forces to advance. He calls that the central tenet of the book.

He is arguing against two groups. The first is Ukraine's High Command, which he says spread its drones along the front and ignored his plans to mass them. The second is Western defense establishments, which he says treat drones as one lesson from Ukraine among many. He also aims at this course's home team: even complete control of the skies, he writes, would not demonstrably decide the war on land.

We think he wins the close fight and has not proved the breakthrough. His evidence that drones punish concentration is strong. His evidence that massed drones restore decisive maneuver is mostly a set of plans nobody ran. For a course that has spent eight weeks watching airmen promise that the next technology changes everything, that gap is the seminar. (Douhet, at least, did not attach his memos.) (S)

The World Around the Book

The Appendix Says "I Told You So" in Eight Parts

The author. Illya Sekirin is a participant arguing his own case, and that is the first thing to know about this book. He is a Ukrainian-born Canadian who joined the Ukrainian army about a month after the invasion. He served as a soldier, an interpreter for Western volunteers, a General Staff translator and researcher, a drone pilot, and finally an adviser to the High Command. He says Ukraine took the step of creating its Unmanned Systems Forces on February 6, 2024, largely because of a memo he sent on January 3. (Few authors get to review their own reorganization.) The access is real, because he prints the replies from senior officials. The stake is just as real. His stated goals include presenting the theories he urged on the High Command and explaining his personal role.

The moment. He wrote while the war was running and while Ukraine's Western aid was, in his words, depleting. The copyright is 2025, the Kursk story runs through March 2025, and Helion published in January 2026. Two arguments were on his desk. Inside Ukraine, he had lost much of the fight over whether to bet the army on drones in early 2024. In the West, analysts were listing drones as one lesson among many, and he calls that failing to answer the exam question. Poland's $6.2 billion order for 180 more K2 tanks in March 2025 is his exhibit for an alliance rearming for the previous war.

The reception. The reception is friendly and thin, and it has not tested the big claim. Robert McKie's April 2026 review on a New Zealand army logistics history site strongly recommends the book and reads it as a study of logistics that can no longer hide. The publisher quotes the Journal of the Air Force Historical Foundation calling it a timely and ambitious contribution. We could not open that review, and we found no critical scholarly review as of tonight. The admirers are right about the tactical reporting. No review we found has audited the counterfactual at the center of the book, which is that his plan would have worked. He invites the audit himself on page 198, where he says his reasoning should be criticized. Take him up on it.

The subtext. Our read: this is a vindication brief addressed to Western defense ministries, arguing that Kyiv's generals ignored the right answer and that NATO should now buy it, preferably with Ukraine as the supplier. This is our interpretation and not his stated aim. Three pieces of evidence carry it.

  1. The book's back end is his own paperwork. Eight appendices of his memos and plans run from page 203 to the end, about a fifth of the book, and the map captions pair his "predicted" operations with the "actual" ones.
  2. The audience is built in. The project grew out of articles for the British Army's Centre for Historical Analysis and Conflict Research, whose statement appears in the front matter, and a retired major general's foreword urges readers to make force design choices accordingly.
  3. The recommendations have a customer and a vendor. Page 199 proposes Ukraine as Europe's postwar drone industrial base, and page 202 tells NATO to pay for it by scrapping or shrinking tanks, artillery, and even fighter jets.

None of that makes him wrong. A man can have an axe and still be right about the tree. In plain terms, read his battlefield reporting as testimony and his "had they listened to me" passages as advocacy. (S)

Bridge From the Last Seminar · Wasser to Sekirin

An F-15E, a Missile, and a Drone Worth Rather Less

Wasser's coalition could afford to shoot drones down one expensive missile at a time, and Sekirin's war cannot. On June 8, 2017, near al-Tanf in Syria, an F-15E Strike Eagle shot down an armed Iranian-made Shahed 129 with an AIM-120C. Twelve days later, another F-15E shot down a second one. Sekirin reports that Russia and Ukraine now each produce more than one million drones a year. (The Strike Eagle crews at al-Tanf were not, to be fair, solving for that.) Keep one question in mind while reading: what did Wasser's coalition have that Ukraine lacks, and is it the airspace, the budget, or the enemy's restraint? (S)

Political Strategy × Sekirin · via electoral-vote.com, November 23 and 25 (no Punchbowl AM today)

Contractors Get a Gag Order. Sekirin Ordered a Glass Box.

The White House has told federal contractors to stop answering Congress, and that matters for anyone who wants to buy drones the way Sekirin does. Electoral-vote.com reports that Executive Order 14438 directs private companies with federal contracts to defy congressional subpoenas unless the White House approves. Companies that comply can have their contracts canceled. The stated reason is that compliance might leak sensitive information. (Punchbowl took the holiday off, and EV says it is back Monday, so this item runs on EV's Monday and Wednesday pages.)

The order lands just before a new House takes over. EV projects 260 Democratic seats to 175 Republican after the last runoffs. Rep. Jamie Raskin, who EV says will chair House Judiciary, has already warned officials not to delete messages or records. EV also reports that White House Counsel Will Scharf told agencies that no federal employee may testify under subpoena without his office's approval. EV's forecast is a long court fight that the administration hopes will outlast its term. That forecast is EV's opinion, and no court has ruled.

Sekirin's recipe for a drone force depends on exactly the kind of scrutiny now in dispute. In chapter 4, page 59, his ninth recommendation says the private sector develops and mass-produces drones better than militaries do. He adds that Ukraine's bureaucrats are "graft-prone and ineffective." His fix is competition among private firms, with transparency and public scrutiny in procurement welcomed. Two items earlier on the same page, he says funding the drone branch may mean cutting other programs. As we read it, his model moves money from a few big programs to many small, fast vendors, and counts on outside eyes to keep them honest.

Our take: Sekirin's speed and his scrutiny are one package, and Washington is arguing about the second half. A drone-heavy force means more vendors on faster contracts, with less time for internal review, so outside review is worth more. The dispute is between two branches over oversight, and courts will settle it on their own schedule. Who wins matters less for this book than how long it takes. A procurement system built for speed with its auditors tied up in court is the system Sekirin was trying to escape. In plain terms, if America copies Ukraine's drone buying, it should copy the glass walls too. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Sekirin, Rise of the Machines

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, preface and chapters 1 and 2

The Wedding Drone Crashes the Party

A weapon changes war only after an army reorganizes around it, and that is the test to hold Sekirin to. His opening argues that drones matter as much as the tank did, that organization has lagged the hardware, and that massing drones would restore maneuver. We think he proves the lag and only asserts the cure.

The preface establishes his access. In January 2024 his memo reached the top of Kyiv, and Deputy Prime Minister Mykhailo Fedorov texted back, "I agree with everything 100%." (A minister's text proves the memo arrived. It proves nothing about the memo.) Sekirin also reports that Defense Minister Oleksii Reznikov once dismissed the Mavic 3 as a "wedding drone."

Chapter 1 supplies the price tag. A Mavic 3 ran about $2,000. From July to December 2024, one Ukrainian charity says it supplied enough interceptors to shoot down 292 Russian fixed-wing drones, a count from the group that supplied them. Chapter 2 supplies the ledger. Sekirin names four traits, omnipresence, accurate fires, discardability, and mobility, then says the state met no more than 20 percent of what drone units needed and only 5 percent of their Mavics. He cites nothing for either percentage.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 26, Sekirin says 'the drone is the new tank,' and on page 39 Ukraine used only 10 percent of its drones at Kursk when he urged 50 to 70. So the blitz works, and Kyiv refused to run it." That treats his belief about an unexecuted plan as a result. A plan nobody ran has never had the chance to fail.

A stronger response: Concede that the character of fighting has changed. On page 31, many Russian assault units prefer motorcycles, scooters, and golf carts to armor because mobility now beats protection. Then look at page 26, where his tank lesson is itself a counterfactual: had the Allies built corps-size mechanized formations, Germany may have surrendered sooner. Page 39 has the same shape. The tank theory earned its reputation when armies ran it in the Second World War, and the drone blitz has had no such trial. On page 28 he even grants that massed drones make it hard to concentrate armor, artillery, and infantry for any attack. Our position: drones have changed what an army can hide, and whether they restore the breakthrough remains unproved.

That position holds up through three orders of effect. First, cheap sensors made the front transparent. Second, concentration became dangerous, and Ukraine spread its drones along a front of 1,200 kilometers. Third, and this link is our inference, a Western army faces two ways to get this wrong: reorganize around an untested theory, or repeat the wedding joke. It credits the change without leaning on the counterfactual.

A question for the room: Turn to page 39, where Sekirin blames the limited gains at Kursk on committing 10 percent of Ukraine's drones. What evidence, short of Ukraine running his full plan, would convince us he is wrong? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 3

Sekirin Springs His Own Trap

Test a war plan by running the enemy's best move against it. Chapter 3 lets us do that with Sekirin's own material, and his drone blitz struggles to survive. He argues that breaking a peer's lines now requires making the drone the centerpiece of the force, with every other arm built around it. We think the design is serious and unproven. He calls drone-led deep operations a "promising yet untested concept," which is more modest than the pages around it.

The design is admirably specific. A brigade of at least 1,300 drone and robot crews strikes a 50-kilometer stretch of front. The ground attack covers 14 kilometers, and the breakthrough point measures just four square kilometers. Robots go first, to draw fire and unmask defenders. Humans follow once the drone commander, who outranks the ground commander in this scheme, is satisfied the zone is clear. Every one of those numbers is a planning figure.

The big scenario is bolder. Four unmanned brigades and seven mechanized brigades drive 300 kilometers, pivot west at Volnovakha, and pin two Russian force groupings against the Dnipro. Envelopment takes about four days and mopping up finishes within 10. (The Russians in this scenario are very accommodating.) His history is fair: deep operations did work in 1944, when Operation Bagration destroyed 28 of the 34 divisions in German Army Group Center. Those were tank armies, and this plan has yet to leave the page.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 43, Sekirin shows Ukraine squandered Kursk by fanning out when it should have turned west toward the Seym. So the blitz is sound, and the failure was execution." The cost of dispersal is a fair reading. The success of the western turn is a guess, because nobody made it.

A stronger response: Concede page 43, since scattering the force gave Russia time to block the advance. Then turn to page 41, where massing armor within 25 kilometers of the front invites attack. Page 49 needs seven mechanized brigades anyway. Page 43 says drones are small enough to hide, which helps the drones and leaves the brigades in plain view. Pages 41 and 42 describe a "drone trap" that cuts off an advancing force with massed drones, flanking fire, and hidden robots. Page 50 accepts a long, narrow corridor as a calculated risk, and that corridor is the trap's ideal customer. Page 52 names the right test: start with shallower operations.

The effects run in order. First, massing for a breakthrough creates a visible target. Second, a defender who sees it can let the spearhead in and cut its route, as his own scenario shows. Third, and this link is our inference, Ukraine would lose brigades it cannot spare, and his fictional trap ends with protests at home and a retreat. Sekirin wrote nearly every link himself, so his defenders have to argue with him.

A question for the room: Turn to page 50, where drones are expected to "annihilate any enemy within a 20-kilometre radius." If AI made that true for both sides, would the blitz get easier, or would the defender's trap simply get better? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 4

Good Brigades Make Good Drone Walls

Drones favor the defender only when the defender is organized, so check the unit before crediting the weapon. Sekirin argues in chapter 4 that drones have done more for defense than offense in Ukraine, and we think he has the direction right. His own evidence shows the advantage belongs to well-run brigades, a less marketable and more useful finding.

Ukraine has about 126 formations along 1,200 kilometres: 114 brigades, seven regiments, and five separate battalions. A typical brigade holds 10 kilometres. A well-organized one flies 10 to 30 crews by day and as many by night, or one to three drones per kilometre. Even Russian assault teams of two to five soldiers can't get close. In a badly run brigade, surveillance is sporadic, fires arrive late, and attack drones sit on standard frequencies the Russians have learned to jam. Sekirin says the bad units outnumber the good ones. He offers no tally, so treat that as a witness talking. (Ukrainian jammers, often privately funded and switched on at will, also take down Ukrainian drones. The enemy appreciates the help.)

The claim you'll hear: "On page 55, Sekirin says drones have been more beneficial to the defensive than the offensive, and Russian gains are measured in infantry strides. So drones have made defense the dominant form of war." That skips the paragraph just above it, where Russian teams infiltrate up to 10 kilometres by the summer of 2025. Sekirin blames thin Ukrainian lines, with infantry in most brigades down to 20 to 40 percent of strength. A result that depends on how many riflemen remain can't be credited to the drone alone.

A stronger response: Concede the wall. On page 54, Sekirin describes a drone belt up to 10 kilometres deep that has become a no-man's land, and that is good testimony. Then go to page 56, where one to three drones per kilometre describes only brigades with good organization, and page 57, where weak brigades break and expose their neighbors. Opinion: drones raise the price of attacking, and organization decides who gets to charge it. Set aside the line on page 55 that drone blitz tactics could enable large-scale maneuver, which is a prediction.

The effects chain together. First order, a well-run drone sector stops small assault groups before they reach the forward positions. Second order, the Russians go through the weak brigade next door and reach the strong brigade's flank, and the no-step-back policy holds that strong unit in place at risk of encirclement. Third order, and this link is our inference, a front becomes about as strong as its worst-run brigades, so a country that buys drones without fixing command gets less defense than its production numbers suggest. Every link but the last comes from Sekirin.

A question for the room: Turn to page 55, where Sekirin credits drones with helping the defense and blames deep Russian infiltration on thin Ukrainian infantry. If a drone defense still needs enough soldiers to hold the ground, how much of the defensive advantage belongs to the drone? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 5

The Drone Brigade Is Undefeated on Paper

Before you trust a force ratio, ask whether both forces exist and whether they were counted the same way. Chapter 5 is Sekirin's blueprint for Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces, from a 12-soldier squad to a full brigade. He openly calls it an ideal. The trouble starts when the ideal goes to war against a real Russian regiment and wins.

The design has real merit. A squad flies one surveillance drone and five attack drones, with two operators per aircraft. Electronic warfare sits inside the drone units, which answers the friendly jamming in chapter 4. The full brigade totals 1,152 drone systems, 180 ground robots, and 3,500 to 5,000 troops. His opponent is real. Russia's 7th unmanned systems regiment, established by April 2024, began with 1,342 personnel, 41 attack units, and 61 reconnaissance units. That count comes from the Institute for the Study of War.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 66, Sekirin shows three of his brigades would outnumber a Russian drone regiment at least 15 to one, destroy it, and encircle a group of 100,000. Kyiv ignored a winning plan." That treats an unexecuted plan as a result. His sentence opens with a condition, "If the Ukrainians fully adhere to my recommendations." These brigades have never fought, and the regiment stays frozen at its starting strength on a page that says the Russians "are not standing still."

A stronger response: Concede the diagnosis. On page 65, most of Ukraine's 400 or so drone groups sit outside the Unmanned Systems Forces, a fair complaint. Then check the arithmetic on pages 65 and 66. He calls the regiment four to five times smaller than his brigade. By his figures, the brigade has 2.6 to 3.7 times the people and about 11 times the systems. Three brigades at 180 robots make 540, short of his 600, unless offensive brigades' extra robots fill the gap. His 15 to 50 range holds at about 34 to one if the units match. They may not. On page 64, one FPV system can include up to 30 drones, and the regiment's "units" go undefined. Opinion: keep the org chart and discard the scoreboard. A fielded brigade beating a Russian force that reacts would change our mind.

The forecast weakens with each step. First order, three brigades massed against one regiment produce a lopsided count. Second order, and this link is our inference, that mass becomes a target, because chapter 4 says massed offensive weapons typically end up destroyed. Third order, also our inference, Russia adapts before the ratio matters, since Sekirin reports new capabilities reaching combat almost fortnightly. A force ratio has a shelf life, and this one was printed before the enemy got a vote. All of it comes out of his own book.

A question for the room: Turn to page 68, where Sekirin wants the Unmanned Systems Forces to have standing equal to the army, navy, and air force. Airmen have heard that pitch before. What would a drone force have to prove in combat before independence beats integration with the units it supports? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapters 6 and 7

The Jammers Took Lunch. The Howitzers Did Not.

In 2022, drones made Ukraine's old weapons faster, and the old weapons still did the killing. That is our read of chapters 6 and 7, and Sekirin's own evidence supports it. What he counts is a defense carried by volunteers and artillery. What he witnessed is a quadcopter finding targets. The idea that drones could have decided the year is his prediction.

Chapter 6 opens on a country unready for a war its allies had described in advance. About 80 percent of Ukraine's battle-ready brigades sat in the Donbas, and not one bridge was mined before the invasion. At Hostomel, some 200 young supply troops met around 400 elite Russian paratroopers. Kyiv then raised 32 Territorial Defense brigades with more than 100,000 volunteers. In Sekirin's own battalion, about one man in five had a weapon. (The others drilled with planks and sticks, a procurement strategy of last resort.)

Chapter 7 supplies the scene. Near Barvinkove in July 2022, Sekirin's team was told that Russian jamming usually eased between 10 and noon, which he credits to a unit "partial to a long lunch break." On July 22, a Mavic 3 used that window, found about a dozen armored vehicles in the village of Mala Komyshuvakha, and streamed them over Starlink to an artillery officer. One or two M777 howitzers broke up the tank company.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 100, one Mavic breaks up a Russian tank company, so drones had already changed the character of war by the summer of 2022." It falls short because the Mavic found that company and the artillery broke it.

A stronger response: Concede that the kill chain got shorter and much cheaper. Then go to page 98, where Sekirin says his team had no FPV attack drones in 2022, and page 102, where Scala's kills came "mostly with artillery." On page 121, his own after-action review says platoon drones need a link to fires, and warns, "You can only make a drone-coordinated surprise attack once." Pages 106 and 107 credit the Kharkiv breakthrough to a brief edge in numbers against thin Russian lines. His argument on page 109, that trapping the Russians at Izium might have won the war, describes a battle nobody fought, so it proves nothing.

The chain makes that answer hard to beat. First order, drones cut the time between seeing a target and shelling it. Second order, Russia adapted: at Pravdyne that October, jamming and spoofing nearly stole Sekirin's drone, and Russian armor hid once its crews knew they were watched. Third order, and this is our inference, the advantage goes to whichever side keeps sensor, link, and gun working together. Kharkiv and Kherson cost Russia 457 tanks to Ukraine's 95, and still trapped no large Russian formation. A drone without a shell behind it is an expensive camera.

A question for the room: Turn to page 101, where Sekirin imagines 100 Mavics taking a four-kilometer front for about $250,000. If page 121 is right that surprise works once, what does that money buy on day two? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 8

Ukraine Won on Points, Twice, and Lost Both Rounds

An exchange ratio measures losses, and a campaign turns on what those losses cost each side. Chapter 8 demonstrates this twice with Sekirin's own numbers. His counts are the chapter's best evidence. His claim that a far larger drone fleet would have rescued 2023 is its weakest. He also says he did not take part in the counteroffensive, so that half is analysis without an eyewitness.

Bakhmut is the first case. Wagner signed about 50,000 convicts, trained them for 10 to 14 days, and spent them against the city. Early in the battle, Ukraine inflicted casualties at seven to one. The estimate Sekirin cites for the whole fight is 20,000 to 30,000 Russians killed and 60,000 to 80,000 wounded, against about 20,000 Ukrainian casualties, at least 5,000 of them killed. Russia took the city anyway on May 20, 2023. The trade was worse than it looks, because Wagner was burning "barely trained ex-convicts" while Ukraine lost some of its most experienced soldiers.

The 2023 counteroffensive repeated the pattern at larger scale. Ukraine advertised it for months. (Eight days before the attack, General Zaluzhny posted a video prayer for "our decisive offensive," a bold approach to operational security.) On June 4, 60,000 fresh recruits and some older formations attacked as many as 100,000 to 150,000 Russians dug in behind mines and fortifications. Most of the Ukrainians had two months of training or less. The final armor count favored Ukraine, with 499 Russian tanks lost to 179 Ukrainian. The Russian line held.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 145, Russia loses 499 tanks to Ukraine's 179, thanks to FPV drones, so a fleet 100 times larger would have broken the line." It falls short because it treats a kill count as a breakthrough and a rhetorical question as evidence.

A stronger response: Concede that the tank count is impressive, especially since page 145 says FPV use was not centrally planned. Those kills never opened the front. Page 144 shows Russia using Mavics, Lancets, and guided rockets against Ukrainian armor, so a hundredfold fleet assumes an enemy who stands still. Page 143 says Sekirin held his view before the first shot, yet page 138 dates his drone papers to late 2023, and the first memo in his appendices, on page 203, is dated November 3, five months after the attack began. He may have said it aloud earlier. The book shows no document.

The consequences explain why the ratio misled. First order, holding Bakhmut killed many Russians and cost Ukraine veterans. Second order, its best brigades needed rebuilding just before the offensive, which then leaned on 12 untested brigades. Sekirin argues part of that link, and the rest is our inference. Third order, pressing on after the failure degraded more brigades and left them exposed at Avdiivka that October. A ratio counts bodies at one moment, and this chain ran for over a year.

A question for the room: Turn to page 132, where Prigozhin treats Bakhmut as ground without strategic value and a way of bleeding Ukraine's army. If an attacker can accept the worse ratio and still get what he came for, what should a strategist measure instead? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 9

The Best Plan Avdiivka Never Saw

Grade an unfought plan on its inputs, because its record will always be perfect. Sekirin's Avdiivka counterstroke is the book's cleanest test. In January 2024 he proposed sending the 3rd Assault Brigade into the flank of the Russian northern pincer under 1,000 drone crews, instead of feeding it into the city. Nobody ever ordered it. We think his diagnosis was right and his forecast is unproven, and the chapter keeps blurring the two.

Sort the evidence into three piles. He counted, from open sources, around 40,000 Russians against 10,000 to 15,000 defenders, with up to 60 glide bombs a day. He witnessed the paper trail, including his own message at 7:59 p.m. on February 16 warning of catastrophe, about six hours before Syrsky ordered the withdrawal. He claims the payoff: panic, quick surrender, and a run at Donetsk. (The plan took him two days to write. Russia had already spent nearly four months on Avdiivka.)

The claim you'll hear: "On page 151, Sekirin shows a 100-to-one drone advantage over the northern pincer, so Syrsky threw away a winning move." That falls short because the ratio rests on his unsourced count of 10 Russian crews, and it assumes Russia would watch 2,000 operators assemble and do nothing. A counterfactual about your own plan is a hypothesis, however confidently it is typed.

A stronger response: Concede the diagnosis. Sending the best brigade into the ruins risked repeating Bakhmut, and on page 154 Azov's chief of staff said so loudly, though Sekirin had sent him the plan. Then check the author against himself. On page 151 he needs seven to 10 days to gather the force. His own January 15 memo, on pages 219 to 224, schedules 47 days of steps before the attack begins. The ministry's prototype unit on page 236 would shorten that, but the withdrawal order came 17 days after his January 31 plan. On page 154, Fedorov says the unit's order has "not been signed yet." The caption on page 155 upgrades that to Syrsky refusing.

The stronger answer holds up through three orders of effect. First, the brigade went into the city and the late withdrawal left hundreds of defenders cut off. Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 10

Surprise Won Kursk. The Drones Took a Bow.

When an operation succeeds, find out which ingredient did the work before crediting your favorite. Sekirin's favorite is the massed drone attack, and Kursk is where he credits it. We think the August 2024 breakthrough mainly demonstrates surprise, and that the real massed drone attack at Kursk arrived six months later, flown by Russia.

Ukraine crossed the border on August 6 with no more than 100 drone crews, against the 1,300 Sekirin wanted in one brigade. Within 24 hours it had opened a breach 10 to 11 kilometers wide and 15 deep through border guards and conscripts. Interceptor drones and jammers blinded Russian reconnaissance. By about August 11 the advance had stalled on every axis. In February 2025 Russia massed its own drones against the Sumy to Sudzha highway, many on fiber-optic cables that jammers cannot touch, flying from within seven kilometers of the road. In March, around 70,000 ground troops, 12,000 of them North Korean, collapsed the salient.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 175, Sekirin says the drone tactics he championed ensured early success at Kursk, so massed drones can now break a front." That falls short because page 165 puts the force at under a tenth of what he recommended, against unsuspecting border guards, and says the operation surprised him as much as anyone. Ukraine won the opening with a fraction of the ingredient he wants to credit.

A stronger response: Concede that drones mattered, since the jammers protected the surprise. Then quote him on page 164: "No degree of drone and electronic warfare technical superiority" can make up for losing it. Give him full credit for his best call. He warned on September 6 that Russia would hit Snagost, Russia did on September 10, and on page 173 Fedorov wrote, "It turned out you were right in your forecast." Discount the rest. On page 168 he says destroying the Russians in the Kursk region means "the war will be won," and the envelopment on page 169 targeted up to 3,000 border troops. (That is a lot to ask of 3,000 border troops.) His page 172 forecast of defeat in Kursk that autumn came true the following March. The 54,900 Russian casualties on page 178 come from Ukraine's General Staff, with no Ukrainian total beside them.

Follow the effects and the credit moves. First, surprise and blinded sensors opened the breach. Second, the surprise wore off, the fan of diverging attacks stalled, and Russian reserves arrived. Third, Ukraine's best brigades sat in a poor salient while Russia copied the massing and cut the road. Our inference is that drones multiply surprise and local mass, and give little to a plan missing both. Every link comes from Sekirin's own pages, which makes for an awkward afternoon for his admirers.

A question for the room: On page 176, Sekirin says Kursk diverted Russian reserves from the Donbas for seven months. On page 178, he says it left Ukraine's best brigades stuck in a bad salient and handed Russia the initiative. Did Kursk buy Ukraine time, or spend it? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapter 11

The Crystal Ball Worked Once

Grade a forecast and its remedy separately, because an adviser can read the enemy perfectly and still prescribe an untested cure. Chapter 11 shows Sekirin at his best as a forecaster and at his least provable as a planner. He called Russia's next move in writing. The two plans he built on that call never ran, so they can't count as evidence.

The forecast earns full credit. On August 29, 2024, Russia had massed 40,000 troops against 12,000 Ukrainians on the Pokrovsk axis, and Ukraine's General Staff expected an attack on Pokrovsk itself. Sekirin warned that the most dangerous move was a turn south by Group of Forces Centre. Russia made that turn and bypassed the reserves sent to block it. His dated memo sits in Appendix VII, which is how prophecy ought to be done.

His next forecast went badly. On October 28 he told Deputy Prime Minister Fedorov to expect three encirclements within weeks, at Kursk, Kupiansk, and Kurakhove. By New Year, he added, Russia would hold all of Donetsk, take Zaporizhzhia, and capture 100,000 prisoners. Kyiv refused his retreat, and the front did not collapse. He now calls the note hyperbolic and says he was "completely wrong about the timeframe." (In a forecast, the timeframe is a fairly load-bearing part.)

The claim you'll hear: "On page 181, Sekirin saw the Russian turn before anyone, and on page 254 he offered the counterstroke, so Ukraine lost southern Donbas because it ignored him." The first half is documented. The second is a counterfactual about a plan nobody executed.

A stronger response: Concede the diagnosis, and concede that holding was expensive. Page 183 counts desertion cases climbing from 5,181 in September to 15,500 in December. Then test the cure. On page 192 he says the only concentration possible in drone warfare is drones. Yet on page 180, Russia concentrated 40,000 soldiers and took most of Novohrodivka within days. On page 250, his own plan pulls whole assault brigades into a "strike fist" on that same battlefield, and page 254 promises a breakthrough to Prohres within one day. Opinion: it might have worked, and nobody can know. Page 185 credits the front's survival to stubborn soldiers, Ukraine's best drone units at Pokrovsk, and a competent new commander.

Why it's stronger: First order, Kyiv refused the retreat and reinforced Pokrovsk with its strongest drone and electronic warfare concentration. Second order, Kurakhove fell on January 6, 2025, with units trapped west of it, while the wider front held and Kyiv approved large drone formations that February, a year after he urged them. Third order, and this link is our inference, his lasting victory is institutional, since Ukraine adopted his drone organization while rejecting his map. That answer grants every fact in his favor and disputes only the step nobody observed.

A question for the room: Turn to page 182, where he admits exaggerating partly to get the High Command's attention. When an adviser inflates a warning and the disaster misses its deadline, what has he done to the credibility of his next true warning? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Sekirin, chapters 12 and 13

Clausewitz Survives the Quadcopter

The character of war has changed enormously in the close fight and much less above it, and its nature has not changed at all. That is our position. Character is how war gets fought: weapons, formations, tactics. Nature is what war is: organized violence, shaped by chance, serving politics, settled by a contest of wills. Sekirin proves the first kind of change and then writes as if it settles everything else.

Chapter 12 holds his best evidence. Russia has lost at least 3,812 tanks confirmed by photo or video, and Ukraine's General Staff estimates 10,400. Artillery no longer fires in batteries, because massed guns get found and hit. Those are a count and an observation, and they deserve respect. Cause is where he slides into claim. A loss tally records a dead tank without saying what killed it, and crediting drones "directly or indirectly" sweeps in every shell a drone spotted.

Chapter 13 moves from counting to predicting. Sekirin calculates that Russia could destroy Poland's 360 K2 tanks with $720,000 of FPV drones, at four $500 drones per tank. The multiplication is flawless. The budget omits jammers, pilots, and command networks, and he later concedes a force of millions will consume serious resources. His conquest of Poland is a forecast about an unfought war.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 198, Sekirin says 'unmanned systems are the question that needs answering,' so drones have rewritten war and NATO should stop buying tanks and fighters." That skips his own conclusions a few pages earlier.

A stronger response: Concede the close fight, because constant surveillance has made massed armor close to suicidal. Then read him against himself. On page 193, operational art still matters and drones can't replace it. On page 195, "The role of the infantry endures." Page 193 also says one drone brigade could have broken Russian lines in early 2024, and a year later he wants three, because Russia copied Ukraine. That is ordinary action and reaction, running fast. War's nature shows on page 189, with the will to fight, and page 194, with nuclear thresholds and face-saving peace terms. Opinion: pairing drones with AI would change who pulls the trigger and how fast, and leave intact why wars start and end. We would change our minds if autonomous systems began escalating faster than political leaders could stop them, because that would break war's link to policy, the strategist's real danger.

Why it's stronger: First order, drones punish whatever concentrates, so armor and guns disperse. Second order, both sides mass drones and the early advantage shrinks within a year. Third order, and this link is our inference, the war settles into attrition, where manpower and industry weigh heavily, the same factors chapter 13 demotes. His own evidence restores what his conclusion throws away.

A question for the room: Turn to page 199, where he compares buying tanks today to raising cavalry in the 1930s. Is the drone the tank of 1940, which restored decisive maneuver, or the machine gun of 1914, which made defense deadlier and rewarded whoever could outlast the other? (S)

Earlier Books, One Minute Each

Spaced Review · five earlier books against Sekirin's question

Drones Have Been the Future Since 1952

  1. Crane, Korea. The Air Force entered Korea with inflated expectations, could not reach the true sources of enemy power, and let an ambiguous armistice excuse it from the lessons. The link: in 1952 the Navy launched six radio-controlled Hellcat drones from the carrier Boxer. One hit an undefended bridge, one was ditched, and the rest missed because of enemy jamming or human error. Evaluators saw more promise in reconnaissance. Ask what Sekirin's FPV pilots have solved that those crews could not.
  2. Clodfelter, Vietnam. Bombing's value depends on the goals sought and the kind of war being fought. The link: in Linebacker II, SAC's long planning lead time locked in repeated routes until losses forced a change. Ukraine's drone war is a test of the same idea, that tactics must adapt faster than headquarters can plan.
  3. Haun, Vietnam. Airpower works best against fielded forces alongside a capable ground force, because it makes the enemy disperse and hide or die massed. The link: this is the closest thing in the course to Sekirin's battlefield. Haun also shows Assault Breaker, a sensor-to-missile idea, dying largely because the Air Force disliked a concept with no role for its strike aircraft. Watch for the Ukrainian version of that fight.
  4. Olsen, Desert Storm. The strategic air campaign degraded Iraq but did not achieve its unstated aim of changing the regime. The link: unmanned decoys on opening night drew out Iraqi radars for HARMs to kill. Cheap, expendable aircraft were already doing serious work in 1991, and the war's political result still depended on something else.
  5. Wasser, Inherent Resolve. Airpower was indispensable because it enabled partner ground forces, and those partners set the pace. The link: in 2017, MQ-1s and MQ-9s flew more than 12,000 sorties and demand still outran supply. Sekirin's answer to that shortage is mass. Ask what mass costs when the enemy can jam it and shoot it down by the thousand.

Opinion: every one of these authors found the machine impressive and the strategy harder. Sekirin has to be the exception to earn his title. (S)

The Bedtime Version

A Wren in the Works

One summer, Big Bear decided the whole berry bank at Quiet Pond was his. He came padding around the water, very big and very sure.

Little Otter lived on the berry bank. She could not push a bear. So she asked the fireflies for help.

The fireflies were tiny, and there were so many.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Nobody could hide.

A little wren watched from a reed and wrote a note. "Put all the fireflies in one big glow," said the note. "Then Otter can walk right through."

Otter's elders read the note. "Maybe," they said. "Not today." They spread the fireflies out, a few here and a few there.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Nobody could hide.

Soon Big Bear found fireflies of his own. Now the wren said Otter would need three big glows. Bear stayed on his side. Otter stayed on hers. The berries sat in the middle.

Juniper the small owl floated down from the sky. "Would the big glow have worked?" asked the wren.

"Nobody knows, little one," said Juniper. "Lights can show us the whole pond. They can't tell us how to share it."

The wren tucked away the note. Bear yawned. Otter yawned.

Blink, blink, went the fireflies. Everyone slept.

For the grown-up: Big Bear is Russia, Little Otter is Ukraine, the fireflies are cheap drones, and the wren is Sekirin with his memos. One big glow is his massed drone attack, which Kyiv did not adopt in time, and by his own count one brigade became three once Russia copied Ukraine. Juniper's answer is our verdict: drones changed what armies can see, and the plan that would have proved more was never run. (S)

Recall

Chapter recall

  • What share of Ukraine's drones does Sekirin say were used at Kursk, and what share did he recommend?

    Only 10 percent, against his recommended minimum of 50 and up to 70 percent. His claim that more would have won more is a belief about a plan never run. S 39

  • What are the dimensions of Sekirin's drone blitz, from front to breakthrough point?

    A 50-kilometer stretch of front, a 14-kilometer ground attack, and a breakthrough point of four square kilometers, using a brigade of at least 1,300 crews. S 45

  • What drone density does Sekirin give for a well-organized Ukrainian brigade, and what happens where organization is poor?

    A good brigade runs 10 to 30 crews by day and night, or one to three drones per kilometre. Poorly organized brigades let Russians break through and reach the flanks of stronger neighbors. S 56-57

  • What force ratio does Sekirin project for three of his brigades against a Russian drone regiment, and why should it be treated cautiously?

    He projects at least 15 to one, more likely 50 to one, in drones and 600 to zero in ground robots. The brigades are an unexecuted design, and the comparison holds the Russian regiment at its starting strength. S 65-66

  • What did the Mavic 3 do at Mala Komyshuvakha on July 22, 2022, and what did the damage?

    It found about a dozen armored vehicles and streamed them to an artillery officer. One or two M777 howitzers then broke up the tank company. S 99-100

  • What limit on drone surprise did Sekirin's own after-action review record at Pravdyne?

    A drone-coordinated surprise attack works only once, because the enemy then hides its armor and takes countermeasures. S 121

  • Why was Ukraine's favorable casualty ratio at Bakhmut worse than it looked?

    Wagner was spending barely trained ex-convicts while Ukraine lost some of its most experienced soldiers, and the ratio equalized once Ukrainian forces were nearly encircled. S 134

  • What is the problem with Sekirin's statement that he foresaw the 2023 failure before it began?

    He says so on page 143, but he dates his drone papers to late 2023, and his first appended memo is from November 3, 2023. S 138, 143, 203

  • How long did Sekirin say he needed to assemble the Avdiivka drone force, and what did his own January 15 memo schedule?

    Seven to 10 days for the Avdiivka plan, against 47 days of preparatory steps in the January memo, though Fedorov's prototype unit would have shortened that. S 151, 219-224

  • How many drone crews took part in the opening Kursk assault, and how many did Sekirin think a UAS brigade should have?

    No more than 100 crews, against the 1,300 he recommended, which is why the early breakthrough says more about surprise than about drone mass. S 165

  • What did Sekirin correctly predict on August 29, 2024?

    That Russia's Group of Forces Centre would turn south instead of attacking Pokrovsk directly, bypassing the reserves sent to stop it. S 181

  • What did Sekirin forecast on October 28, 2024, and how did it hold up?

    Three encirclements within weeks and the loss of Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia by New Year. The front did not collapse, and he admits he was completely wrong on timing. S 182

  • Why can't his Pokrovsk counterattack plan count as evidence for his theory?

    It was never executed, so its success is a counterfactual, and it required assembling whole assault brigades on a battlefield he says punishes concentration. S 250

  • What is the difference between the character and the nature of war?

    Character is how war is fought, through weapons, formations, and tactics. Nature is what war is: violence, chance, politics, and a contest of wills. Sekirin cites the distinction himself. S 157

  • What does Sekirin's $720,000 figure measure, and what does it leave out?

    The price of four $500 FPV drones for each of Poland's 360 K2 tanks. It leaves out the jammers, pilots, and command network needed to deliver them. S 198

  • Which Sekirin number best shows drone advantage eroding through adaptation?

    One UAS brigade could have broken Russian lines in early 2024, he says, but a year later he would want at least three large formations. S 193
