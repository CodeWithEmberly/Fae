Tonight's Reading · for Friday's seminar

Nguyen, Hanoi's War: The Enemy Had a Name, and It Wasn't Ho

Lien-Hang Nguyen argues that Le Duan, first secretary of the Party from 1960 until his death in 1986, ran North Vietnam's war, with Le Duc Tho as his right-hand man, and that Ho Chi Minh and Vo Nguyen Giap were pushed aside at nearly every key decision. She makes two larger claims on top of that. Leaders in Hanoi and Saigon, more than Washington, set the nature and pace of American intervention. And Hanoi won less through its three general offensives, all of which failed to spark the uprising they were built for, than through a world relations campaign that rallied antiwar movements and blunted Nixon's dealings with Moscow and Beijing. (The book's subtitle calls all this "the war for peace," a phrase only a diplomat could love.)

The book argues against two habits. American scholarship treats Washington's choices as human and contingent and Hanoi's victory as fate, and Nguyen says individuals in Hanoi shaped the war as much as historical structures did. Official Vietnamese histories, for their part, present a unified Party under a kindly Ho, and Nguyen shows purges, blackmail, and a police state behind that picture. Her sources are the reason to take her seriously: in 2003 she became the first scholar, Vietnamese or foreign, admitted to the Foreign Ministry archives, though the Party and military archives stay closed.

For this course, Nguyen is the view from the target. Clodfelter and Randolph measured the bombing from Washington and the cockpit. Nguyen shows what the Politburo was arguing about while the bombs fell, which is the only way to answer whether bombing moved the North's leaders, and which leaders. She is also harder on Hanoi's leadership than most American authors are on Washington's, which makes a pleasant change. (S)

  8. "Talking while fighting." Hanoi's policy from 1969 of negotiating in Paris while keeping military pressure on the South (ebook 213).
Bridge From Today · Randolph to Nguyen

Same 1972, Seen From the Other End of the Bomb

Randolph and Nguyen describe the same year from opposite sides, and they agree more than you might expect. Randolph says Nixon's air and naval response blunted the Easter Offensive and that Linebacker wiped out North Vietnam's small industry and 70 percent of its electrical capacity in two months. He also says the bombing was "a price the Politburo was willing to pay," and that Hanoi's total commitment let it reach its minimum aims and set up 1975. Nguyen supplies the Politburo's side of that sentence. On 1 June 1972 the leaders moved the nation's resources from the offensive in the South to the defense of the North, and by June they privately admitted the big-unit offensive had failed.

The two also agree on where air power did the most work. Randolph says its most powerful effect was blunting the offensive in the South, and Nguyen says American bombs and South Vietnamese counterattacks cut short any political struggle in the cities. Keep one question in mind while reading: if the Politburo was willing to pay the price, why did it change its strategy within a month of the mining? Nguyen's chapters 7 and 8 suggest the answer was the allies as much as the bombs. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Nguyen, Hanoi's War

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, introduction

The Man Who Ran Hanoi's War Let Uncle Ho Take the Photos

Before you analyze an enemy's strategy, find out who writes it. Nguyen's introduction names the author of Hanoi's. Le Duan held the top Party post from 1960 until his death in 1986, the longest reign in modern Vietnamese history, and Le Duc Tho was his right-hand man. Nguyen calls Le Duan the first among equals in the Politburo, hidden behind a "bamboo curtain" that has kept Hanoi's decision making out of view since the war. Her complaint about the scholarship is that it treats American choices as human and the Vietnamese victory as inevitable.

Le Duan helped hide himself. One journalist, writing at his death, said he cut a "bland figure," and he promoted collective leadership in the Party instead of a cult of personality. The sources were hidden too. The Party, military, and Foreign Ministry archives were closed to foreign and Vietnamese scholars alike. In 2003 Nguyen became the first researcher admitted to the Foreign Ministry archives, and she remains the only one (the line for the reading room is short). Her best find is a classified chronology of Hanoi's diplomacy that quotes the 1968 to 1973 telegrams between the Politburo and the delegation in Paris, which she calls the "holy grail." Where the files stay shut, she reads the sanitized Party document collection with the tools Kremlinologists used to read between the lines.

The weak seminar comment is "Ho and Giap outlasted the Americans." It is weak because it borrows the famous names without asking who decided anything; it repeats the picture Le Duan was happy to let the world see. The strong version is "Before we ask whether bombing broke Hanoi's will, ask whose will. Le Duan ran the Politburo from 1960, and Nguyen says he sidelined Ho and Giap at nearly every key decision." Opinion: with the Party and military archives still shut, some of her conclusions are inference wearing a confident suit, and saying so out loud will win more of the room than pretending otherwise.

First order, Le Duan monopolized decision making and, in Nguyen's account, blackmailed Ho into silence in 1963 and 1964, and in 1967 and 1968 Le Duc Tho arrested the deputies and friends of Giap and Ho. Second order, the man in charge bet three times, in 1964, 1968, and 1972, on a general offensive meant to spark a mass uprising that never came, over the objections of Ho, Giap, and the military brass. Third order, an enemy reading the war through Ho's face was reading the wrong man's mind; Nguyen ends the book saying that failing to see the war from the other side produced a faulty understanding of the enemy's war and of America's own. That chain pre-empts the easy reply that leadership names don't matter, because the same strategy failed three times under the same author. Nguyen herself wonders how the war would have gone had Ho and Giap been in charge. Le Duan would have preferred we never asked. (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 1

Hanoi Hired a Caretaker and Got a War Party

Whoever holds the drafting pen and the personnel office holds the strategy, and a leader who rises by selling a war will find that the war has become his credential. Nguyen argues that Le Duan did not wait for southern insurgents to drag Hanoi into war. He fanned the southern flames to push reluctant comrades in the North, and war gave a battered Party a cause to rally behind. The chapter's epigraph is his: "Revolution is offensive."

The personnel record shows how it happened. Land reform went so badly that in 1956 soldiers fired on their own people. Le Duc Tho ran the cleanup, Truong Chinh stepped down as first secretary, and Tho became the Party's organizational chief. Le Duan came back to Hanoi in 1957, and the old guard backed him because they thought he lacked a base of his own (a hiring decision that aged badly). Ho had given Giap the job of drafting Resolution 15, the decision on the South. In early 1958, most likely under pressure from Le Duan, Ho told Giap to hand it over. Le Duan and his deputy Pham Hung ran it through twenty-two more drafts, and he kept two southern emissaries locked away in Hanoi while he did, which is one way to manage an inbox. At the plenum of December 1958 and January 1959 he warned that the southern insurgency faced annihilation unless the Party acted. The unspoken message was that the South might act without the Party.

The weak seminar comment is "The southern insurgency forced Hanoi's hand." It is weak because it takes Le Duan's argument as the cause; it describes the pressure he used and misses that he was the one applying it. The strong version is "Le Duan took the pen from Giap and used the threat of losing control of the South as his argument. War in the South was his road to the top of the Politburo." Opinion: ask of any war plan who inside the government profits if the war happens.

First order, Resolution 15 committed the Party to overthrowing the Diem government by military as well as political means. Second order, in May 1959 the Party created Group 559 to run the supply route through the mountains, later known as the Ho Chi Minh Trail, and in September Group 959 to run routes through southern Laos. Northern resources began flowing south at the expense of the North's development, which is exactly what the North-first moderates had feared. Third order, Nguyen calls Resolution 15 the first stage of Le Duan and Le Duc Tho's campaign for total war, and says the two men engineered the greatest seizure of power in the Party's history. That chain pre-empts the reply that Hanoi merely reacted to Diem: Nguyen shows the southern crisis was real and that Le Duan used it to win a fight in Hanoi. In March 1959 he assured the moderates that the Party would not use war to unify the country unless the enemy used war first. The Politburo wanted a caretaker with no base. It got a man whose base was the war.

A question for the room: if the war was Le Duan's credential, what would an early negotiated peace have cost him personally? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 2

Le Duan Won the Debate by Arresting the Other Side

Internal security is an instrument of war policy, so when a regime escalates, check whom it arrests before counting what it builds. Nguyen argues that from 1960 to 1963 Le Duan and Le Duc Tho turned the Party Secretariat and the Ministry of Public Security into one machine and aimed it at the North-first moderates, who wanted to build socialism at home before fighting for the South. The bigger war needed a quieter Hanoi.

The proof is the Ninth Plenum of December 1963. Truong Chinh asked Hoang Minh Chinh, a Soviet-trained theoretician, to draft the Party line, and Chinh urged neutrality between Moscow and Beijing. Truong Chinh had no intention of recommending it (he had read the room, and the room had a police force). By Hoang Minh Chinh's later account, nearly half the Politburo agreed with him anyway. The hardliners used threats and blackmail and gave Ho Chi Minh the option of "following the Politburo line or standing aside." In January 1964, 400 cadres studying Resolution 9 heard that its most important part could not be written down. It is hard to dissent from a paragraph that does not exist. Bui Cong Trung soon lost his seat and his job, and Duong Bach Mai died under "mysterious circumstances."

The weak seminar comment is "Hanoi was a disciplined monolith." It is weak because it is true on the surface and ignores the author's own counterevidence: the unity was produced by arrests, and about fifty mid-ranking cadres wrote letters against Resolution 9 before it passed. The strong version is "Hanoi looked unified because Le Duan made dissent a security matter, and the go-for-broke strategy that came out of the Ninth Plenum brought the Americans in." Opinion: manufactured unity works fine until the plan fails, and then nobody is left to say so.

First order, Resolution 9 committed the Party to a general offensive and general uprising, a plan to topple the fragile Saigon regime in 1964 without the long war the Viet Minh had fought against France. Second order, the gamble failed. Nguyen calls the February 1965 attack on Pleiku, which McGeorge Bundy likened to a streetcar, a product of Le Duan's go-for-broke strategy, and the marines landed at Da Nang in March. Mao, meanwhile, was willing to fight the Americans down to the last Vietnamese. Third order, the North paid. By late 1965 Rolling Thunder had flown 25,000 sorties over the North with little effect on infiltration, but it disrupted socialist development, emptied factories that didn't serve the war, and moved city people to the countryside. The moderates' plan for the northern economy was squandered in the war they had argued against. Le Duan had an answer even for failure: if city uprisings ran into trouble, his forces would practice, learn, and try again later. That chain pre-empts the reply that the purge was a sideshow to strategy. The purge was the strategy's precondition. Nothing builds consensus like a reeducation camp, right up until the plan meets an enemy you cannot arrest.

A question for the room: if nearly half the Politburo agreed with Hoang Minh Chinh, what does the phrase "Hanoi decided" mean in any sentence about 1964? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 3

Le Duan Tested His Tet Assumptions by Jailing the Doubters

Before approving a plan, write down what it assumes, then ask who is still allowed to say an assumption is wrong. Official Vietnamese histories treat Tet as a shrewd response to American failure and little else. Nguyen adds the Politburo feud and the police: Le Duan revived the General Offensive and General Uprising, which had failed in 1964, and cleared its path with the largest purge in Party history.

The assumptions were bold. In mid-1967 Le Duan declared that the United States could not maintain its current troop level, and he read the 1966 uprisings in Da Nang as proof that the southern cities were ready to burn. Giap had warned that the army needed to build up before attacking the cities, and in July 1967 Ho questioned whether the plan was realistic and urged preparation for a long war. Giap then left for Eastern Europe and Ho for Beijing. The security forces arrested critics in three waves, catching General Staff officers Nguyen Van Vinh and Le Trong Nghia in October, just after they briefed the Politburo on the offensive. The stated reason was protecting surprise. When the plan went to the Fourteenth Plenum in January 1968, Ho abstained, as he had on Resolution 9, and watched the revolution proceed without him.

The weak seminar comment is "Tet was a bold gamble that paid off politically." It is weak because it scores the outcome and skips the decision; it credits Le Duan with a result his own plan did not aim for. The strong version runs the check: for each assumption, name who doubted it and say where that person was in January 1968. If the answer is Hoa Lo prison, the assumption was never tested. Opinion: American bombing suited Le Duan's security men nicely, since it fed the spy fever they used to justify the arrests.

First order, the purge removed the doubters, and secrecy gave southern commanders little more than two weeks to prepare. Second order, calendar discrepancies sent some central provinces into the attack a day early, which gave away the surprise the arrests were meant to protect, while other headquarters got their orders too late. The uprising did not come. Third order, Le Duan drew the wrong lesson: he decided his 1964 strategy had worked and the Americans had snatched victory at the last moment. General Tran Do later said the Party had thrown in all its forces, had nothing left for the counteroffensive, and fell into a critical situation in 1969, 1970, and 1971. Nguyen says it took three years to recover. And Le Duan tried the same strategy again in 1972. That chain pre-empts the defense that Tet was a strategic victory, because the planners' own aim was a mass uprising, and the people who said it wouldn't come were in jail when it didn't. Le Duan guarded the secret so well that some of his own units did not get it in time.

A question for the room: if the security ministry was arresting the officers who briefed the plan, who was left to tell Le Duan that the cities would not rise? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 4

Hanoi Got Serious in Paris When It Ran Out of Offensives

In any negotiation fought alongside a war, lay the diplomatic calendar over the battle calendar, because a side that is still attacking is only probing at the table. Nguyen argues that Le Duan accepted talks after Johnson's 31 March 1968 speech because he had no choice, then kept betting on a general uprising. Le Duc Tho stated the house view in the chapter's epigraph: "Diplomacy could never replace military strength."

Hanoi accepted preliminary talks on 3 April. On 4 May it hit 119 bases, towns, and cities in a second wave, and the first Paris session met on 13 May. Moscow, which had helped find the venue, was dismayed (allies love surprises). The public meetings were for probing only. In September Tho lectured Averell Harriman on American intervention for nearly an hour, which is one way to run out a clock. His private cables told another story. Tho's 28 September telegram, two days before the third wave ended, sketched a real negotiating plan, and Nguyen concludes that the Politburo took diplomacy seriously only after its forces were spent. Tho's secretary, Luu Van Loi, described a war fought around a green carpet while bombs still exploded on the battlefield.

The weak seminar comment is "Tet shocked both sides into peace talks." It is weak because it treats one event as the cause and ignores the calendar in the author's own chapter: Hanoi kept attacking for five months after it said yes. The strong version is "Hanoi said yes in April, attacked 119 targets in May, and drafted its first serious plan two days before the offensive ended on 30 September." Opinion: run the check on your own side too.

First order, the second and third waves cost Hanoi the political capital it had won in the first, and nearly destroyed its southern infrastructure. Second order, on 10 October the Politburo hardened its demands again, and the Paris delegation sent Tho home to talk his bosses down. On the way, in Beijing, Chen Yi lectured him, and Tho answered that reality would give the answer. By 20 October the new demands were off the table; on 27 October Hanoi dropped all outstanding demands, and Johnson announced a full bombing halt on 31 October. Beijing recalled its troops and reduced military aid, which Hanoi read as anger at its choice of Soviet over Chinese advice. Third order, the flexibility came too late. Thieu, told by his ambassador that he would get a better deal from the Republican candidate, undercut Humphrey's campaign, and Nixon passed messages telling him to stand firm. Nguyen notes that Nixon and Kissinger then ran their war much as Le Duan ran his: decisions centralized, kept secret, and aimed at the Sino-Soviet split. That chain pre-empts the claim that talks began when the war turned, because the talks turned only when Hanoi's offensives ran out. Harriman got the history lecture. The Politburo got the reality briefing.

A question for the room: if Hanoi's first serious plan came two days before its forces were spent, how should anyone read an enemy's peace feelers while it still has an offensive in reserve? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 5

The Cambodian Incursion Missed COSVN and Hit Congress

Score an operation on the periphery by the options it leaves the enemy afterward, because the ground it takes is the smaller half of the ledger. Nguyen argues that by 1970 Hanoi, drained by its 1968 and 1969 offensives, adopted an "economy of forces," shelved the urban offensive, raised diplomacy to equal rank with fighting, and stepped up support for Cambodia and Laos. As the war shrank inside South Vietnam, it spread next door.

The sideshows were not well hidden. In February 1970 Kissinger told Le Duc Tho that most of the Pathet Lao troops were speaking Vietnamese, and 40,000 North Vietnamese troops had been in Laos in 1969. After the Cambodian National Assembly voted Sihanouk out on 18 March, the exiled prince formally approved communist use of Cambodian territory. On 30 April Nixon sent American and South Vietnamese troops after COSVN, the communist headquarters for the South. After two days of heavy combat the communists withdrew deeper into Cambodia, to a headquarters they had already moved. Nguyen says the military balance on the ground barely changed.

The weak seminar comment is "The Cambodian incursion failed because it didn't find COSVN." It is weak because it scores only the first-order result and misses where the operation's real effects landed, which was in Washington and Paris. The strong version is "The incursion barely moved the balance in Cambodia and moved it a great deal at home, and Hanoi read the result and hardened its position." Opinion: an operation that misses the enemy's headquarters and hits your own Senate has found something, just not what it was looking for.

First order, the incursion missed the headquarters and left the balance on the ground about where it was. Second order, it revived the antiwar movement. Four students were killed at Kent State. Two of Kissinger's staff, Anthony Lake and Roger Morris, resigned. Senators Cooper and Church proposed cutting off money for operations in Cambodia, McGovern and Hatfield proposed ending funds for Indochina altogether, and the Senate repealed the Tonkin Gulf Resolution. None of the amendments passed both houses, but Nguyen says time was running out at home for Nixon's war. Third order, on 7 September Kissinger offered a twelve-month withdrawal schedule with no matching demand for North Vietnamese withdrawal. Hanoi read it as weakness forced by the antiwar movement, ordered its negotiators to take a more rigid position and exploit the peace movement, and had the PRG offer a softer-looking plan on 17 September that for the first time linked withdrawal to the release of American prisoners. Kissinger, Nguyen says, took the bait. Meanwhile communist forces had taken five northeastern provinces of Cambodia by June, and the bombing of Cambodia shifted power toward the Khmer Rouge, which suited neither Washington nor Hanoi. That chain pre-empts the defense that the incursion bought time for Vietnamization, since the time it bought was spent in Paris on Hanoi's terms. When the enemy's headquarters leaves before you arrive, check whether your own capital is still there.

A question for the room: if Hanoi read an unmatched withdrawal timetable as weakness, what should Kissinger have asked for in return on 7 September? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 6

Hanoi Wrote Its Public Peace Plans for Congress

Ask whom a peace offer was written for. Nguyen's sixth chapter shows Hanoi running two negotiations in 1971: a secret one with Henry Kissinger and a public one aimed at the American home front. By the end of the year, victory in Laos and Nixon's courtship of Beijing and Moscow had tipped the Politburo toward the fighting half of "talking while fighting." Peace, she concludes, never had a chance that year.

The cleanest evidence is five days in Paris. On 26 June Le Duc Tho and Xuan Thuy handed Kissinger a secret nine-point plan, having spread a green tablecloth over the usually drab table to look flexible (diplomacy by upholstery). On 1 July, at the 119th public session, Madame Binh unveiled seven points that pressed harder on Thieu. Tho then told the New York Times that Hanoi would trade troop withdrawals for prisoners, a deal he always tied to other terms in private. Nixon and Kissinger called it a "duplicitous stunt." Le Duan's own telegram to his negotiators named the two targets: stir up the antiwar movement in Congress and on the streets, and topple Saigon.

The weak seminar comment is "Hanoi was intransigent in Paris." It is weak because it describes the private talks and misses the design; Hanoi was flexible in public and rigid in private on purpose. The strong version is "The public offer was a weapon aimed at American voters, and Le Duan said so in writing in July 1971." Opinion: Hanoi learned the trick from Nixon. In January Thuy cabled home that Nixon's five-point proposal was hollow and built for world opinion, and that it worked anyway, because fewer Americans were dying and Nixon was using the prisoner issue. When Kissinger told Hanoi on 12 July to choose between propaganda and negotiation, he was grading a copy of his own homework.

First order, the public plan made Hanoi look reasonable while its private terms stayed hard. Second order, it landed on a home front that was already restless. Nguyen lists the Vietnam Veterans Against the War, with John Kerry testifying in the Senate and 700 veterans laying their medals on the Capitol steps, the May Day protests, and the Pentagon Papers on 13 June, and she says Nixon feared the veterans more than the radicals. Third order, Hanoi misread its own good news. The South Vietnamese defeat in Laos, where 17,000 troops met 22,000 and later 60,000 North Vietnamese, gave the Politburo a morale boost that Nguyen says may have been a pyrrhic victory, because it led Hanoi to think it had dealt Vietnamization a decisive blow. On 29 November 1971 Le Duan ordered the South to prepare for a large offensive in 1972. That chain pre-empts the reply that propaganda is only noise, because the plan's audience was the one body that could cut off the war's money, and the confidence it bred sent Hanoi into the 1972 offensive. Both capitals stalled in Paris that year, each convinced it had found the other's breaking point. They were both wrong, which is a kind of agreement.

A question for the room: if the peace plan was aimed at Congress, should the Americans have answered it in Paris or on Capitol Hill? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 7

Brezhnev Shouted for the Record and Toasted for Real

Judge an ally by what it ships and what it refuses to cancel, because speeches are free. Nguyen argues that Le Duan and Le Duc Tho launched the 1972 offensive partly to get ahead of their own patrons, since a smashing victory would protect Hanoi against Chinese and Soviet pressure to settle. The Party had already decided that under renewed American bombing it could not both build socialism in the North and fight in the South, so it chose the fight. Beijing and Moscow protested Nixon's escalation for the record and then leaned on Hanoi to settle more or less on Washington's terms.

Hanoi timed the attack between Nixon's Beijing summit in February and his Moscow summit in May. Tanks crossed the demilitarized zone on 30 March. Nixon answered on 8 May with Linebacker and the mining of the ports. Moscow protested mildly and said nothing about the summit, and Hanoi heard the silence. At his dacha on 24 May, Brezhnev told Nixon he did not want to hurl more epithets at him, hurled a few more, and then toasted him. Beijing condemned the mining and sent mine-clearing teams, yet kept Soviet ships out of Chinese harbors until Le Duc Tho pleaded on 18 June. When Soviet cargo arrived under torn blankets instead of tarpaulins, Beijing decided Moscow wanted American satellites to see it. Kissinger landed in China the day after Tho left.

The weak seminar comment is "China and the Soviet Union stood by Hanoi." It is weak because it takes public statements as evidence and ignores the author's counterevidence about what the allies actually did. The strong version is "They sent defensive arms against Linebacker, no offensive weapons for the war in the South, and both summits went ahead." Opinion: Hanoi read its allies better than its enemy. Le Duan told Zhou Enlai that Beijing had rescued a "drowning" Nixon, which was right, while Tho had bet that Nixon, busy with summits, could not hit back, which was not.

First order, the summits went on and the allies sent only defensive help. Nguyen's verdict is that Nixon's gamble paid off, because neither Beijing nor Moscow would risk its relations with Washington for Hanoi. Second order, on 1 June, after Nixon's Moscow visit, the Politburo shifted the nation's resources from the offensive in the South to the defense of the North. By mid-June American bombing and South Vietnamese counteroffensives had rolled back the early gains, and Hanoi's leaders privately admitted that the big-unit offensive had failed. Third order, Le Duan gave up on the uprising. He blamed American bombs and Saigon's success at drafting more men, and for the rest of the war, Nguyen says, the leaders confined their struggle to diplomacy. That chain pre-empts the airman's claim that Linebacker alone turned Hanoi, since the bombing worked inside a diplomatic squeeze that left Hanoi without a patron willing to escalate. Moscow drank to détente while Hanoi's leaders sat in bomb shelters.

A question for the room: if Linebacker worked because Moscow and Beijing would not escalate, how much of the credit belongs to the bombers and how much to the summits? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, chapter 8

The Christmas Bombing Hit Hanoi's Calendar Harder Than Its Will

Before crediting bombs with breaking a leadership, find out what that leadership already wanted, then ask whether the bombs changed its goal or only its price. By summer 1972 Le Duan had conceded defeat on the battlefield. Linebacker had pushed the Politburo from attacking the South to defending the North, Saigon retook Quang Tri, and Beijing and Moscow cut aid while urging Hanoi to settle. He shelved the aim of toppling Saigon and went after American withdrawal at the table, hoping to avoid more damage and save his forces for later.

The Party did worry about morale. During the offensive the security minister, Tran Quoc Hoan, joined the Politburo to clamp down on unrest, and the Party press ran seventeen articles on "counterrevolution" in May alone. American psyops planners even proposed replacing Radio Hanoi's evening news with a fake broadcast from an aircraft; Laird vetoed it. Kissinger thought the campaign was striking a raw nerve. Nguyen judges a counterrevolution unlikely given the Party's control. With talks deadlocked on 13 December, Nixon ordered Linebacker II for 18 December, the day Tho was due home. In twelve days, 3,420 sorties hit the Hanoi and Haiphong area, killing more than 2,000 civilians and costing 27 American aircraft, 15 of them B-52s. On 26 December, the heaviest day, Hanoi offered to resume talks.

The weak seminar comment is "Linebacker II bombed Hanoi back to the table." It is weak because it takes a sequence as the cause and ignores the author's counterevidence: the Politburo had already told its negotiators that signing now or later no longer mattered, and the final round was booked before the bombs fell. The strong version is "Linebacker II raised the price of delay for a Politburo that already wanted out. Nguyen scores it as the failure of Hanoi's hope to settle without more damage." Opinion: she gives two verdicts on one page, that Nixon's bombs "had done their damage" and that they mobilized world outrage instead of breaking Hanoi's will. Pick one per argument, and say which.

First order, the bombing did real damage, and on its ninth day Hanoi offered to talk. Second order, the January agreement looked a lot like the fall draft. Some officials and scholars say it was essentially done by Thanksgiving, though others point to changes on the demilitarized zone and on civilian detainees. In early January both houses of Congress passed legislation to cut off funds for Indochina once the prisoners came home, and Thieu, facing an aid cutoff, finally relented. Third order, the outcry over the bombing meant that Nixon could no longer use American air power in Vietnam. Thieu's stalling bought him about three more years, until communist forces recovered. That chain pre-empts both the airman's claim and the critic's, because the bombing worked in the short run and used up its own license. Hanoi had planned for its future, too. Early in 1973 the Ministry of Public Security got a decree creating a Counterreactionary Department, and its war at home could go on indefinitely. The final round of talks opened on 8 January, a date booked before the bombing began. That is an expensive way to keep an appointment.

A question for the room: if the Politburo already wanted to sign, what did twelve days of bombing buy that the calendar would not have? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Nguyen, epilogue and conclusion

Fifteen Years Waiting for an Uprising, and Then Tanks

In a war fought through allies, the small powers often set the pace, so study their capitals as hard as your own. Nguyen's conclusion says that leaders in Hanoi and Saigon dictated the nature and pace of American intervention, and that Vietnamese elites created the context Washington worked in. Le Duan monopolized decisions, sidelined Ho and Giap at nearly every key point, and bet three times on an uprising that never came. What carried Hanoi, she argues, was its world relations campaign, which rallied antiwar movements abroad and blunted Washington's superpower diplomacy. Thieu, the supposed puppet, dragged out the American exit and exposed the limits of the Nixon Doctrine.

On 30 April 1975 troops under General Van Tien Dung rode Soviet tanks into Saigon. The Party never needed the masses to rise; South Vietnam's leaders had more or less fled a week earlier. Le Duan died in 1986, and on 15 July, 100,000 people lined the six miles from Ba Dinh Square to Mai Dich Cemetery. China was conspicuously absent, and Ronald Reagan did not register the death. Giap, stripped of his defense post in 1980 and his Politburo seat in 1982, outlived both Le Duan and Le Duc Tho and now ranks abroad among history's great generals. Le Duan won the war and lost the obituary.

The weak seminar comment is "We lost Vietnam because of bad decisions in Washington." It is weak because it keeps Washington at the center, the very habit the book was written against. The strong version is "Washington made plenty of bad decisions, but Hanoi and Saigon set the terms. Le Duan pushed escalation over Ho's objections, Hanoi's antiwar diplomacy blunted Nixon's triangle, and Thieu slowed the withdrawal." Opinion: quote her caveats too. She admits nothing is definitive until the Party, military, and Foreign Ministry archives open, she warns that crediting small powers too much lets the superpowers off the hook, and she concedes that Nixon's superpower diplomacy "succeeded in squaring" the triangle. A student who quotes her thesis without her warnings is grading her on a curve she refused.

First order, Hanoi's diplomacy and Saigon's obstruction shaped what Washington could do; both used the tools of junior allies, obstruction, blackmail, and threats, on their patrons. Second order, the Paris agreement of January 1973 ended the American phase of the war and nothing else, and a "war of the flags" followed as both Vietnams grabbed ground while the great powers watched. Zhou Enlai had told Le Duc Tho the important thing was to let the Americans leave, because the situation would change in six months or a year. Third order, the war ended by force in 1975, and Nguyen's last line says leaders ensured that "peace could only be won through the barrel of a gun." That chain pre-empts the reply that the diplomats won the war, since the diplomacy only removed the Americans and the army did the rest. The diplomats won the argument, and the tanks delivered the verdict.

A question for the room: if Hanoi's best weapon was world opinion, why did the war still end with a tank column, and what does that say about how far diplomacy can carry a war aim? (S)

Six Old Friends Who Met Le Duan Without Knowing His Name

  4. Schlight, the war in the South. The Air Force did close air support well but never had its own war objective or reliable data on what it achieved.   5. Clodfelter, the limits of air power. Bombing's value depends on the goal and the kind of war: it failed Johnson and worked for Nixon's limited aims in 1972.   6. Randolph, the Easter Offensive. Nixon's air and naval response blunted the offensive and got the United States out, but American power could not compel a stable settlement. The link: Randolph expected Hanoi to stay below the threshold that would bring the Americans back, and Nguyen's Zhou Enlai told Le Duc Tho to let the Americans leave and wait six months or a year.
