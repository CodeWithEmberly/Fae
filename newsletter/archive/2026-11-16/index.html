Seminar 13 · Lambeth, The Transformation of American Air Power · "Airpower Vision for American Dominance"

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered

  1. How had technology changed airmen's approach to their craft? It changed what airmen count. Lambeth says they stopped talking about sorties per target killed and started talking about kills per sortie (chapter 1). His Desert Storm arithmetic makes the point: a nonstealthy package needed 38 aircraft so that 8 could bomb three aim points, while 20 F-117s attacked 37 (chapter 5). He credits training as much as hardware: Red Flag gave pilots the equivalent of their first eight or ten combat sorties in peacetime, and fighter pilots' yearly flying time rose from about 150 hours to more than 230 (chapter 3).
  2. What underlying assumptions are implicit within the text? The biggest one is stated outright: the study covers major theater wars and sets aside smaller operations and irregular conflicts such as urban combat (chapter 1). He also assumes good intelligence, while conceding that air power can kill only what it can see, identify, and engage, and that the 1991 campaign failed to eliminate Iraq's nuclear program (chapter 1). He assumes an army that can be attacked from the air before the ground war, plus nearby bases. His own conclusion admits that Korea, with more than 500,000 troops along the demilitarized zone, would mean close ground combat from the start, and that regional access has to be secured in advance (chapter 9).
  3. Is Lambeth's argument still persuasive? The narrow version is, and the broad one isn't. Against an armored invasion, he argues, air power can now produce strategic effects from the opening of a joint campaign, so that land power increasingly secures a win rather than achieves it (chapter 9); the Gulf numbers support that. His claim that air power's effects are now a matter of when rather than whether leans on Kosovo, a campaign he himself calls agonizingly slow, and his own list of reasons Milosevic quit includes Russia's shift, the war crimes indictment, and preparations for a ground invasion (chapters 6 and 9).
  4. What are the new limitations placed on airpower? Are they new, or just altered? Altered, with one new twist. Vietnam's limits came from fear of escalation: MiG bases inside the Hanoi circle were off limits, and SAM sites could be hit only while firing (chapter 2). Kosovo's came from collateral damage and coalition politics: aircrews stayed at medium altitude, ground forces were ruled out before the first bomb, and Lambeth says Congress and public opinion would not have permitted a tougher strategy (chapter 6). The new twist is that precision created its own limit. Once zero noncombatant casualties became the expectation, he writes, air power became a victim of its own success.
  5. How can this text inform today's strategy in terms of force modernization? Lambeth frames it as a fork. The Air Force could keep stressing fighters, short-range attack, and expeditionary operations, or it could emphasize improved ISR, longer-range attack, and unmanned platforms for the riskiest missions (chapter 9). He warns that runaway cost growth could price the department out of the defense business, and that a technology edge pushes adversaries toward cheap countermeasures against JSTARS, AWACS, and tankers. His sales advice is to let the theater commanders make the case, since they are the most credible witnesses before the Joint Requirements Oversight Council.

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. (S)

Today's Reading · Lambeth, The Transformation of American Air Power

Lambeth Buries Douhet and Keeps the Shovel

Benjamin Lambeth rejects air power's founding prophecy and keeps most of its optimism. He tells airmen to repudiate Douhet's claim that command of the air is victory, which he calls false in 1921 and false now, and he says three separate times that air power cannot win wars single-handedly. Then he argues that it can now produce "the effects of massing without having to mass," which is about as close to Douhet as a RAND study can get with a straight face. The book's real move is a definition: "strategic" names the effect sought, whatever the target. By that test, he concedes, the Baghdad strikes of 1991 did little, and the attacks on Iraq's army did the strategic work.

That concession lines him up with Olsen, whose Warden spent 2 percent of the strikes on the inner ring. It also puts him across the table from Fravel. Both men studied Kosovo at the same moment; the PLA called air strikes the most likely way China would be attacked, and Lambeth called the campaign a step backward in efficiency. (The target audience was more impressed than the shooters.) Opinion: Lambeth wrote the best case airmen had in 2000. Whether it holds depends on the assumption he states up front and then rarely revisits, that the next war looks like a major theater war. (S)

Counterpoint · Haun against Lambeth

Counterpoint: Haun Checks Lambeth's Math on Iraq's Army

Lambeth's central claim is that by 1991 American air power could break an enemy army from the opening of a joint campaign, so that ground forces now secure a win rather than achieve it. His Desert Storm evidence is effort and outcome: 67 percent of weapon releases against discrete aim points went at Iraq's fielded forces, and the ground war took four days.

Phil Haun, whom we read on November 9, makes the strongest case against him, using the same war and largely the same survey. His appendix on Desert Storm finds that Instant Thunder and the air interdiction campaign were the only modern air operations where most factors favored air power, "yet both failed." Interdiction cut Iraqi transport capacity from 200,000 tons a day to 20,000, but an army sitting in static defenses needed only 10,000 to 15,000. By the start of the ground war, 25 to 30 percent of the Iraqi army had been attrited, and airstrikes destroyed only about 10 percent of Iraqi armor and artillery; desertions did the rest. The Republican Guard, better supplied and less bombed, could still move: on the night of February 24, three brigades of the Tawakalna division shifted more than fifty kilometers overnight to face VII Corps at 73 Easting. During the 100-hour ground battle, Haun says, air power played only a minor role. Earlier, Horner's ban on low-altitude flying had cut losses to antiaircraft guns and also cut the effectiveness of tactical aircraft against Iraq's fielded forces. (Even the goalposts moved. Lambeth puts Schwarzkopf's precondition at 30 percent attrition of Iraqi forces; Haun puts it at 50 percent of Iraqi armor. Neither says the air campaign reached its number.)

Lambeth's evidence can show where the bombs went, and it can't show what they did. Sixty-seven percent is a measure of effort. His rebuttal of the Army claim that tanks and helicopters killed most Iraqi armor rests on the Air Force's own survey plus CIA imagery analysts, and when critics note that more Iraqi equipment was destroyed or captured in four days of ground war than in six weeks of air strikes, he asks whether it had been abandoned. That's a fair question, though a question is not a count. Haun's evidence has its own limit: he grants that direct attack on Iraq's fielded forces, "while not independently decisive," played a primary role in shaping the battlefield. Once you read both closely, they agree on what happened and disagree on what to call it.

Opinion: Haun wins the scorecard, and Lambeth wins the definition, and the definition matters less than what came after. First order, direct attack made the Iraqi army disperse and dig in, Haun says, so coalition ground forces met little resistance. Second order, the war cost fewer than 200 American combat deaths, and Lambeth says that near-bloodless win became the standard Americans use to judge every commitment. Third order, Kosovo was judged against it: zero noncombatant casualties became the expectation, and Lambeth's own word for the result is that air power became a victim of its own success. The seminar comment: "Haun and Lambeth agree on what air did to Iraq's army; the bigger effect of that cheap victory was the standard it set, and Kosovo was graded against it." The Gulf War's most durable target turned out to be the American public. (S)

Questions to Ask in Seminar · Lambeth

Four Questions for a Booster's Seminar

  1. Is Lambeth's transformation really a transformation in intelligence? He writes that air power "can kill only what it can see, identify, and engage" (Lambeth, chapter 1, page 9). One answer: yes. He calls air power and intelligence opposite sides of the same coin, and his own example of failure, the 1991 campaign's inability to eliminate Iraq's nuclear program, is an intelligence failure the airplanes could not fix (page 10). The other answer: no. The stealth arithmetic is a weapons gain, 20 F-117s attacking 37 aim points while a nonstealthy package needed 38 aircraft for three (page 156), and the targets were known in both cases. The first answer is stronger for the next war, because stealth only matters after someone finds the target.
  2. Was Kosovo a win for air power or for the strategy around it? Lambeth says air power "can never be more effective than the strategy it is intended to support" (Lambeth, chapter 6, page 232). One answer: air power. Milosevic gave up after 78 days without a NATO ground offensive, and Lambeth counts it as the third regional win of the decade for air power (page 181). The other answer: the strategy, and the parts of it that weren't air. Russia backed the Western position on June 3 (page 191), Milosevic was indicted (page 192), and engineers were strengthening the road from Durres to Kukes for Abrams tanks three days before he quit (page 193). The second answer is stronger, because Lambeth himself says there is good reason to be wary of the air-alone claim (page 191).
  3. Does land power now just "secure a win rather than achieve it"? That is Lambeth's phrase for the role of land forces against large-scale aggression (Lambeth, chapter 9, pages 320 to 321). One answer: yes. The coalition lost fewer lives to hostile fire in five weeks of fighting than to accidents in five months of workups (page 306), and two of every three weapon releases went at Iraq's fielded forces (page 137). The other answer: only against an enemy who sits still. In Korea, Lambeth concedes, more than 500,000 troops along the demilitarized zone would mean close ground combat from the start (page 300), and Haun finds air destroyed only about 10 percent of Iraqi armor and artillery before the ground war (Haun, appendix B, page 272). The second answer is stronger as a general rule, since Lambeth's own best counterexample is the most likely war on his list.
  4. Is precision its own worst limitation? Lambeth warns about what happens "once zero collateral damage becomes accepted as a goal of strategy" (Lambeth, chapter 6, page 205). One answer: yes. After the Gulf War's bunker videos, political leaders and the public expected perfection, so each error over Serbia, such as the Djakovica convoy strike that killed as many as 64 refugees, became front-page news and a new constraint (page 205). The other answer: no, the limit is political, as it was in Vietnam. Lambeth says Congress and public opinion would not have permitted a tougher Kosovo strategy (page 226), and in Vietnam John McNaughton advised that leaving the SAM sites alone would signal Hanoi not to use them (page 19). The first answer is stronger, because it explains why the political limit got tighter after the most successful air war in American history. (S)
The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Lambeth · extrapolation

The Strait Needs Eyes, and the Eyes Need Somewhere to Land

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is keeping the Strait of Hormuz open after the United States withdraws from the war with Iran, and his working answer is sustainable ISR, sensors, drones, and robotics rather than strategic bombing. What follows is our extrapolation from Lambeth, and not Shaun's answer.

Lambeth supports the answer in three places. Air power can kill only what it can see, identify, and engage, he writes, so intelligence is an indispensable precondition for results. In his conclusion he describes the Air Force's choice as business as usual with fighters and short-range attack, or a shift to improved ISR, longer-range attack, and unmanned platforms for the higher-risk missions of the new century. He adds that public sensitivity to casualties drives a demand for technology to substitute for human life wherever possible. A drone orbit over the strait checks all three boxes, and no aircrew is in it.

He cuts against it in two places. First, he predicts that a technology edge spurs have-nots to build cheap countermeasures, and his examples are attacks on high-value soft targets, JSTARS, AWACS, and tankers. A sustained sensor presence is exactly that kind of target, parked in the same place every day. Second, he warns that neither bombers nor carriers have the numbers and persistence to sustain a campaign-scale air operation, that only land-based aircraft can, and that regional access depends on partnerships built in advance. A withdrawal from a war with Iran is not the obvious moment to ask the neighbors for more ramp space. His study also covers major theater wars, so a years-long patrol falls outside it.

Opinion, extrapolated: Lambeth would endorse the sensors and ask two questions about them. Who defends the soft high-value assets when Iran starts hunting them, and whose runway do they fly from? A persistent presence is only as persistent as its basing agreement. (S)

The Readings So Far · Lambeth, Kitfield, Fravel, Olsen, and Haun

Everyone Grades Desert Storm on a Different Curve

Four books on the shelf grade the same six weeks of 1991, and each uses a different answer key. Olsen grades Warden's plan against its unstated aim and finds 2 percent of the strikes on the inner ring. Haun grades by mission type and finds the independent missions failed. Fravel grades by what the audience learned, and the PLA concluded it had better survive air strikes. Lambeth grades by effect on Iraq's army and calls it strategic. (Same war, four rubrics, and no two agree on the grade.)

Kitfield and Lambeth tell one story from opposite sides of a boundary. Kitfield's Army reformers, Starry and DePuy, came home from studying the 1973 war resolved to win the first battle of the next war. Lambeth's Air Force rebuilt through Red Flag and more flying hours. In 1991 the two reformed services met at the fire support coordination line, and Lambeth's chapter 4 carries McPeak's account of a corps commander keeping the air commander off the main resupply route for 17 hours. Both services reformed after Vietnam, and in 1991 they met at a border neither had drawn to the other's satisfaction.

Last week's news keeps pinning itself here. Friday's Starshield item was about a link that a supplier could restrict mid-war; Lambeth warned in 2000 that GPS signals are weak and that JDAM would depend on them entirely. Friday afternoon's funding fight met Lambeth's finding that public support depends on judged stakes and judged competence. Opinion: Lambeth's book reads best as a list of things that worked in 1991, each with a sticky note saying "until someone adapts." (S)

Technical Writing · Week 4, Day 1 · active voice and agency

Who's Kicking Whom at the Fire Support Coordination Line

Every sentence should tell the reader who did what. Helen Sword, in Stylish Academic Writing, borrows Richard Lanham's test question, "Who's kicking whom?", and says stylish writers keep nouns and verbs close together so readers can answer it. Her rule on the passive is practical: a be verb plus a past participle signals a passive construction, a few of them add variety, and too many in one paragraph add up to lifeless, agentless prose. She also asks for at least one sentence per paragraph with a person as the subject and an active verb right behind it.

Lambeth's chapter 4 shows why agency matters in military history. Before: "Of the 3,067 targets nominated by the Army, little more than a third were attacked." The sentence hides the decision, and the Army spent years blaming the wrong man for it. After: "The Army nominated 3,067 targets, and Schwarzkopf, who set the apportionment and approved the air commander's targets, sent aircraft against little more than a third of them." Now the reader knows whom to ask. The Gulf War Air Power Survey found exactly that confusion in the field: some ground commanders blamed Horner or Glosson without realizing the apportionment was Schwarzkopf's.

Opinion: a passive sentence in a strategy paper is often a hiding place for the most important actor. "Restrictions were imposed" is a different claim from "the president imposed restrictions," and only the second can be argued with.

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: run the "by an idiot" test. If you can add "by an idiot" after the verb ("targets were attacked by an idiot") and the sentence still parses, it's passive, and you should decide whether the idiot deserves a name. (S)

Augustine's Law Gets a Lame-Duck Session

The administration's $1.45 trillion defense request for fiscal 2027 is dead, Matt Vallone argues in War on the Rocks this morning, and the midterms killed it. The request paired a $1.1 trillion base budget, which he thinks can be salvaged, with $350 billion through reconciliation, which he thinks can't. Together they would have grown defense spending almost 44 percent over fiscal 2026. Congress has about five legislative work weeks left, the continuing resolution expires December 11, and the House margin is two seats. His most likely outcome is another continuing resolution at fiscal 2026 levels, $857.6 billion, plus the $151.5 billion from the 2025 reconciliation bill still being spent. The Pentagon's $87.6 billion Iran supplemental has seen no action in either chamber, and he rates its prospects as bleak.

The casualties are listed by name. Reconciliation carried most of the money for the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group ($53.6 billion), industrial base programs ($40.6 billion), a sovereign AI arsenal ($34 billion), Defense Production Act purchases ($30 billion), and much of Golden Dome. Moving money into regular appropriations means less flexibility and closer oversight, and Vallone expects big prime contractors to fare better than the new entrants that live on private investment. (The startups who pitched speed are about to meet the calendar.)

Lambeth saw this problem coming in 2000. In his conclusion he says the precondition for any air and space revolution lies on the affordability front, and he cites Norman Augustine's prediction that by 2054 the entire defense budget would buy a single aircraft, a trend Augustine said was still on track in 2000. He also says the Air Force had yet to make the really hard choices about which long-familiar investments to give up, and he names the alternative: ISR, longer-range attack, and unmanned platforms. Twenty-six years later, the unmanned money sat in the most fragile account on the ledger.

Opinion: a continuing resolution is the purest form of business as usual, since it funds last year's choices at last year's rates. Lambeth's advice was to let theater commanders argue for new capability before the Joint Requirements Oversight Council, because a service chief gets heard as a hardware accumulator. A lame-duck Congress with five weeks may not be listening to anyone. Augustine's law, meanwhile, needs no appropriation. (S)

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of 1966 strike sorties hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how did January 1973's agreement differ from October's?

    "Largely cosmetic" differences (ch. VI, e217).

  • Randolph: how does he score the Easter Offensive for Hanoi?

    "A strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome" (339).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

  • Haun: how much Iraqi armor and artillery did airstrikes destroy before the ground war?

    About 10 percent; desertions accounted for most of the 25 to 30 percent attrition (272).

  • Kitfield: what was the U.S. Army's 1976 doctrine called, and what did it warn?

    "Active defense," in FM 100-5; it warned the Army to "fight outnumbered and win" (192).

  • Olsen: what share of the air effort went against Warden's inner ring?

    2 percent; 740 of 43,123 strikes hit the "central nervous system" (167, 295).

  • Fravel: who started eight of China's nine strategies since 1949?

    Senior military officers, subject only to the chairman's approval (ebook 429).

  • Lambeth: what role does he forecast for land power against large-scale aggression?

    "To secure a win rather than to achieve it" (320–321).

Writing drills

  • Name the actor: "Mistakes were made in apportioning sorties during Desert Storm."

    "Schwarzkopf set the apportionment, and some ground commanders blamed Horner or Glosson for it" (Lambeth, 134). The passive version hides the one person who decided (Sword, 49).

  • Replace the research-as-agent subject: "The data shows air power became strategic in 1991."

    "Lambeth argues that air power achieved strategic effects against Iraq's army in 1991" (Lambeth, 268). A person as subject, an active verb right behind (Sword, 61).

  • Review (Friday, citations): fix the too-close paraphrase "Air power can only kill what it is able to see, identify, and engage (Lambeth)."

    Quote it, since the phrasing is his ("can kill only what it can see, identify, and engage"), or rethink it: "Lambeth makes intelligence a precondition for air power, not an accessory" (Lambeth, 9). Add the page (Booth et al., 207–209).

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
