Tonight's Reading · for Friday's seminar

Wasser, The Air War Against the Islamic State: The Partners Held the Stopwatch

Airpower was indispensable against the Islamic State, and it still did not set the war's pace. That is the RAND team's argument, and we think it is right. Washington chose a partner-led, limited-risk strategy: Iraqi, Syrian, and Kurdish forces did the fighting, and coalition aircraft supplied the intelligence and the firepower. Territory was the measure of success, so the close fight came first and the deep strikes on oil and cash were useful but peripheral. Wasser's bottom line on the great debate is that a more aggressive air war from day one might have sped ISIS's defeat, but not dramatically.

The report argues against two camps. The first is the airpower camp, which says an airman in command, looser rules, and strategic attack would have finished ISIS sooner; the authors call the evidence for those claims mixed. The second is everyone who files the war under counterinsurgency. The coalition released an average of 26,645 weapons a year, while fewer than 2,000 fell on Iraq even in 2007, the peak of that counterinsurgency. (Nobody calls 117,000 weapons a police action.)

For this course, the syllabus asks whether Slessor or Douhet explains the campaign. Slessor does, and it is not close: airpower won by working with an army on the ground, and here the army belonged to someone else. The limits came from partners, lawyers, and a Russian air force that showed up over Syria on September 30, 2015, and not from a nuclear shadow. Read it asking one question: who held the stopwatch? (S)

The World Around the Book

The Air Force Paid for the Study. The Study Declined to Agree With the Air Force.

The author matters less than the customer. Becca Wasser led a team of nine at RAND Project AIR FORCE, the Department of the Air Force's own federally funded research center, and the Assistant Vice Chief of Staff of the Air Force sponsored the work. The team built its case from more than fifty interviews with participants and two strike data sets, and the draft went to the Air Force on May 27, 2020, after formal peer review. Wasser was a senior policy analyst who designed wargames and led research for the Pentagon, the Army, the Air Force, and the State Department, and she left for the Center for a New American Security that June. She was a wargamer and a policy analyst, and it shows: the report's best chapters are about how decisions moved through headquarters.

Our take on the axe: there is no documented personal grudge, so look at the question instead. The Air Force paid RAND to test the airmen's favorite complaint about this war, and RAND came back and called the evidence for it mixed. A study funded by the service and published under its program is the last place you would expect that verdict, which makes the verdict more credible. Read the report as a referee's ruling, and notice when it gives the airmen a point, such as centralized strike approval clearly hurting in year one.

The moment shapes the argument. The caliphate fell at Baghuz in March 2019, and the team wrote while the arguments about the war were still loud. The authors say much ink had been spilled criticizing the slow, limited start, and they wrote against that criticism. They also wrote facing forward: the preface says the lessons are meant for future wars against nonstate and near-peer adversaries. Ben Lambeth, Monday's author, wrote a rival account, Airpower in the War Against ISIS, arguing that coalition airpower was underused for more than two years. When you read chapter 7, remember that it is half history and half a reply to Lambeth's camp.

The reception is thin, and the scholarly part is undocumented. We found no review in a scholarly or military journal. The press read it as a story about priorities: Oriana Pawlyk of Military.com, on February 9, 2021, led with the campaign favoring close air support over deep strike. The sharpest critic was Robbin Laird at Defense.info, in July 2021. He said the report mostly repeats the official account, takes its lessons from a slow Army counterinsurgency mindset, and is a poor guide for China or Russia. He held up Lambeth's book as the better guide.

Our verdict: Laird is wrong about the history and partly right about the future. RAND's own data answers his main charge, because the coalition began with few, if any, deliberate target packages and with partners who could not yet attack. No bolder air commander fixes either problem in a month. His point about China lands, though, and the authors concede it themselves: the coalition had time to fix its targeting process, and a future enemy may not give it that time. (Patience was the war's most important capability, and it does not come in the next war's budget.) (S)

People and Terms

The Cast, With Call Signs

  1. CJTF-OIR. The coalition task force that ran the war; its commander controlled all the airspace over Iraq and Syria (page 70).
  2. TEA. Target engagement authority, the power to approve a strike. It never sat below a one-star general in Phase I (pages 55, 84).
  3. Tactical Directive 1. The change that let advisory commanders able to control strikes approve ones that once needed a one-star or three-star general (page 133).
  4. Strike cells. Operations centers that tied airpower to partner forces when no American controllers were at the front (page 299).
  5. Lt Gen Charles Q. Brown. Took over AFCENT in June 2015, found well over 90 percent of strikes were dynamic, and ordered target development fixed (pages 67 to 68).
  6. Tidal Wave II and Point Blank. The deep-strike operations against ISIS's oil and cash (pages 204, 226).
  7. MG Joseph Martin. The Mosul ground commander whose headquarters built a faster targeting process (pages 99 to 100).
  8. SDF. The Syrian Democratic Forces, the coalition's irregular light infantry partner in Syria (page 190).
Bridge From Today · Fuller to Wasser

Fuller's Drones Lost the Hunt. Wasser Gave Them a Day Job.

Fuller ended where Wasser begins. His last chapter says the Islamic State blunted the drone campaign: it had antiaircraft weapons, it lived in cities, and it governed. A slow drone hunting leaders is the wrong tool against an enemy that holds ground. Wasser shows the drone's new job. With no American controllers on the front lines, drones overhead let strike cells see for the partners, and six or seven of them could circle small Ramadi for days. (Fuller's Predator was an assassin. Wasser's Reaper is a spotter with a very long shift.) The other change is ownership. Fuller's war belonged to the CIA, and Wasser's belonged to uniformed task forces with lawyers at every step.

Keep one question in mind while reading. Fuller measured success by leaders killed, and Wasser measures it by ground retaken. Which yardstick fits an enemy that is half army and half terrorist group, and what does each one miss? (S)

Political Strategy × Wasser · via Punchbowl News AM and electoral-vote.com, November 19

Congress Discovers the Word "Offensive"

Congress is trying to limit the Iran war by defining it, Punchbowl News reported this morning. Reps. Ro Khanna and Yassamin Ansari say they will oppose any government funding bill that does not explicitly bar funds for offensive operations in Iran. The deadline is December 11. Meanwhile, Senate Republicans have all but abandoned a $350 billion defense reconciliation package, and the annual defense bill, law for 65 straight years, faces its hardest path in memory, partly because of a push for $1.5 trillion in Pentagon funding and partly because of a war Punchbowl calls highly unpopular. On January 3, Hakeem Jeffries takes the gavel with a 260-seat majority. Electoral-vote.com adds the Senate math: 56 Democrats. It also notes the Iran war may hang over the presidential prospects of Marco Rubio and JD Vance. (The war now has a primary calendar.)

Wasser's report shows why "offensive" is a harder word than it looks. In Syria, U.S. pilots shot down three aircraft attacking partner forces, and the task force announced that it would defend its partners and did not seek a fight with the regime or Russia. Each shootdown was defensive by the coalition's account, and someone in uniform made that call in the cockpit or the operations center. Congress has also done this before. After Crimea, the FY2016 defense bill banned military cooperation with Russia, so CENTCOM deconflicted with Moscow's pilots but would not coordinate with them. A rider written in Washington set the rules for pilots over Syria.

Our take: a ban on offensive operations will be enforced by whoever drafts the rules of engagement. The vote only supplies the words. Obama's 2014 design kept Washington from "owning" the ISIS problem so it could leave if needed. Congress is now trying to buy the same exit after the fact, and that is harder. The cheapest wars to start are the hardest to define. (S)

Chapter by Chapter: Wasser et al., The Air War Against the Islamic State

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, summary and chapter 1

Under Ninety Minutes to Kill One Drone: Wasser on Why the Air War Ran Long

Before you blame a slow air war on its leash, ask what the airplanes would have hit with the leash off. Wasser's RAND team tests the airpower camp's complaint that politics stretched out the war against ISIS. The verdict is that more aggressive air operations might have slightly sped up ISIS's defeat but probably would not have changed the timeline much. Airpower was indispensable because it made partner ground forces work, and that strategy shaped everything the airplanes did.

The opening scene shows how crowded this war was. On June 8, 2017, two F-15E Strike Eagles near the al-Tanf garrison in eastern Syria found an Iranian-made Shahed 129 armed with two Hellfire-like missiles over Syrian partners and U.S. advisers. The jets left to refuel and came back to find one missile gone. (A missing missile clarifies intent wonderfully.) The pilot asked to shoot; the operations center tried jamming first. When the drone turned back, two Russian Su-27s within range made him wait so his shot would not look aimed at them. The guidance arrived: "if that thing [UAS] turns hot, smoke it." Within an hour and a half of the first sighting, an AIM-120C brought it down. This was a war under different authorities in Syria and Iraq, with hostile third parties sharing the sky.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 10, Wasser says airpower was indispensable to defeating ISIS. So an airman should have run an air campaign, and it would have ended sooner." It asserts more than the passage shows. The same page says airpower was indispensable for enabling partner ground operations, and on page 11 the authors say even a USAF commander would have struggled to sustain early strategic attacks, because intelligence problems blocked deliberate targets.

A stronger response: concede the airmen their best point. On page 11, Wasser agrees that centralized strike approval clearly limited airpower in the first year. Of the complaints about an airman in command and civilian-casualty limits, the same page says flatly, "The evidence for these two claims is mixed." The big oil and banking targets ran out, and territory supplied most of ISIS's wealth anyway. Earlier delegation might not have helped, since few Iraqi or Syrian partners were able and willing to attack. Opinion: the leash cost something, but the dog had few places to run, meaning looser rules mattered less than having targets and an army to support.

First order, Washington wanted a limited liability, limited risk approach and made Iraqi and Syrian ground forces the main effort. Second order, close air support outranked strategic attack, and drones overhead stood in for controllers kept off the front lines. Third order, deliberate targeting, unused in a real operation for years, had to be relearned, while the task force and air component commanders feuded over battlespace management, which shaped the deep fight. That chain pre-empts the claim that rules alone slowed the war, because the strategy decided what airpower was for before anyone argued about approvals.

A question for the room: Turn to page 8, where the authors warn that metrics mislead and that Vietnam's body counts contributed to failure. They judge airpower by correlating major campaign shifts with battlefield outcomes. Is that a better yardstick than counting bombs, or the same trap with a nicer map? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 2

The Bombers Came Home Full: Phase I Waited on the Ground War

In a by-with-through war, the partner on the ground sets the bombing tempo, so loosening the rules buys less than critics promise until somebody is attacking. Wasser's Phase I, from August 2014 to March 2016, started small and reactive and picked up once ground offensives began and a deep fight was built. By the end, Iraqi and Syrian partners had reclaimed 40 percent of the territory ISIS once held.

Kobani shows what a visible enemy does to the numbers. In August 2014, U.S. aircraft flew 949 air-to-ground sorties and released weapons on only 100. (Bosnia in 1995 ran over three times that sortie rate.) Over the last three months of 2014 and January 2015, the coalition launched 887 strikes at Kobani. ISIS massed in the open behind a clear front line, and Syria required no host-nation clearance. Lt Gen John Hesterman said approval was "measured in minutes, not hours, or even halves of hours." B-1s emptied their bomb bays on 31 missions, which is not how a bomber short of permission behaves. Then ISIS adapted. Strikes on fighters fell from 77 percent of targets in August 2014 to 33 percent in October, and over the first ten months 74 percent of sorties came home without dropping a bomb.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 55, Wasser describes a zero civilian casualty cutoff and approvals taking 30 minutes or hours, and critics called the result a drizzle. The rules cost us the first year." It reads one passage and ignores the counterevidence. On page 83, the authors say Kurdish and Iraqi forces could not or would not attack in late 2014 and early 2015, so ISIS stayed hidden in its cities.

A stronger response: concede the rules did damage. If we look at pages 83 and 84, the authors agree that centralized strike authority limited airpower and never dropped below a one-star in Phase I. Page 61 shows that when targets existed, approval took minutes. Pages 64 and 65 show deliberate targets took three to six weeks from an atrophied targeting enterprise, and no rule change conjures targets. On page 84, Gen Mark Welsh says it plainly: "the DoD approach is not to defeat ISIS from the air." Opinion: the rules slowed the bombing, and the empty battlefield slowed the war.

First order, precision strikes at Kobani shredded massed formations. Second order, ISIS shifted from lasting and expanding to simply lasting: it dispersed, blended into the population and dug layered defenses, so targets dried up. Third order, the campaign needed deep strikes it could not yet produce. Lt Gen Charles Q. Brown took over AFCENT in June 2015, found over 90 percent of strikes dynamic, and ordered the broken target-development process fixed. Ramadi still became a slog that December, partly because no deep fight had shaped it, and the task force resolved to soften Mosul and Raqqa from the air first. That chain pre-empts the counter that looser rules alone would have ended things sooner, because Kobani is what taught ISIS to hide.

A question for the room: Turn to page 85, where the authors report six or seven drones over small Ramadi for days while ground forces sat still, and admit they cannot judge it. Was that scarce ISR wasted, or the redundancy a partner-led war requires? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 3

ISIS Moved Every Two Weeks. The Paperwork Took Six.

When the enemy moves faster than your approval process runs, the approved target becomes a historical document, so build a short loop where the ground maneuver is owned and choose which safeguards ride along. Wasser's third chapter runs from April 2016 to the fall of Baghuz in March 2019. Its argument is that airpower did its heaviest work when partners advanced and forced ISIS into the open, and the authors admit they cannot separate what the deep strikes on ISIS's money did from what losing territory did.

In late 2016, ISIS launched ten to 15 car bombs a day at Mosul, and about 80 percent killed or damaged Iraqi units. The CAOC's deliberate-targeting process took four to six weeks, while ISIS moved its facilities every two weeks. The Iraqis drew up more than five plans and picked one at the last minute. The civilian-protection rule required hours of unbroken video over a target, and any interruption restarted the clock, a bar described as the "platinum standard." MG Joseph Martin bought time by cratering approach roads with 500-pound bombs fused to burst ten feet underground. (The Iraqis then bulldozed through the crater of their choice, a sentence no doctrine manual has ever had to contain.) Then his headquarters built its own nondoctrinal deliberate-dynamic process and turned out targets in 24 to 48 hours.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 100, Wasser shows Martin's process produced targets in 24 to 48 hours, so the CAOC's six-week process was just bureaucracy and should be scrapped." It asserts more than the passage shows. On page 112 the authors say the trade-offs of the faster process still need research, and page 99 says part of the slowness came from video rules meant to prevent civilian deaths.

A stronger response: concede the clock. A six-week process against a two-week target loses, and page 100 says the CJTF-run process never got fast enough to interrupt ISIS resupply. Page 99 shows the slow part was the protective part. Page 112 reports that Airwars counted more than 1,400 civilian deaths in Iraq in March 2017, the month one Mosul strike on rooftop snipers killed more than 100 people in a basement (the Pentagon blamed explosives ISIS staged there). On page 113, the authors list delegated authority as a possible cause, beside city fighting itself. Opinion: keep the speed, and argue openly about which safeguards travel with it.

First order, faster targeting and delegated authority gave Iraqi units responsive fires, and they cleared Mosul by July 2017. Second order, aircraft found more to hit: in Phase II only 39 percent of counterland sorties came home without dropping, against 56 percent in Phase I. Third order, the civilian toll opened a fight over transparency, and the authors say it remains to be seen whether those deaths feed ISIS or a successor. That chain pre-empts both camps, since speed enthusiasts and safeguard defenders each must sign for the half they would rather skip.

A question for the room: Turn to page 93, where 8,803 Phase II strikes, a quarter of the total, hit terrain and roads, and the authors concede, "We do not have sufficient evidence to determine what percentage" were really morale strikes. If RAND cannot tell, how would a commander know whether he is bombing for effect or for confidence? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 4

Count the Months Before You Count the Bombs

A strike total measures volume, so divide it by the months and ask what it misses before calling a battle intense or a policy useless. Wasser's fourth chapter walks through six close-fight cases, Mosul Dam in August 2014 through Raqqa in 2017, and finds a template: at the height of a city fight, the coalition delivered 100 to 200 strikes a month. Its argument: airpower plus a ground partner took ground, and the processes changed more than the tempo.

The coalition flew more than 1,500 strikes at Mosul over roughly 12 months, a record at the time. Yet Mosul never took more than a third of the coalition's strikes in any month, because Raqqa's shaping ran at the same time. Kobani's peak month, 253 strikes in January 2015, beat every month at Mosul. (A border town most Americans could not find on a map outbombed Iraq's second city.) Mosul's big number came from duration: months of shaping, then nine months of close air support. Then the authors complicate it: between October 2016 and April 2017, 57 percent of the weapons released from coalition aircraft went to Mosul. Same battle, ordinary or enormous, depending on the column you read.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 172, Wasser says Tactical Directive 1 had no appreciable impact on the scale of air strikes in Mosul, so delegating strike authority didn't matter." It reads one passage and ignores the authors' counterevidence. On page 184, the same delegation significantly sped approvals in Raqqa and raised the number of strikes the special operations cells could authorize.

A stronger response: concede the Mosul data, since strikes clustered at 100 to 150 a month on both sides of the river. The directive was a speed tool, and speed never shows up in a monthly count. On page 133, MG Joseph Martin says, "The resultant responsiveness was tremendous." Page 177 explains why the count stayed flat: forward advisers mostly authorized surface fires, while strike cells kept running the air strikes because they deconflicted faster. On page 134, a controller says the coalition could not have massed fires quickly enough for Tabqah and Raqqa without the change. Opinion: grading a speed reform by volume is grading a fire department by gallons used.

First order, the directive let any advisory commander able to control strikes approve ones that once needed a one-star or three-star general. Second order, faster approvals in Raqqa let special operations cells answer a lightly armed SDF in contact, with controllers running three or four strikes at once inside a cylinder four to five nautical miles wide. Third order, the fight for Raqqa city lasted about four and a half months, under half the time it took to clear Mosul. The authors warn that Raqqa is about a quarter of Mosul's size, so we infer delegation helped without claiming it explains the gap. That pre-empts the reply that Raqqa was simply smaller, because we grant it first.

A question for the room: Turn to page 132, where the authors say a brief burst of strikes at Tikrit "validates the general correlation" between airpower and ground gained. Page 153 puts that burst at 37 strikes in one week, and page 157 has the militias back for the final assault. Can one week carry that much of the chapter's argument? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 5

Bank Shots Beat Road Work: Wasser's Deep Fight Rewards Narrow Targets

A deep strike pays when the target is valuable and still sitting there when the approvals clear, so pick targets your process can catch. Wasser's fifth chapter grades four deliberate-strike operations against ISIS. The two aimed at money, Tidal Wave II against oil and Point Blank against cash, did real damage. The two aimed at supply roads mostly did not, because ISIS moved about every two weeks and the deliberate-targeting cycle took four to six.

Point Blank's January 2016 strike on a Mosul bank shows the method at its best. For cash in the basement of a nine-story building, weaponeers dropped three GBU-39 Small Diameter Bombs with staggered fuse delays to open the roof, then two 2,000-pound JDAMs into the vault. The fire took an estimated 75 percent of ISIS's cash for fighter salaries, and a nearby mosque came through untouched. As one interviewee put it, "it wasn't a pickup basketball game." The road war looked different. Planners spent three months building 14 targets near Abu Kamal and Al-Qaim, hit them all on February 15, 2016, and watched ISIS return within 48 to 72 hours. The task force's own intelligence and operations staffs asked, "Is the juice worth the squeeze?" (Three months of homework for a weekend away is a poor exchange rate.) Near Hawija, a large deliberate strike on a car-bomb factory hit 44 aimpoints and destroyed an empty chicken farm.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 210, Wasser says Tidal Wave II cost ISIS about 90 percent of its oil revenue, so the deep fight starved the caliphate." It confuses a first-order effect with the outcome. On page 206, taxes brought ISIS six times as much as oil did, and on page 221, ISIS covered the lost oil money by raising taxes and extortion.

A stronger response: concede the oil numbers, since monthly oil income fell from a $50 million peak to under $4 million. On pages 206 and 207, oil was chosen because airpower could hit it without first retaking the ground. The payoff shows up on page 221, where pay cuts drove desertions and ISIS scaled back its recruiting of foreign fighters. On page 255, airpower paired with partner ground forces liberated the territory, and strategic attacks hastened it. Opinion: the money strikes made the close fight shorter and cheaper, which is plenty, and the authors doubt they could have done the job alone.

First order, the oil and cash strikes cut ISIS's income and burned its reserves, more than $500 million in cash by August 2016. Second order, ISIS cut salaries, lost fighters to desertion, and squeezed its subjects with harsher taxes and extortion. Third order, the personnel shortage bit harder once Iraqi and Syrian offensives drove up attrition, and coalition officials still judged that rolling back territory was the surest way to destroy ISIS's wealth. That chain pre-empts both the airpower-alone reading and the shrug that the deep fight was a sideshow.

A question for the room: Turn to page 253, where the authors admit they cannot judge whether ground commanders truly needed all the surveillance aircraft they got. If the close fight was the main effort, who should decide when a Reaper leaves it to chase a target that may move in two weeks? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 6

Got Your Back, Got the Fitter: Delegated Authority Kept the Syrian Sky Small

Tell pilots what they may shoot before the crisis, and a shootdown can end a confrontation. Wasser's sixth chapter covers the missions that let the bombers bomb, including tankers that flew about a quarter of all sorties. ISIS had no air force worth the name, but Syrian, Russian, and Iranian aircraft shared its sky, and the coalition had to guard its partners without starting a second war.

On June 8, 2017, two Navy Hornets watched a Syrian Su-22 Fitter drop bombs over the al-Tanf exclusion zone and let it leave. Gen David Goldfein and Lt Gen Jeffrey Harrigian used that to restate the rules of engagement face to face, including to Navy pilots on USS George H.W. Bush. One pilot recalled the message: "I have your back; I've given you authority to fight." On June 18, a four-ship from the Bush was flying close air support for the Syrian Democratic Forces near Jadin. One Super Hornet's targeting sensor was misbehaving, so its pilot switched to air-to-air mode to watch a Russian Su-35 circling overhead, and he spotted the Fitter coming from the south. (A broken infrared pod turned out to be a career opportunity.) The AWACS made guard calls, and he flew three headbutts, dumping flares in its path. The Fitter dove and released its bombs on the partners. His AIM-9X missed, his AMRAAM did not, and the U.S. had its first kill of a manned aircraft since 1999. Two Hornets then refueled and went to Mosul to fight ISIS. (Busy morning.)

The claim you'll hear: "On page 272, Wasser says OIR faced a very permissive air environment and ISIS had no air capability beyond small drones, so counterair was a sideshow." It reads one sentence and skips the rest of the page, where the sky turns genuinely contested from 2017, and page 264, where U.S. pilots down three aircraft, likely regime-flown, to protect partners.

A stronger response: concede that ISIS was no air threat and that, on page 264, no one is known to have tried to shoot down a coalition jet. Then point to page 263, where Syrian aircraft and Iranian-made drones tried to strike partners with U.S. special forces nearby. On page 265, Jim Mattis says the Euphrates deconfliction line may look "a little more squiggly," and pilots had to hold their airspace without ceding precedent. Page 273 supplies the yardstick: partners fought without losses to hostile airpower, and nothing escalated into war with Syria or Russia. Opinion: the guidance mattered more than the missile.

First order, the shootdown removed a jet that had just bombed partner forces. Second order, the deconfliction arrangement with Moscow was suspended and then restored, and the task force announced it would defend its partners and had no wish to fight the regime or Russia. Third order, after an F-15E downed a second Iranian-made drone on June 20, aerial tensions eased, and those were the last air-to-air engagements as the authors wrote. That chain pre-empts the worry that killing a Syrian jet invited a fight with Russia.

A question for the room: Turn to page 269, where the Hornets let the June 8 Su-22 leave after it bombed. Was that restraint a gap Harrigian then closed, or the reason the June 18 kill read as self-defense? (S)

Chapter by Chapter · Wasser, chapter 7

The Air War Moved at the Speed of Its Slowest Partner

An air war moves at the speed of its slowest prerequisite, and against the Islamic State the slow ones were intelligence and the partners on the ground. Wasser's closing chapter grants that the early rules were too tight, then argues that a more aggressive air war from day one would have shortened the war only slightly. The partners set the clock, and airpower kept time.

The money war shows it best. Operations Tidal Wave II and Point Blank stressed ISIS's finances at a key moment, but they could not start until the coalition had built a federated intelligence enterprise and a SOF raid had produced exceptional intelligence. That raid killed Abu Sayyaf, and its haul was never replicated. There were few known currency stashes to begin with, and Point Blank hit all of them quickly. (More sorties do not create more vaults.) The ground side ran just as slow. Capable partner forces were in short supply the first year, Iraqi forces had fled ISIS in 2014 and often would not move without coalition aircraft overhead, and Iraqi or Kurdish officials had to approve every strike in their territory. Wasser borrows the adage that the enemy gets a vote and adds, "so too do partner forces." It was no small war, either: 234,000 sorties over 242 weeks, the largest total in the authors' table.

The claim you'll hear: "On page 300, Wasser says the stringent rules of engagement and centralized strike authority created bottlenecks, and outcomes improved once they were relaxed. So gradualism cost us the first year of the war." It reads one passage and ignores the authors' verdict a page later. On page 301, Wasser concludes the gain would have been modest, because partners' capability and willingness to attack drove the timeline.

A stronger response: concede the first half. If we look at page 300, the constraints did shrink the number of targets aircraft could engage. The same page says there were few, if any, deliberate target packages at the outset, and that airpower alone was unlikely to pry ISIS out of its cities. On page 301, ISIS dispersed and went clandestine as the campaign wore on, offering fewer lucrative targets. Opinion: the rules debate is the comfortable one for airmen, since it blames lawyers. The harder lesson is that nobody can buy partner readiness by the sortie.

First order, tight rules and slow target validation choked the strike process. Second order, the coalition improvised: it ran strikes as dynamic or on-call when they could have been preplanned, and it invented a nondoctrinal middle fight that developed targets in 48 to 72 hours inside the land component's area. Third order, those workarounds handed ground entities a say over airpower that doctrine does not support and bred tension between the components, which the authors expect to worsen as the Army fields a 500-kilometer precision-strike missile. That pre-empts the tidy reply that looser rules were the fix: the rules loosened and the process still needed patching.

A question for the room: Turn to page 306, where the authors write that "the coalition benefited from time to refine its targeting processes" and warn that future wars may not allow it. If OIR's fixes took more than four years of patient tinkering, which of its lessons survive an opponent that will not wait? (S)

Six Old Friends Read the RAND Report

Chapter recall

  • What do Wasser's authors conclude about whether a more aggressive air campaign would have defeated ISIS faster?

    It might have slightly accelerated the defeat, but it is unlikely to have significantly altered the timeline. W xv
