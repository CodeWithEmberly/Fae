Counterpoint · Fuller against Lambeth

Counterpoint: The Drone That Stayed Beats the Bomb That Landed

Fuller is right and Lambeth is wrong about what changed airpower most for the wars this course studies: staying over the enemy mattered more than hitting the aim point. Lambeth's main argument is that American air power had "finally become truly strategic," through stealth, standoff precision, and battlespace awareness, with the precision strike at the center. Fuller calls the drone's endurance "the drone's original, but frequently overlooked, defining characteristic." Against an enemy who hides, which describes most enemies in a limited war, Fuller's variable decides more.

Fuller's evidence is what persistence did to the enemy's daily routine. He argues al-Qaeda's core declined as much from the drones' sustained coverage as from their strikes, because coverage kept the leadership cut off from support and influence. Bin Laden's October 2010 letter told fighters to stay off the roads and meet only once or twice a week, "when the clouds are heavy." By 2013, Zawahiri could reach his feuding affiliates only by handwritten letters carried by a courier. (A global movement, run at the speed of the Pony Express.) It was also affordable. Fuller calls the campaign "relatively cheap, in U.S. lives as well as cash."

Lambeth's own pages show precision failing against an enemy who hides. In the Scud hunt, F-15E crews reported destroying some 80 launchers, and the best Lambeth can say is that some Scuds may have been destroyed. His verdict: "all of the precision attack capability in the world is irrelevant" against an enemy who refuses to cooperate in its own destruction. And when he lists the payoffs of the transformation, the "first, and by far most important" is situational awareness, not strike. (Lambeth, it turns out, was a persistence man who had not yet met his drone.)

Lambeth's best shot is Bosnia, and it lands. Operation Deny Flight kept NATO aircraft over Bosnia from April 1993 to December 1995, and Lambeth calls it "little more than a costly exercise in converting jet fuel into noise," with crews "boring holes in the sky." Only the strikes of Deliberate Force paid off. Fuller concedes the second half of the case himself. Against the Islamic State's air defenses and cities, the drones went back to mostly reconnaissance, and while al-Qaeda's core withered, Rand counted Salafi-jihadist groups growing from twenty-eight to forty-nine.

Deny Flight still loses the argument, because it was presence without a trigger. The armed Predator came from exactly that frustration: in Kosovo, Jumper wanted to fix the problem of a drone watching a target it could not hit, and in May 2000 Air Combat Command announced it would arm the Predator. We concede the airspace point in full, because persistence works only where the enemy cannot shoot the orbit down. First order, the drone kept the enemy hiding. Second order, hiding throttled his command, down to couriers and cloudy days. Third order, and this is our inference, the measure of airpower in a limited war becomes how long you can stay, which favors cheap platforms over exquisite ones. That is the argument Sekirin carries to Ukraine next week.

What would change our mind? A case in which armed, persistent coverage over a defended area failed while occasional precision raids succeeded. The line for seminar: "Lambeth proved precision is wasted on an enemy who hides; Fuller showed the answer is to stay until hiding is the enemy's whole job." Lambeth sharpened the scalpel. Fuller noticed the patient kept leaving the room. Precision, in plain terms, is wasted until someone stays long enough to find the target. (S)

The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Sekirin · extrapolation

Cheap Drones, Expensive Habits

Sekirin would endorse the drone half of Shaun's hold and then hand him the bill. The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis and how Airmen should prepare, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the Iran war without a settlement, and his thesis is that Airmen should prepare now to hold the strait the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Sekirin, not Shaun's answer, and it tests one preparation: the small American footprint of drones, advisers, and controllers.

Sekirin supports the footprint on cost. He argues that money spent on exquisite hardware is wasted if cheap, discardable drones can destroy it quickly, and he names the drone's four strengths as omnipresence, accurate direct fires, discardability, and mobility. A hold built on persistent, expendable drones is close to what his book prescribes. (He would also like to sell you some. He proposes Ukraine as Europe's postwar drone industrial base.)

The same logic cuts against the hold in the Gulf, because in Hormuz the cheap mass belongs to Tehran. Ukraine built its long-range drones to match Iran's Shahed 136, which Russia has used against Ukraine's power grid, and Wasser opens with an Iranian Shahed 129 firing on American advisers near al-Tanf in 2017. Sekirin also warns that cheap drones do not make a cheap force. A centrally commanded drone force that can survive enemy countermeasures "will not come cheaply," he writes, and paying for it means cutting tanks, artillery, and even fighter jets. Cost per kill is the right measure for the airframe. The network, the jamming resistance, and the trained crews are where the money goes.

He also has a warning about who runs it. Ukraine's new drone branch went first to a commander whose previous command was a motorised brigade, and Sekirin blames his inexperience for its troubles; in June 2025 a major who led an elite drone unit replaced him. Opinion, extrapolated: Sekirin backs the footprint and doubts the price tag. Two questions for the draft:

  1. What does the hold cost per year as a system, counting the network and the crews, not per drone?
  2. Who in the Air Force would command it, and has that person ever flown one? (S)

An Enormous Quantity of Data, Very Little Progress

Every war on this shelf graded itself with the number that was easiest to count, and four books catch the graders at it. Gaddis reports that pilot reports, not photographs, measured the bombing of North Vietnam, "presumably because damage claims tended to be higher." Sortie rates set the allocations of fuel and bombs, so the Air Force and Navy competed on them. The results were small bomb loads, bombs dropped on targets already destroyed, and, during shortages, sorties flown with no bombs at all. (A sortie without bombs still counts as a sortie. Accounting is undefeated.)

Schlight finds the same habit in the South. With no objective of its own, the Air Force measured readiness, response times, sorties, and tonnage instead of effects. His verdict: "An enormous quantity of data described the Air Force's effort, but little its progress." Kitfield puts McCaffrey in Abrams's briefing room while a briefer grades the country's hamlets by letter on the Hamlet Evaluation Survey, which counted more than 90 percent of the population as pacified. McCaffrey thought it was nonsense.

Haun shows the subtler failure, two honest numbers that disagree. The DIA and Seventh Air Force said Commando Hunt I cut supplies to the South by 82 percent. The CIA said the enemy needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day and got them. Haun's ruling: "Both DIA and CIA assessments were correct." They measured different things. Yesterday's Starshield item belongs here too. Wasser's coalition flew sorties of little tactical value just to give Iraqi troops "visible evidence of reassurance," so even reassurance got logged as a sortie. Today's news item adds an AI grader that scored the same wargame twelve points apart.

Our take: before you trust any number in this course, ask what it was built to count and who benefits when it goes up. The metric always wins the war it measures, because people fight for whatever they are graded on. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 5, Day 2 · using evidence and evaluating sources

Consider the Source, Then Read Its Invoice

Before you use a source, find out what it is trying to prove and whether its author could know what he claims. Adler and Van Doren, in How to Read a Book, tell readers to assume that every book about current events wants to prove something. They give five questions: what the book is trying to prove, whom it is trying to convince, what knowledge (or prejudice) it assumes you share, what special language it uses, and whether the reporter actually knows the facts. The last one, they say, is probably the hardest to answer.

Sekirin is next Monday's test case. He writes that Ukraine created its drone branch on February 6, 2024, "largely because of the memo I sent" on January 3. He was there, so he can vouch for what he sent. Whether he knows why the president decided is Adler's fifth question, and his book can't answer it. Before: "Ukraine created its Unmanned Systems Forces in February 2024 because of Sekirin's January memo." After: "Sekirin, who advised Ukraine's High Command, says the February 2024 decision came largely because of his January 3 memo; we have no second source for the link." Now the reader knows who is speaking and which part of the claim is his to make.

Disclosure works in a source's favor. Adler and Van Doren put it plainly: "Scientific objectivity is not the absence of initial bias." It comes, they say, from frank confession of it. Today's news item shows how. Thomas Nagle tells his readers that he founded a company that grades wargames and has an interest in AI contracting. His stake tells you which claims to check first, the ones that help his business, and which to credit extra: his report that his own first grader failed.

An old editor's trick: put the stake in the attribution clause. "Sekirin, who advised the High Command," or "Nagle, who sells wargame grading." If you can't write that clause, you don't know the source well enough to cite it yet. Every source has an angle. The good ones tell you the degree. (S)

Grading the Generals on a Curve Nobody Can Repeat

The Pentagon wants AI-graded wargames to help decide which colonels and Navy captains get a first star, and the man building one such grader says it isn't ready. Writing in War on the Rocks, Thomas Nagle reports that Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth announced at the Naval War College on November 9 that competitive AI-assisted wargaming and decision-forcing scenarios would become the "centerpiece of senior officer evaluation." A memo the same day directs every staff and war college to build a standardized capstone wargame or exam to support "talent management and stratification decisions," with a grading framework due in 180 days. Nagle, who founded WarPaths and discloses his business interest, says his first AI grader scored the same play four times and came back twelve points apart on a 100-point scale. It also gave a deliberately novice player an unreasonably high score. As of November 19, his improved grader passed two of the six tests he set for it.

Sekirin's book is the best case on our shelf for grading judgment instead of trusting the old pipeline. He describes pre-2014 Ukraine as an army of "corrupt generals promoting corrupt and often incapable officers," which left a generation of unimaginative commanders. At Debaltseve in 2015, a relief attempt failed, the encircled troops broke out across open fields with heavy losses, and then, in his words, "Syrsky, on the other hand, got promoted." Ukraine's new drone branch went to a commander whose previous command was a motorised brigade. An army that promotes after a failure needs a better measure. (Any measure, really.)

Sekirin also explains why a scored game is the wrong instrument for ranking generals right now. Predicting the enemy's next move, he writes, is a call "guided as much by art as it is science," because no intelligence can anticipate "the X-factor that is human command." Nagle reaches the same place from the other side: grade the reasoning behind each decision, because the outcome depends on the scenario, the opponent, and luck. Opinion: right goal, premature tool. A grader that can't give the same play the same score twice is mostly measuring itself. Use these games now for practice and feedback, and let them rank officers once the grader passes all six of the tests its own builder wrote.

Would a capstone have caught the Debaltseve plan? We doubt it, because a test rewards what it can see. Before a machine sorts the colonels, someone should sort the machine. In plain terms, audit the grader the way you would audit a source, and only then let it near a promotion board. (S)

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

    546 Navy and 730 Fifth Air Force sorties destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential in four days (119).

  • Zhang: what did Zhou Enlai tell a Soviet general about China's air force in Korea?

    China could endure losing half a million to a million soldiers, "but we will not sacrifice our newly built air force" (116).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of Air Force sorties in South Vietnam hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces, through October 1966?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how did January 1973's agreement differ from October's?

    "Largely cosmetic" differences (ch. VI, e217).

  • Randolph: how does he score the Easter Offensive for Hanoi?

    "A strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome" (339).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

  • Haun: what did DIA and CIA say about Commando Hunt I, and who was right?

    DIA said it cut supplies by 82 percent; the CIA said the enemy needed fewer than fifteen truckloads a day and got them. "Both DIA and CIA assessments were correct" (128).

  • Kitfield: what were Weinberger's six tests, and when did he give them?

    Vital interests, enough force to win decisively, clearly defined political and military objectives, forces sized to them, reasonable assurance of public support, and force as a last resort; National Press Club, November 28, 1984 (269).

  • Olsen: what share of the air effort went against Warden's inner ring?

    2 percent; 740 of 43,123 strikes hit the "central nervous system" (167, 295).

  • Fravel: who started eight of China's nine strategies since 1949?

    Senior military officers, subject only to the chairman's approval (ebook 429).

  • Lambeth: what does he call Operation Deny Flight?

    "Little more than a costly exercise in converting jet fuel into noise" (179).

  • Henriksen: what did key leaders expect from the bombing, and why?

    Two to four days would move Milosevic, because they believed Deliberate Force had done so in Bosnia in 1995 (ebook 13).

  • Fuller: what does he call the drone's overlooked defining characteristic?

    Its endurance, which kept al-Qaeda's core in constant concealment (ebook 303, 308).

  • Wasser: what was the defeat-ISIS strategy built to avoid?

    Turning the conflict into "a potentially endless commitment" for the United States, through a limited-liability, limited-risk, partner-led approach (298).

Writing drills

  • Add the stake: "Fuller says the drones crippled al-Qaeda's core."

    "Fuller argues that the drones' endurance crippled al-Qaeda's core as much as their strikes did (ebook 308–309)." Naming what he argues, not just what he says, tells the reader it's a claim to weigh (Adler, 252).

  • Which of Adler's five questions does this sentence fail? "Ukraine's drone branch was created because of a single memo."

    The fifth: does the reporter know the facts? Only the memo's author makes the claim (Sekirin, xvi), and he could not see the president's reasoning (Adler, 253–254).

  • Review (Monday, active voice): find the actor in "Coverage was maintained and the core was contained."

    "CIA drones kept al-Qaeda's core under constant watch and cut it off from its supporters" (Fuller, ebook 308–309). Run the "by an idiot" test first: "was maintained by an idiot" scans, so both verbs are passive.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
