Seminar 16 · Wasser et al. · "American Airpower Returns to Conventional War"

Today's Seminar Questions, Answered

  1. How does this air campaign fit in the overall arc of airpower history? It is the biggest air war since Desert Storm by total size and one of the smallest by intensity. Inherent Resolve ran 242 weeks and 234,000 sorties, but at about 1,000 sorties a week, against 19,800 a week in Desert Storm (chapter 7). Its first month flew 949 air-to-ground sorties, only 100 of which dropped anything, and Deliberate Force in 1995 had flown more than three times that rate (chapter 2). It also marks the hinge back toward contested skies: Russian fighters overhead, and in June 2017 the first American air-to-air kill of a manned aircraft since 1999.
  2. What lessons did strategists take into OIR, and what did they leave with? They came in with the habits of counterinsurgency and left having to relearn the habits of war. The targeting enterprise had atrophied, the civilian casualty cutoff was zero, and strike approval sat with one general until the fall of 2014 (chapter 2). The report's exit lessons say that politics will sometimes require airpower to be used suboptimally, that a joint intelligence enterprise is a prerequisite and slow to build, that unused skills atrophy, and that precision munition stockpiles are insufficient (chapter 7).
  3. Given that the Islamic State still inspires terror, how effective was OIR? It was effective against the objective it was given, the territorial caliphate, and the authors say so plainly: "On the whole, OIR was a success" (chapter 7). It did less against the movement. General Votel told the Senate in February 2019 that the group was "well-positioned to reemerge if pressure on the group is relieved," and General LaCamera called the end "the repositioning of an army" (chapter 3). We grade it a success at what it was asked to do. The question assumes a broader mission than Washington ever gave it.
  4. How did the return of great power competition alter the war, or vice versa? Russia turned a close air support war into a rehearsal for contested airspace. Russian strikes began September 30, 2015, Congress had already barred military cooperation with Russia, so CENTCOM could only deconflict, and within a month the two signed a safety memorandum (chapter 2). By 2017 pilots raised on high-level approvals had to be told they could defend themselves, and the Su-22 shootdown followed (chapters 6 and 7). Going the other way, OIR drained the precision munitions and the drone fleet a great power war would need (chapter 7).
  5. Was Obama right to back local ground forces even if it took longer? Yes, and Wasser's evidence makes the case better than the administration did. Washington chose a "limited-liability, limited risk" strategy so it would not "own" the problem and could still leave (chapters 2 and 7). The authors judge that a more aggressive air war might have sped the defeat "but not dramatically," because the partners' readiness set the clock (chapter 7). The price was real: partner preferences bent the air war, and civilian casualties remain disputed. It was still the cheapest way to win something that lasted.

Chapter lessons for this book are on last night's page. (S)

Today's Reading · Wasser et al., The Air War Against the Islamic State

The Longest Air War Was Also the Politest

RAND's team argues that airpower was indispensable against the Islamic State, and that the strategy, more than the airmen or the lawyers, decided how it was used. Washington put Iraqi and Syrian partners in the lead to limit its own risk, so close air support beat strategic attack for the bombs. The physical caliphate was the center of gravity, because territory paid the bills through taxes and extortion. (A protostate, it turns out, funds itself the old-fashioned way: by shaking down its subjects.)

The report closes a loop from yesterday. Fuller's drones hunted men under empty skies; Wasser's drones became the eyes of an entire army that had no American controllers on the ground, an approach the report calls "air accompanied." Henriksen's argument over who approves a target returns too, now as a single general approving every strike until the fall of 2014. The quarrel is the same, with more paperwork.

Our take: this is the most useful book on the shelf for a paper about a long, cheap commitment, because it is a study of one. The authors measured success by what the policy asked for, and the policy asked for patience. (S)

Counterpoint · Wasser against Lambeth

Counterpoint: Lambeth Wanted a Downpour. The Partners Set the Forecast.

Wasser is right and Lambeth is wrong about whether air power could have won this war on its own clock. Against an enemy that held cities and taxed the people in them, the ground partners set the pace, and no amount of extra bombing would have changed that much.

Start with the money, since the Islamic State did. By one estimate in the report, taxes brought in six times as much as oil, and the coalition could reach that income only by taking back the land it came from. The deep strikes did what deep strikes can do. Tidal Wave II cut oil revenue from a peak of fifty million dollars a month to under four million, and air strikes plus border work stripped about ninety percent of it. That was the part of the economy a bomber could find. The rest was a harvest tax, a highway tax, and a zakat collected door to door. Then look at the enemy's learning curve. When the Islamic State massed at Kobani, General Hesterman said, "we kill them at a very great rate." The group drew the obvious conclusion and stopped massing. Its only real gains after that came when weather or politics kept the aircraft away. An air force cannot attrit an army that has decided to stop being one. (It can, however, keep that army very humble.) Wasser's verdict follows: a more aggressive air war "might have accelerated ISIS's defeat," but "not dramatically," because partners' capability and willingness drove the timeline.

Lambeth's best shot is a strong one. His book's main argument is that American air power had become "truly strategic in its potential effects," able to neutralize an enemy's army with a minimum of casualties and to serve as the swing factor in theater war, so that ground forces might increasingly "secure a win rather than to achieve it." Wasser's own pages lend him ammunition. The report lists Lambeth's 2015 op-ed, "The U.S. Is Squandering Its Airpower," among the critics of the early limits, and it concedes that centralized approval "clearly limited" airpower in the first year. Approvals took more than thirty minutes and sometimes hours, and only one general approved strikes until the fall of 2014. When authority moved down, results improved. And the partners, the report admits, could not have beaten the group at all without coalition airpower.

We concede the first year. Holding strike approval at the top was a self-inflicted wound, and Lambeth was right to say so in 2015. It still loses, because Lambeth's thesis is about defeating "large-scale aggression," and the Islamic State stopped being large-scale aggression as soon as it learned what a B-1 could do. Wasser shows the binding constraint was elsewhere: no deliberate target sets, a targeting cycle of three to six weeks against an enemy that moved every two, and too few partners able to attack. Even a USAF commander, the authors write, would have struggled. The second-order effect is the one that matters for airmen. A theory that says air power achieves the win invites a strategy that skips the slow work of building partners. The third-order effect is the Phase IV problem: Votel's warning that the group could reemerge is a warning about who holds the ground afterward, and no air campaign answers it. What would change our mind? Evidence that an early, unrestricted air war retook a city without a partner force moving in behind it. The report has none.

The line for noon: "Lambeth says air power can achieve the win; Wasser shows that against an enemy that taxes cities, the partners decide when the win arrives, and air power decides how much it costs." The airmen brought the umbrella. The partners decided when it rained. (S)

Questions to Ask in Seminar · Wasser

Questions to Ask in Seminar

  1. Were the strike cells the war's best innovation or a doctrinal mistake we should not repeat? The report says they added "another layer of coordination between the CFACC and the ground entities" (Wasser, chapter 7, page 305). One answer: they were the innovation that won the close fight. With no forward controllers, the cells integrated airpower with partner units dynamically (page 299), and the ground component's "deliberate-dynamic" process produced targets in twenty-four to forty-eight hours where the formal cycle took four to six weeks (pages 99 to 100). The other answer: they handed airpower's priorities to the ground. For the first ten months there was no deep fight, the shifting fire support line shrank the area the air commander controlled, and the authors expect "negative lessons learned" (page 305). Our take: the first answer wins. The cells existed because the air component's own process was too slow. Fix that process first, and keep the workaround until the fix works.
  2. Should the Air Force buy its next force from this war's shopping list? The authors write that "The unsung heroes of OIR were the tanker and RPA communities" (Wasser, chapter 7, page 302). One answer: yes. About seventy tankers, some 15 percent of the KC-135 and KC-10 fleets, supported the theater, and in 2017 alone MQ-1s and MQ-9s flew more than twelve thousand sorties and were still overtasked (page 303). The other answer: this was a war in which the enemy could not shoot back at a drone. Over Syria the coalition flew "permissively in a nonpermissive environment" (page 304), and the June 2017 shootdown took F/A-18s, not Reapers (pages 270 to 271). Fuller showed slow drones losing their loiter against Islamic State antiaircraft weapons, and Iran has downed about fifty MQ-9s this year. Our take: the second answer wins. Buy the tankers, which every war needs, and be careful about drones built for skies nobody contests.
  3. Did the deep strikes against the Islamic State's money matter? The authors call deep strikes "a relatively small but notable contribution to OIR" (Wasser, chapter 7, page 301). One answer: they mattered. Tidal Wave II cut oil revenue from about fifty million dollars a month to under four million, and by May 2017 Harrigian said the group had its lowest income since 2014 (page 210). The other answer: they hit the slice of the money a bomber could find. Taxes produced six times what oil did, and they could be cut only by taking back territory (page 206). Our take: the second answer wins. The strikes helped at a key moment (page 301), and the war was won by the partners who took the tax base back. (S)
The 628 Paper · Hormuz through Wasser · extrapolation

The Gulf Bases Are Already Built. The Partners Still Get a Vote.

The prompt asks for the most important potential crisis facing the nation and how Airmen should prepare for it, in ten pages due December 1. Shaun's crisis is the Strait of Hormuz after the United States leaves the war with Iran without a settlement. His thesis: Airmen should prepare now to hold the Strait of Hormuz the way America holds Korea, cheaply and with partners, so that time works for Washington and against Tehran. What follows is our extrapolation from Wasser, not Shaun's answer.

Wasser supports the paper's second point, that cost felt at home ends American wars, with a working example. The defeat-ISIS strategy was built to limit risk to American forces and "to avoid turning the conflict into a potentially endless commitment," and Washington refused to "own" the problem so it could still leave. The infrastructure for a hold already exists, too: Al Udeid in Qatar, Al Dhafra in the United Arab Emirates, and Ali Al Salem in Kuwait were built up before the war, with fighters, bombers, ISR, airlift, and tankers on hand, which let the United States respond quickly to the crisis on Mount Sinjar in August 2014. Those are Hormuz bases. (The real estate, at least, is prepaid.)

Wasser cuts against the partner council in one specific way. "The enemy gets a vote," the authors write, and so do partner forces. Iraqi or Kurdish officials approved every strike in their territory, partners maneuvered by their own interests, and the coalition was often "beholden" to them. A council of Gulf partners that can end the hold can also slow it, or veto the strike that matters on the night it matters. A cheap hold also eats tankers: about seventy of them, some 15 percent of the fleet, kept this one going.

Opinion, extrapolated: Wasser would back the Korea model and warn that partners buy legitimacy at the price of speed. Two questions for the draft:

  1. Which strikes in the strait need a partner's approval, and which can an American commander order alone?
  2. How many tankers does the hold need in a quiet year, and who pays for them? (S)
The Readings So Far · Crane, Olsen, Fuller, and Wasser

Ninety Percent of the Wrong Thing

Strategic attack keeps scoring ninety percent against targets that turn out not to decide the war. Crane's June 1952 hydroelectric strikes destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential in four days. Olsen's planners aimed at Saddam's "central nervous system," and only 740 of 43,123 strikes, about 2 percent, went against that inner ring. Wasser's Tidal Wave II stripped about 90 percent of the Islamic State's oil revenue, and the group kept paying its fighters with taxes worth six times its oil. (Air forces are excellent at the ninety percent they can see.)

The pattern is that bombers hit what is fixed, and the enemy's strength is often mobile or social. Fuller's CIA learned the same thing hunting men: a drone was useless without spies to say where to look. Wasser's targeteers found that the Islamic State moved its facilities every two weeks while a target package took four to six weeks to approve, so approved packages were often irrelevant by the time they cleared.

Thursday's news item ties in. The Navy fired at least 850 Tomahawks in a month of the Iran war and recoded expired ones to keep going. Wasser's last lesson says precision munitions are required by politics and existing stockpiles are insufficient, and that was written about a war against an enemy with no air force. Opinion: every book on the shelf has the same accounting problem. The target list is long, the magazine is short, and the thing that decides the war is usually not on the list. (S)

Technical Writing · Week 4, Day 5 · citations and Chicago style

Nine Authors, One Interview, and a Footnote That Tells the Truth

A citation must name the source you actually read. Irina Ruppo, in A Guide to Academic Writing, says referencing styles matter less than using sources correctly, and that nobody should memorize them. Look up the convention, she advises, especially for an unusual source such as a government report. Her other two rules fit today's book exactly: when a piece has many authors, write them all down, and when you are stuck, phrase the search precisely, as in "How do I cite a source within a source?"

Wasser's report is both problems at once. It has nine authors and leans on RAND's own interviews. Take the claim that in Syria the air component ran the deep strikes about 90 percent of the time. The report attributes that to a RAND interview with retired Lieutenant General Sean MacFarland. Before: a note that cites "MacFarland, interview," as if you had sat across the table from him. After: a note that names the interview and its date, then says "quoted in" Becca Wasser and colleagues, the report's title, RAND, 2021, and the page. The first version claims access you never had. The second tells the reader exactly how many hands the fact passed through, which is Tuesday's lesson in a footnote. (Nobody on your committee believes you interviewed a three-star.)

Pro tip, an old editor's trick: search your draft for the words "interview," "said," and "according to." Every hit that came to you through someone else's book needs "quoted in" or "cited in" in its note. Chicago lets you shorten nine authors to the first name plus "et al." in the notes; the bibliography is where the whole team gets its credit. (S)

Leadership × Wasser · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, November 18

Fordow Was a Fifteen-Year Study and a One-Night Exam

B-2 crews have told the inside story of Operation Midnight Hammer for the first time, in a piece by Chris Gordon and Tobias Naegele in Air & Space Forces Magazine. Fourteen pilots flew from Whiteman, Missouri, to Iran and back, half active duty and half Missouri Air National Guard, and every one of them a Weapons School graduate. Six B-2s went to Fordow and one to Natanz, about 125 aircraft supported them, and fourteen Massive Ordnance Penetrators, 210 tons in all, made their combat debut. The first detonated at 2:10 a.m. local time. Behind the night sat years: a former B-2 pilot at the Defense Threat Reduction Agency had spent about fifteen years studying the site, the lead pilot said plans were pulled off the shelf and refreshed, and within a week of the commander's call, fifty to sixty tacticians were formalizing the plan. (Over Gibraltar, one pilot accidentally inflated his life preserver in the cockpit, and the crew flew the rest of the way in combat vests. Nobody said the details would be glamorous.)

Wasser shows what happens when nobody keeps the folder current. When OIR began, the air commander was short of targeteers and intelligence professionals who knew how to build a target set from scratch, and the coalition had to cobble together a federated intelligence enterprise across the Defense Department. Its targeting enterprise had atrophied, so the first year produced few deep strikes, and a deliberate target took three to six weeks. The report's lesson is blunt: skills not used in recent operations atrophy, and relearning them slows the response. Its recommendation is to keep growing targeteers.

Our take: the decisive leadership decision behind Midnight Hammer was made years before the mission, by whoever kept paying an analyst to study one mountain and kept a plan current for a strike nobody had ordered. That is unglamorous work, and it does not show up in a promotion record until the night it does. The tankers carry the same moral. One planner called them "the limiting factor" in the escort's size, and Wasser called OIR a "tanker war." Commanders get the credit for the night. The folder was built by people who never got a call sign. (S)

Space × Sci-Fi × Wasser · via Air & Space Forces Magazine, November 19

Houston, We Have a Single Supplier

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan failed on November 15 with forty Amazon satellites aboard, Greg Hadley reports in Air & Space Forces Magazine. It was Vulcan's first launch in nine months, after a grounding over its solid rocket boosters, and ULA blamed an inadequate fuel supply to the first stage's Blue Origin engines. The same engine family powers New Glenn, which has not flown since a pad explosion in May, so one problem could touch two of the Space Force's three options for its most important launches. The Space Force has already moved half a dozen missions to SpaceX, the only provider currently cleared for those missions, and Todd Harrison of AEI says there is "not much capacity left on the Falcon 9." The Pentagon is offering $150 million loans to Rocket Lab and Stoke Space, whose rockets first fly in 2027. (Assured access to space is currently assured by one company.)

Wasser describes this problem in an air war. Tankers and drones were the platforms in greatest demand in OIR, and drones were overtasked with so many missions that demand outpaced supply. The report's fix is to identify alternative options, or platform substitutes with similar capabilities, and fold them into the allocation process before the shortage arrives, to reduce the risk of overstretch. Launch is a high-demand, low-density asset with a very long lead time.

Andy Weir's novel The Martian plays this out. To resupply the stranded astronaut, NASA rushes a probe called Iris and skips its normal inspections, the launch fails, and the rescue survives only because China's space agency offers its own booster, the Taiyang Shen. In Weir's story the substitute appeared in time because another agency happened to have one. (Fiction gets to arrange its coincidences.)

Our take: the Space Force is in Wasser's position with a slower clock. Two new rockets in 2027 are the platform substitutes, and the loans are cheap compared with a satellite waiting on the ground. The next failure will not wait for the certification paperwork. (S)

The Shelf

  • Gaddis: what did flexible response in Vietnam assume about force?

    "Calibration," moving up or down a range of precisely calculated actions without extreme escalation or humiliation (241).

  • Crane: what did the June 1952 hydroelectric strikes achieve?

    546 Navy and 730 Fifth Air Force sorties destroyed 90 percent of North Korea's electric power potential in four days (119).

  • Zhang: what did Zhou Enlai tell a Soviet general about China's air force in Korea?

    China could endure losing half a million to a million soldiers, "but we will not sacrifice our newly built air force" (116).

  • McDougall: what killed the missile gap?

    Reconnaissance satellites in 1961, which cut the CIA's count of Soviet missiles to at most fourteen (327–330).

  • Schlight: what share of Air Force sorties in South Vietnam hit enemy troops engaged with allied forces, through October 1966?

    About 3 percent (216).

  • Clodfelter: how did January 1973's agreement differ from October's?

    "Largely cosmetic" differences (ch. VI, e217).

  • Randolph: how does he score the Easter Offensive for Hanoi?

    "A strategic success, despite its ambiguous, or even failed, military outcome" (339).

  • Nguyen: who does she say actually ran Hanoi's war?

    Le Duan, first secretary from 1960 until his death in 1986, with Le Duc Tho as his right hand (intro, e19–20).

  • Haun: how much Iraqi armor and artillery did airstrikes destroy before the ground war?

    About 10 percent; desertions accounted for most of the 25 to 30 percent attrition (272).

  • Kitfield: what were Weinberger's six tests, and when did he give them?

    Vital interests, enough force to win decisively, clearly defined political and military objectives, forces sized to them, reasonable assurance of public support, and force as a last resort; National Press Club, November 28, 1984 (269).

  • Olsen: what share of the air effort went against Warden's inner ring?

    2 percent; 740 of 43,123 strikes hit the "central nervous system" (167, 295).

  • Fravel: who started eight of China's nine strategies since 1949?

    Senior military officers, subject only to the chairman's approval (ebook 429).

  • Lambeth: what role does he forecast for land power against large-scale aggression?

    "To secure a win rather than to achieve it" (320–321).

  • Henriksen: what did key leaders expect from the bombing, and why?

    Two to four days would move Milosevic, because they believed Deliberate Force had done so in Bosnia in 1995 (ebook 13).

  • Fuller: what does he call the drone's overlooked defining characteristic?

    Its endurance, which kept al-Qaeda's core in constant concealment (ebook 303, 308).

  • Wasser: what was the defeat-ISIS strategy built to avoid?

    Turning the conflict into "a potentially endless commitment" for the United States, through a limited-liability, limited-risk, partner-led approach (298).

How This Page Teaches

Listen first, test after. The articles read cleanly aloud with no footnote numbers. Sources and the Q&A wait at the end, where answering from memory does the real work.

Spacing. Each book stays on The Shelf for weeks, so you meet it again on day 2, day 7, and day 30. That's the EV effect: a little, every day.

Connections. Every news item gets pinned to a book. Ideas tied to a story stick better than ideas alone.

Write it yourself. The "Your turn" box matters most. Producing the paragraph teaches more than reading mine.

House Style

What electoral-vote.com does on the page, turned into rules for this site. These come from reading the site's front page in late October 2026. They describe its habits without copying its text.

1. One page, numbered items, linked list on top

Each day is a list of headlined items, with links at the top so readers can skip. Weight decides length. A big item runs four to seven paragraphs and a small one runs two.

2. Headlines carry the joke or the claim

EV headlines are title case, often a pun, sometimes a two-part setup across consecutive items. A headline should make a claim or land a joke. It should never just name a topic.

3. Open on a hook, close on a line

Openings are an aside, a generalization, or a joke. Closings are a dry one-liner, a deadpan sentence, or "here's what to watch next."

4. Every news item ties to a book

EV's signature habit is translating news into what it means for the race. Here, every news item gets pinned to something on the shelf, with a page number.

5. Rhythm: short, short, long-with-a-turn

Alternate plain declarative sentences with a longer one that turns sardonic at the end. Parentheticals carry the asides.

6. Show your sources

Every factual claim gets a footnote, and opinion is labeled as opinion. Readers should always be able to tell which is which.

7. State uncertainty out loud

Use conditional chains ("if X holds, then Y") and say so when a source contradicts itself. Confident and hedged at once is the house tone.

8. Initials as bylines

Sign each item (S). If a co-writer joins, add their initial, the way EV uses (V) and (Z).

9. A fixed daily lineup

Politics × books, defense news × books, leadership, writing, and the nightstand. Readers learn where to look, the same way EV readers know where the Senate table is.

10. Running jokes that carry over

Callbacks to earlier items (Kennan's "X" article, the empty poll column) reward daily readers and connect the issues.
